1. Iran hits Bahrain again as a third US carrier heads for the Gulf

On Sunday Iran launched its second drone attack on Bahrain in three days, ending a lull of about two months. Iran said the drones hit a US radar system in Bahrain (China Daily). Bahrain said it intercepted the aerial attacks after a nationwide alert (The National). Bloomberg headlined the episode “Iran targets Bahrain base but says path to US deal is open.” The New York Times, citing a US military official, reported that the USS Theodore Roosevelt will join two other US carriers in the region within a day, making three. Neither the Pentagon nor CENTCOM has confirmed the schedule.

Tehran’s public line combines threats with an offer to talk. Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf said Iran will not remain in a state of “neither war nor peace” and is ready for either negotiations or war. The government said its response to any new attack “will not be limited or short-lived.” Hürriyet Daily News summarised the message as “Iran warns it will not wait for another US attack.” Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran remains committed to diplomacy but that Washington had chosen “a different path.” In Israel, Mossad chief Roman Gofman returned from talks with US officials on Iran and is to brief the security cabinet and caretaker Prime Minister Netanyahu. Asharq Al-Awsat reports widespread Arab condemnation of the attacks on Bahrain. Washington is concentrating carrier air power in the Gulf on the eve of the Shenzhen summit, which leaves military escalation and great-power bargaining running side by side.

Key facts: - Tempo: two attacks on Bahrain in three days, after roughly two months without Iranian strikes on Gulf states. - Target: Iran says a US radar installation. Bahrain hosts the US Fifth Fleet. - Force posture: three US carriers in theatre if the Roosevelt deployment is confirmed (NYT via Anadolu). - Israel: the Mossad chief’s briefing to the security cabinet follows his Washington talks. Netanyahu still heads a caretaker government.

2. Tankers sink in Hormuz minefield as Houthi drones target Mecca and Medina

The IRGC said two oil tankers struck naval mines and caught fire while crossing the southern Strait of Hormuz, and that one of them sank. There is no independent confirmation yet of the vessels’ names or of casualties among their crews. In the Red Sea and Arabian Sea theatre, the UK maritime agency (UKMTO) reported that four armed men in a boat approached an oil tanker east of Yemen’s Socotra island.

On land, the Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed four drones, two over Mecca and two over Medina. That brought the day’s total to seven, after it had earlier reported two ballistic missiles fired at Riyadh and three drones over the oil-producing Eastern Province. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki announced the interceptions on X. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree called the coalition’s account “a lie and a fabrication.” Ukrainian news agency UNN reported that Saudi Arabia’s Yemeni allies “retreated under pressure from the Houthis” on the ground. The Saudi–Houthi front and the US–Iran confrontation are now running in parallel.

Key facts: - Hormuz: two tankers hit mines and one sank, according to the IRGC. Mines in the shipping lane threaten crews whoever laid them. - Saudi Arabia: seven drones and two ballistic missiles in a single day, according to the coalition, with targets including Mecca, Medina, Riyadh and the Eastern Province. - Socotra: UKMTO reports an armed approach to a tanker. No boarding was reported. - Denial: the Houthis reject the coalition’s account of the interceptions.

3. Beijing confirms Trump–Xi–Putin summit in Shenzhen for 19 November

China has officially confirmed that Xi Jinping will host Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen on 19 November, on the sidelines of APEC. It will be the first three-way meeting of the leaders of the three largest nuclear powers. The White House had announced the meeting last week. Beijing’s confirmation, reported by TRT World, Hromadske and a wide range of Asian outlets, fixes the date. Several outlets frame the meeting around nuclear arsenals: Firstpost asks “Will they have a consensus on nuclear arsenals?” The Hindustan Times describes the trip as “Trump’s Asia reset: deals in Manila and Shenzhen, then a rare nuclear summit.” The Week calls hosting such a meeting an “unusual step” for China, and Ukrainian News Agency reports that Xi is “wary” of the format.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres was in Manila for the 16th ASEAN–UN Summit. He said a commitment by the US, Russia and China to “meaningful denuclearization” would be a “greater achievement for the world.” Trump’s Asia trip begins in Manila, where ASEAN meetings are under way. Euronews reports that European governments are still debating what role they will have in any Ukraine settlement. The 15 November briefing reported that US officials expect no agreement.

Key facts: - Date and venue: 19 November, Shenzhen, APEC sidelines, confirmed by Beijing. - Precedent: the first trilateral meeting ever held at this level. Commentators compare it to Yalta. - Itinerary: Manila, then Shenzhen. - UN: Guterres links the summit to nuclear disarmament.

4. Nvidia crisis drags Asian markets down before earnings and Anthropic’s IPO

Asian markets opened the week lower as the fallout from Jensen Huang’s resignation as Nvidia chief executive combined with nerves about Nvidia’s quarterly results, due this week, and Anthropic’s planned stock-market debut. Reuters headlined: “Asian shares fall before Nvidia earnings, Anthropic IPO test tech rally.” The Economic Times reported that Japan’s Nikkei fell more than 1 percent as chip stocks slid. Other reports said it briefly dipped below ¥60,000, and CNBC TV18 reported falls in both the Nikkei and Kospi. US stock futures were mixed: some wraps had them edging up, while TradingView had Nasdaq futures turning lower.

The political fight over Huang’s exit has widened. Huang resigned on Thursday as president, CEO and board member amid scrutiny of a reported relationship with a businesswoman whom US intelligence officials reportedly suspect of ties to Chinese intelligence. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO. A “Stand with Jensen” petition demanding his reinstatement while an independent investigation is held passed 130,000 signatures by Sunday. Elon Musk, Epic Games’ Tim Sweeney, Replit’s Amjad Masad and Y Combinator’s Garry Tan back it. The letter says “Suspicion or association alone is not a sufficient basis for ending Jensen’s leadership” and “Jensen deserves due process, not mob justice.” The WSJ’s Risk Report says Huang’s departure is “unlikely to mollify Congress, plaintiffs’ lawyers.” A New York Times editorial-page piece argues that “the new Congress must rein in Trump’s dangerous China policy.” On the WSWS, Andre Damon argued that Democrats are using the affair to attack Trump from the right over chip exports to China, even though, he wrote, “I don’t think that we know,” in Rep. Jim Himes’s words, how firm the underlying intelligence is.

Key facts: - This week: Nvidia’s third-quarter results, Anthropic’s IPO (pricing expected 18 November, trading as “ANT” on 19 November), US retail sales and Microsoft Ignite. - Anthropic offering: 825 million shares at $70–78, implying a valuation of $1.3–1.45 trillion (Investrade). - Petition: more than 130,000 signatures, including current and former Nvidia employees and shareholders. - Interim leadership: Colette Kress. Huang led the company he co-founded for 33 years.

5. FAA traces nationwide ground stop to routers and disables their broadcast function

Three days after Friday’s nationwide ground stop, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the FAA has switched off the air traffic control routers’ ability to broadcast data while the investigation continues (ABC News). Tech Times reports that the failure has been traced to two routers flooding the network. Duffy has ordered an investigation of the L3Harris routers and ruled out a cyberattack. He told reporters he expects air traffic to be back to normal by Monday, and Bloomberg reports that airlines are still working to restore schedules. USA Today says facilities in Arkansas and Texas are under scrutiny, and KEYE names an Amarillo radar site among the possible sources. According to the Nevada Globe, Trump has used the outage to demand a “congressional funding overhaul” of air traffic control.

The WSWS Perspective (see below) traces the outage to an L3Harris technician installing a router at a remote site in El Dorado, Arkansas, while replacing copper lines with fiber. The router reportedly sent about 1,000 log-on attempts every four to five seconds, and the instability spread to 17 facilities. As Claude Delphian wrote, the fact that “a single router” could halt national air traffic shows the system “is unsafe under current management.”

Key facts: - Scale: more than 15,000 flights cancelled or delayed on Friday. Departures were halted nationwide for more than four hours. - Cause (preliminary): contractor routers flooding the FAA telecom network. The replaced routers dated from 2001–2006. - Response: router broadcast function disabled and a probe of L3Harris equipment opened. Officials say it was not a hack. - Contractor: L3Harris has run the FAA’s telecom network since 2002 and spent $1.15 billion on share buybacks in 2025.

6. Prague’s largest protest since 1989 opens a week of strikes across Europe

On Sunday, 15 November, a crowd that organizers Milion chvilek estimated at 350,000–450,000 filled Prague’s Letná Plain. Protesters oppose a bill, adopted on 24 October, that would abolish the licence fees funding Czech Television and Czech Radio and pay for them from the state budget instead, which critics say would put them under government control. Expats.cz called it the largest protest since the Velvet Revolution, and UPI and the BBC (via UA.NEWS) also reported the size. Police gave no estimate. Around Prague reports that the government of Andrej Babiš will not withdraw the bill. Speakers demanded its withdrawal and the removal of Tomio Okamura of the far-right SPD as speaker of the lower house. Staff at both broadcasters struck on 3 November.

The rally came at the end of a weekend of mass protest across Europe. In Portugal, tens of thousands marched against a new rental law on 14 November, about 60,000 in Lisbon by organizers’ count. In France, joint youth and “yellow vest” protests drew about 200,000 nationwide according to organizers, and police reported 219 arrests ahead of Tuesday’s strike. Police arrested at least 20 people at a Gen Z march in Berlin, and thousands demonstrated in Edinburgh against plans for large AI data centres in Scotland. The Latin Times reports that Paris hotels face thousands of cancellations ahead of the strike. As Alex Lantier and V. Gnana wrote on the WSWS, these movements share “a common opposition to austerity, police-state rule, neo-fascism,” and to cuts made to pay for war.

Key facts: - 17 November: one-day national strike in France called by the union confederations. - 20 November: a European youth strike called by student unions in Germany, Britain, France, Italy, Spain and Greece. Italian unions strike the same day, alongside a Portuguese public administration strike. - 21 and 24 November: the Portuguese CGTP’s Lisbon march and rally at parliament. The CGTP and UGT have refused to call a national strike. - Portugal’s rental law ends rent-increase caps and allows eviction after two months of arrears instead of three. It passed its first reading because the far-right Chega party abstained.

Four Western governments demand Israel admit 150,000 tents as Gaza floods

The foreign ministers of Australia, Canada, Italy and the UK issued a joint call for Israel to let 150,000 tents and other winter supplies into Gaza immediately. “Stop the delays now,” they said. They called the humanitarian crisis “entirely reversible” and demanded entry for “dual-use items,” the category Israel uses to block shelter materials, water equipment and medical supplies. The first winter storm has flooded displacement camps. Hamas says deliveries remain below half the quantities agreed in the ceasefire. UNICEF urged governments to press Israel to allow more aid, and Yeni Safak, citing medical sources, reports three Palestinians killed in Israeli attacks across Gaza on Sunday. The four governments are all suppliers or diplomatic backers of Israel, and their statement contains no measures against it.

  • Demand: 150,000 tents plus “dual-use items.”
  • Signatories: Australia, Canada, Italy, UK.
  • Deaths: three Palestinians killed on Sunday, according to medical sources.

Ukraine strikes the Crimean Bridge as Russia claims 1,336 drones downed in one night

Ukrainian forces struck the Kerch (Crimean) Bridge, which was then closed to traffic, according to the Kyiv Post. Russia’s defence ministry claimed it downed 1,336 Ukrainian drones overnight, including hundreds aimed at Moscow, according to TRT World. Volleys of this size are now routine. On 15 November the briefing reported about 1,200 drones on Friday. President Zelensky claimed Ukraine has “neutralized” 55 percent of Russia’s refining capacity (TVP World, EA WorldView). Separately, Ukrainian officials say Russia has looted more than 35,000 objects from museums in occupied territory. The strikes come three days before the Shenzhen summit, where Washington has said Ukraine will not be on the agenda.

  • Bridge: Kerch crossing shut after the strike. The extent of the damage has not been confirmed.
  • Drones: Russia claims 1,336 intercepted. Independent figures are not available.
  • Refining: Zelensky’s 55 percent figure cannot be checked and should be treated as a wartime claim.

Estonia to seal Russian border from 1 December

Foreign Minister Margus Tsahkna announced that Estonia will close all its border crossings with Russia from 1 December, citing a “failed sabotage attempt” against a defence company. No details of the alleged plot have been made public. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas confirmed that a suspected Russian “shadow fleet” tanker boarded by EU naval forces in the Mediterranean was flying a false flag. Closing the border will cut off families and cross-border workers in Narva, a city with a large Russian-speaking majority.

  • Closure date: 1 December, all crossings.
  • Shadow fleet: the tanker boarding is the latest in a series of EU interdictions at sea.

Canary Islands record 1,500 migrant arrivals in a single day

About 1,500 migrants reached Spain’s Canary Islands in one day, the highest daily total since 2020, according to the Spanish agency EFE. The Atlantic route from West Africa is one of the deadliest migration routes in the world. The arrivals come as the EU prepares a new Frontex regulation (see WSWS coverage) and while Spain is campaigning for the 29 November general election.

Stellantis sale of Brampton plant to armoured-vehicle maker provokes Ottawa threat

Stellantis Canada has agreed to sell its Brampton Assembly Plant, idle since December 2023, to Roshel Inc., a maker of armoured military vehicles. More than 2,200 laid-off autoworkers who had been waiting three years for recall will lose their jobs. The CTV News and Toronto Star weekend features carried their verdict in their headlines: “betrayal.” The Globe and Mail and Automotive News report that Ottawa and Ontario are putting together an “aggressive package” to persuade Stellantis to cancel the sale. WardsAuto reports that the federal government threatened legal action. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly has given the company 30 days to repay “all or part” of the roughly $222 million in subsidies paid out of a $529 million deal, after Stellantis moved promised Jeep Compass production to Belvidere, Illinois. Roshel has supplied nearly 3,000 vehicles to Ukraine and has also sold vehicles to US ICE. As Carl Bronski wrote on the WSWS, the conversion from auto to military production is a global trend driven by rearmament. The Carney government has pledged to spend 5 percent of GDP on defence and related infrastructure by 2035.

  • Jobs: 2,200 direct jobs. The WSWS estimates 10,000 more in supply and support.
  • Unifor: about 17,000 Detroit Three workers in Canada have been without a contract since 21 September, despite a 97.3 percent strike vote.

Chinese Premier Li Qiang arrived in Manila for the 29th China–ASEAN Summit at the invitation of President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. The Philippines is Washington’s frontline ally in its dispute with China over the South China Sea. The US and nine partners signed an agreement to secure AI supply chains, a step in Washington’s effort to organise allied production of chips and minerals outside China’s reach. In a related regional story, Indonesia apologised to Malaysia and Singapore for haze from its forest fires.

AI selloff spreads through Asia as Japan’s economy contracts

Japan’s economy shrank at a 0.1 percent annualised rate in July–September, its first contraction in a year, Anadolu reported, without giving a breakdown. The figure came out as Tokyo stocks fell more than 1 percent on Monday on semiconductor weakness tied to the Nvidia crisis. Japan’s exporters depend heavily on AI-related demand. Nick Beams noted on the WSWS that Capital Economics estimates about 80 percent of current world trade growth comes from AI-related goods. He quoted HSBC’s Frederic Neumann: “There is very little that can be done to prevent a global recession should the AI boom collapse.” The yen at about 159.75 to the dollar sits close to levels that have previously brought intervention by Japan’s Ministry of Finance.

The week’s main events are Nvidia’s results and Anthropic’s IPO. Morningstar called it “a big week ahead,” and Moomoo’s weekly setup asks whether the IPO can “revive the AI trade as yields rise.” India went the other way: the Sensex gained about 500 points in early trade (NDTV Profit).

Republicans block effort to impeach Hegseth over the Iran war

Republicans blocked efforts to impeach War Secretary Pete Hegseth over his conduct of the Iran war, according to an AFP-sourced report on Yahoo News. Republican leaders had earlier signalled that enough defections might allow an impeachment to go ahead. The vote comes as the outgoing Congress closes and Democrats prepare to take control after their midterm gains, which brought what the Detroit News calls a “historic freshman wave” in Michigan alone. Democrats are now attacking the administration over the conduct of the war and China policy, not over the war itself (see Top stories, Nvidia). In a separate case, the WSJ reports that Congress’s attending physician resigned after an inspector general found lax handling of drugs.

ICE detains a pregnant US citizen and a Maryland student as courts push back

A lawyer told the San Francisco Chronicle that ICE agents pepper-sprayed and detained a pregnant US citizen in Modesto, California. The Modesto Bee reports, citing advocates, that agents put her face down on the ground at a Stanislaus County facility. A judge blocked the deportation of Oscar Belgal, a witness to an ICE shooting in New York (ABC7). A Washington state court found that a county violated the state’s sanctuary statute by cooperating with ICE and ordered the sheriff to stop. ICE is also running a new recruitment drive in Florida.

  • Citizens targeted: the Modesto case adds to a growing record of US citizens detained by immigration agents.
  • Sanctuary law: the Washington ruling enforces state limits on local cooperation with federal agents.

US loses measles elimination status after 26 years

The United States has lost the measles elimination status it held since 2000. Forbes, ABC News and others reported the decision over the weekend, and the New York Times ran a long feature, “What it took for America to wipe out measles.” Elimination status is lost when a chain of transmission continues for 12 months or more. The 15 November briefing reported this year’s record of more than 5,400 cases and kindergarten MMR coverage of 92.4 percent. Local reports in the past 24 hours show how widely the virus is spreading:

  • Deaths: a second child has died in Wisconsin’s outbreak (Iron Mountain Daily News).
  • Spread: measles is “gaining a foothold again” in Minnesota, new cases are confirmed in Monroe County, and Pennsylvania has published an outbreak map.
  • Vaccination: Kansas measles vaccination rates have fallen 4 percent (KWCH).
  • International: Nine.com.au carries warnings that Australia is heading in the “wrong direction,” and Scimex has collected expert reactions.

Losing elimination status puts the US alongside countries where measles circulates continuously. It follows years of cuts to public health and a federal health leadership that has encouraged vaccine scepticism.

Congo’s Ebola outbreak passes 5,300 deaths at six months

Six months after it was declared, the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo is “defying control,” Bloomberg reports, with conflict and hunger driving the crisis. The Cool Down puts the death toll at 5,387 and says fighting and unpaid wages for health workers are stalling the response. DNE Africa reports about 400 new cases a week. Other reports put deaths above 5,500, and Nigeria’s PM News, citing the WHO, says the epicentres are “under control” and that 3,000 people have recovered. The National argues that the outbreak “exposes Covid’s unlearnt lessons.” The figures show mass deaths from a preventable disease in a region devastated by wars over its mineral resources, while international health funding is being cut.

Arts and culture

Czech public broadcasting and Ukraine’s museums: culture in the line of fire

The Letná demonstration (see Top stories) was at bottom a defence of cultural institutions. Czech Television and Czech Radio are among the main producers of Czech drama, documentary and music programming, and their staff struck on 3 November against a bill that would make their funding depend on the government’s annual budget. The Babiš government, in coalition with Okamura’s far-right SPD, is following the path Orbán took in Hungary and the PiS government in Poland: control of public broadcasters is a means of controlling what history and social reality the public sees. For hundreds of thousands, the 1989 echoes are a living memory. Many of the opposition parties now leading the protest have also voted for austerity and NATO rearmament, which bears on where the movement can go.

Ukrainian officials say Russia has looted more than 35,000 objects from museums in occupied territory (ArtDependence). The figure cannot be independently checked. Wartime destruction and removal of heritage is a recurring crime of modern war. It is also a propaganda weapon on both sides, and the claim should be read with that in mind.

Perspective published: “The nationwide air traffic shutdown and the breakdown of American capitalism” by Claude Delphian (16 November 2026). Read it in full on wsws.org.

The Perspective’s distinctive contribution is its historical argument. The SMART AI traffic system, which had been rolled out to all 22 en route centres days before the outage, went to Air Space Intelligence under a 12-year, $875 million contract. Delphian traces the line back through Carter’s 1978 Airline Deregulation Act and Reagan’s 1981 firing of more than 11,000 striking PATCO controllers. He cites Paul Volcker calling the defeat of the strike the most important single action against inflation.

  • Staffing: in 1981 controllers handled about 14,200 commercial flights a day. Today about 11,000 fully certified controllers handle more than 44,000. In May the FAA cut its target for certified controllers from 14,633 to 12,563. In February 2025, weeks after the Potomac midair collision killed 67 people, the administration fired nearly 400 FAA staff.
  • NATCA: Congress approved a 3.8 percent raise, but the FAA paid only 1 percent. It released the rest in October, after NATCA agreed to badge tracking and demand-based scheduling.
  • Programme: public ownership of critical infrastructure under workers’ democratic control. Expropriation of the financial oligarchy and an end to the war budget. Rank-and-file committees at every facility, independent of NATCA and linked with pilots, mechanics and flight attendants. A political break with the Democrats.

This week in history: November 16–22

  • 1926 — British miners return to work (22 November). After a lockout of nearly seven months, the miners went back on the owners’ terms: wage cuts, longer hours and the end of the national wages agreement. The TUC had called off the May General Strike after nine days, leaving the miners isolated. The defeat set the British labour movement back for a decade. Trotsky analysed it as the product of the TUC General Council’s betrayal and the Stalinist Anglo-Russian Committee’s cover for it.
  • 1951 — Ismailia clashes (18 November). British troops and Egyptian police fought in the Suez Canal city after Egypt abrogated the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty. According to a contemporary US report, 85 percent of the Egyptian workers employed by the British in the Canal Zone had left their jobs. The struggle led to the January 1952 Cairo fire and the Free Officers’ coup that July.
  • 1976 — Spain’s Law for Political Reform (18 November). The Francoist Cortes voted 425–59, with 13 abstentions, for Adolfo Suárez’s law, which scheduled the first elections since 1936 and the Cortes’ own dissolution. It came six days after a general strike of more than half a million workers on 12 November. The law excluded the Communist Party and parties to its left. It was the start of the “transition,” in which the Stalinists and Social Democrats agreed to preserve the Francoist state apparatus and the monarchy.
  • 2001 — Danish Social Democrats routed (20 November). The party’s vote fell from 35.9 to 29.1 percent, ending its position as the largest party in parliament, which it had held since 1924. Anders Fogh Rasmussen formed a Liberal–Conservative government that depended on the far-right Danish People’s Party, which won 12 percent after an anti-immigrant campaign. The Social Democrats later adopted much of that agenda.
