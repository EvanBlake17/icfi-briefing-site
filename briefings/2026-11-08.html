1. US launches Task Force Iron Gate to choke Iran’s land and Caspian routes

US Central Command announced “Task Force Iron Gate” on Saturday. The Tampa-based unit will try to stop Iran moving weapons and materials across its land borders and the Caspian Sea. This takes the months-long naval blockade of Iranian ports onto land. The Caspian is Iran’s direct link to Russia and Kazakhstan, and its land borders run with Iraq, Turkey, the South Caucasus, Turkmenistan, Afghanistan and Pakistan. Enforcing the task force’s mandate would therefore bring US forces into friction with trade involving Russia and China, not only Iran.

The announcement came on the eve of November 9. US officials told Fox News that night’s new moon made it favourable for operations, and the White House has been holding war-planning meetings. Qatari-mediated talks are reportedly continuing.

Key facts: - Cooper: CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper said the task force reinforces “the success of our ironclad blockade against Iran at sea.” - Interdictions: Since July, US forces have redirected 147 commercial vessels and disabled five that refused to comply. On Saturday CENTCOM said it fired on the stern of the St. Kitts-flagged cargo vessel M/V Huda in the Gulf of Oman to disable it as it tried to break the blockade. - Explosions in Iran: IRNA reported blasts on Qeshm Island and in Jask, Sirik and Bandar Abbas, all on the Strait of Hormuz coast. It did not say what caused them, and no casualties were reported. - Mines: The IRGC Navy said two oil tankers struck sea mines near Hormuz. Maritime authorities had not independently confirmed this. - Rescue: The UAE said it rescued 22 people from a cargo ship that caught fire after the mine reports.

2. Far-right gunman kills six in Krakow’s main square

At about 3 pm on Saturday a man drove onto Krakow’s Main Market Square and fired at random people from his car with an AR-15-style semi-automatic rifle. He killed six people, four women and two men, and wounded 15, including police officers and foreign nationals. A British national was among the dead. Police shot the suspect and detained him. He is a Polish citizen and is believed to have acted alone. Three bodies, two women and a man, were later found at his home. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said they appeared to be his immediate family.

Interior Minister Marcin Kierwinski said the suspect appeared “fascinated by far-right ideologies” and held ultra-nationalist views. Tusk called the attack terrorism and declared two days of national mourning. Poland’s government has spent years promoting nationalism and one of the largest rearmament programmes in Europe, and the official response so far has passed over the political climate that this produces.

Key facts: - The attack: The suspect wore military-style clothing, and several more weapons were found in his car. He returned fire during his arrest and wounded two officers, one of them critically. - Precedent: This is Poland’s deadliest shooting since the fall of Stalinism in 1989. It follows a wave of stabbings in October, including three school attacks in less than a week, which Tusk called a “cult of violence.” - Firearms: Poland issued more than 50,000 firearms licences in 2025, bringing the total to 411,000. - Reaction: Condolences came from President Karol Nawrocki, UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas and Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Polish officials have repeatedly blamed Russia for sabotage on Polish soil, but have made no such claim about this attack.

3. Herzog picks Eisenkot to form Israel’s next government

President Isaac Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot, the former chief of staff, the mandate to form a government after the October 27 election. He passed over Benjamin Netanyahu, whose right-wing and religious bloc remains the largest at 53 seats. Eisenkot has 28 days to assemble a coalition, with a possible 14-day extension.

Eisenkot’s endorsement by 67 Knesset members depends on Arab-led parties, and he has ruled out governing with them. Joint List leader Ahmad Tibi said he expects a minority government that rests on their “external support.” That would be a government kept in office by Palestinian citizens’ votes but closed to their participation. Netanyahu has gone on the attack: “Such a government, dependent on Islamists, will dismantle the state of Israel from within.”

Key facts: - Mandate: Eisenkot was nominated with 67 endorsements to Netanyahu’s 46. - Netanyahu’s leverage: If no government is formed, the Knesset dissolves and new elections follow, with Netanyahu staying on as caretaker prime minister. - Continuity: Eisenkot led the army during the Gaza war. The reporting gives no sign that his coalition talks involve ending the occupation or the war.

4. France’s cost-of-living protests enter a fourth weekend as unions claim 250,000

For the fourth weekend running, protests against rising living costs spread across France on Saturday. The Interior Ministry counted about 146,000 marchers nationwide, 46,000 of them in Paris. Unions claimed 250,000, with 75,000 in the capital. Days earlier, an estimated 575,000 joined the union day of action on November 5. The movement began in mid-September over conditions in under-resourced high schools and has since taken in wages, fuel prices and public services. Students still lead it, and workers from several sectors have joined.

The government responded with force. It deployed about 70,000 security personnel, and Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said authorities were “ready and waiting” for rioters. Police used tear gas and batons in Paris, saying about 200 people attacked officers who were arresting a man for setting fire to a bin. In Lyon they used tear gas on several hundred people blocking a road.

Key facts: - Mélenchon’s launch: Jean-Luc Mélenchon presented his programme to about 17,000 supporters on Saturday. It includes retirement at 60, a €1,700 monthly minimum wage, leaving NATO, partial renationalisation, a 2 percent minimum tax on billionaires’ wealth and an end to repaying France’s debt to the ECB. - Polls: Mélenchon polls at 17.5–18 percent and leads the left. Marine Le Pen leads overall, and an October Toluna Harris Interactive poll had her beating Mélenchon 70 percent to 30 in a run-off. - The government’s line: Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu on the debt proposal: “Borrowing money and deciding not to give it back – that’s called theft.” - The unions: Al Jazeera’s correspondent reports that union leaderships, “weakened in recent years,” feel “renewed momentum.” The unions are pressing the government for higher pay and better facilities.

The movement’s demands are fuelling an election campaign whose two front-runners are a far-right nationalist and a left-nationalist reformist.

6. Saudi-led strikes kill 33 near Sanaa as Houthis fire missiles at Riyadh

Houthi Health Ministry spokesperson Anees al-Asbahi said coalition air attacks on al-Shariyah village, near Sanaa, killed 33 people, including eight women and 13 children, and wounded at least 39. On Sunday the Saudi-led coalition said its air defences destroyed three Houthi ballistic missiles, two aimed at Riyadh and one at the oil-producing Eastern Province.

The fighting is part of the government’s “Dawn of Yemen” offensive to retake Houthi-held territory, including Sanaa. In September the Houthis seized much of the Red Sea coast, and the government campaign began in October.

Drone attack on a Colombian army base kills three soldiers

A drone attack on a military base in Santa Rosa del Sur, in the foothills of the San Lucas range in northern Colombia, killed three soldiers and wounded at least 16. No one has claimed responsibility. The gold-rich area is contested by the Gulf Clan, the ELN and groups led by former FARC members. El Hierro declares an emergency after 3,300 migrant arrivals in a week

El Hierro, the smallest of the Canary Islands with about 11,400 residents, declared an emergency after more than 3,300 people arrived in about 30 boats since early November. Maritime rescue services rescued 3,261 people off the Canaries in the first six days of the month. Regional leader Fernando Clavijo called the situation “absolutely unsustainable” and said the pace is nearing that of the 2024 record, when more than 46,000 people arrived.

  • Deaths: Two people were found dead on a boat this week, and survivors told AFP that five more probably died during the voyage. At least nine people have died off the islands in recent weeks.
  • Conditions: Alpidio Armas, head of the island council, said conditions at the hospital have become “undesirable,” and officials are looking for temporary housing. Buses have been diverted to migration operations and medical staff brought in from other islands.
  • EU border policy: Until this surge, arrivals from January to October were down 5.5 percent year on year at 13,327. Al Jazeera attributes the earlier drop to tighter surveillance and deals with origin and transit states. Patrols off Senegal, Mauritania and Morocco have pushed departures south to The Gambia, Guinea-Bissau and Guinea, making the Atlantic route longer and more lethal.

Russian diesel tankers turn away from the US

The diesel arrangement Trump announced after his October 9 call with Putin is coming apart before any fuel has arrived. Human brain tissue grows to half the brain volume of engineered mice

A Stanford team led by psychiatrist Sergiu Pasca reports in Nature that it engineered mice to develop without most of the cerebral cortex and much of the hippocampus. At two days old, the mice were injected with human brain organoids, clusters of neural cells grown in the lab from stem cells. Transplants took in 25 of 29 mice. Within three months the human tissue had grown about fivefold, connected to the mouse blood supply, filled more than 90 percent of the empty cavities and made up roughly half the brain’s volume.

  • Function: The mice walked and vocalised mostly normally, and they did better in a maze than mice without the grafts, which suggests the human tissue contributed to cognition. Some human cells formed von Economo neurons, which are rare and normally absent in mice.
  • Disease modelling: Mice exposed to low oxygen showed brain damage and impaired gait, which could give researchers a model for cerebral palsy. The authors also point to schizophrenia and dementia.
  • Ethics: The team ended the experiments when the human cells reached six months, before they formed wider connections with the mouse brain. UC San Diego sociologist John Evans said the study is not alarming in itself, but the field has no clear “stopping points” between mice and non-human primates.

The work is a major technical achievement in studying human brain development. Whether research like it continues to be done openly and for public benefit depends on scientific institutions that are now being defunded and placed under political control.

Lincoln in the Bardo at the Metropolitan Opera

Missy Mazzoli’s opera of George Saunders’s 2017 Booker Prize-winning novel premiered at the Met on October 19 and is still drawing strong reviews. It runs alongside Cherubini’s Medea and Puccini’s Tosca. The novel is set in a Georgetown cemetery in February 1862, on the night after the burial of Lincoln’s 11-year-old son Willie. It is a choral meditation on grief, told through the voices of the dead, against the background of a war whose dead would number in the hundreds of thousands. Lincoln’s private grief becomes the personal side of a national bloodletting over slavery.

New American work rarely enters the Met’s mainstream repertory. This premiere comes as the federal government guts arts funding, and as the history of the Civil War and Reconstruction is being rewritten in museums and classrooms. Its success with audiences shows a hunger for serious art about historical catastrophe and collective loss.

Martin Sherman and Matthew McFrederick share the Theatre Book Prize

For the first time in its history, the Society for Theatre Research’s Theatre Book Prize has been shared. It went jointly to Martin Sherman’s memoir On the Boardwalk and Matthew McFrederick’s study Staging Beckett in London, and was announced at Jermyn Street Theatre on November 5. Sherman wrote Bent (1979), the play that brought the Nazi persecution of gay men in the camps to the stage. McFrederick’s book traces how Beckett, whose work grew out of the devastation of the Second World War, was received in London. The pairing honours both a playwright’s personal testimony and scholarship on theatre’s postwar reckoning with barbarism.

Jacobin / DSA

  • “Enough with the ‘public option’ already” (John Canham-Clyne, November 7): The author rejects the public option as a delay tactic that would make public plans copy private insurers. He calls for “improved and expanded Medicare for All,” along with interim measures such as regulated hospital prices, a lower Medicare age and the end of Medicare Advantage. He attacks “corporate Democrats,” naming Slotkin, and praises Abdul El-Sayed. Position: This is a factional argument inside the Democratic Party. The strategy is “public pressure” and progressive primaries, aimed at a party that rejected single-payer under Obama and Biden. The private insurance industry is treated as a policy mistake, not as part of the capitalist profit system.
  • “Trump hurt average Americans, so they voted against the GOP” (Branko Marcetic, November 6): Marcetic argues that Republican “populism” could not be switched on because the party governed against working people. Position: The vote is presented purely as a rejection of the GOP. There is no account of what the Democrats will do with their majorities: funding the war on Iran, municipal austerity under DSA-backed mayors, and their pledge of “bipartisan common ground.” By leaving this out, the piece lends the Democrats credit.
  • “In Finland, the left can lead on fair AI policy” (Mike Watson, November 7): The article promotes Finland’s Left Alliance, now polling above 12 percent under Minja Koskela, before the April 18 election. It focuses on the order halting Google’s data centres and on a proposal that AI productivity gains should shorten working hours. Koskela’s demand is that the boom “be reflected in increasing tax revenues, jobs, and expertise.” Position: This is national-reformist. AI is to be taxed and steered for “Finns” by a parliamentary party competing with the Social Democrats for a place in government. Neither workers’ control of technology nor international coordination against the tech monopolies comes up.

Assessment: Jacobin’s post-election output is aimed at channelling the anti-Trump vote into a left-Democratic legislative agenda in the US, and into left parties seeking a share of government abroad. It does not mention the Iran war at all.

Left Voice

  • “The decline of Macronism and the crisis of state authority” (Juan Chingo, November 6): Chingo argues that the October 17 mobilisation opened a “pre-revolutionary period.” He warns that Mélenchon could lead a popular front “that would channel popular aspirations toward an institutional way out,” and that Le Pen offers a “Bonapartist” restoration of order. He calls for assemblies in workplaces, schools and neighbourhoods, an “active general strike” and an amnesty for arrested protesters. Position: The warning against a popular front is correct, but the diagnosis does not lead to building a revolutionary party. The CGT’s November 5 call is read as “pressure from below,” which credits the bureaucracy with a left turn. The proposed organs of struggle are left without any programme for taking power. The WSWS’s November 2 article “Révolution permanente tries to bolster union bureaucracies” charged that this orientation props up the union apparatus.
  • “Announcing the English-language edition of Socialist Strategy and Military Art” (Adrien Masson, November 7): 34 Press and Left Voice are publishing Emilio Albamonte and Matías Maiello’s 2017 PTS book, which draws on Clausewitz, Gramsci and Trotsky’s “art of winning.” Position: The book is the theoretical basis of the Trotskyist Fraction. Its Gramsci-inflected framework of “strategy” and “hegemony” treats revolutionary leadership as a matter of strategic technique, detached from the Fourth International’s historical struggle against Stalinism and Pabloism. The English launch is a bid for intellectual standing among radicalising youth.
  • Also noted: A podcast on the 1940–41 Rapp-Coudert purge of New York college teachers draws parallels with today’s repression of the Palestine movement. It is useful history, but it is presented as a civil-liberties issue.

Assessment: Left Voice is positioning itself as the “revolutionary” option within the French and German movements. In practice it is a ginger group pressing the existing union and left-party leaderships. No direct attacks on the ICFI or WSWS were found in today’s material.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Krakow massacre: Poland’s nationalist war drive breeds a far-right killer

A gunman described by the interior minister as “fascinated by far-right ideologies” killed six people in Krakow’s main square, along with what appear to be three members of his own family. It is Poland’s worst shooting since 1989. The official response treats it as an isolated act of terror, while the government that promotes militarism, nationalism and one of Europe’s largest rearmament programmes escapes scrutiny. An article could link the attack to the October wave of stabbings, the growth of gun licensing and the official political climate across the NATO front-line states.
