1. Houthis hit Aramco’s Dhahran offices after Saudi-led coalition bombs Sanaa oil depot

The Saudi–Houthi war turned to direct attacks on oil infrastructure on Tuesday. Hours after the Saudi-led coalition ordered civilians out of the Yemeni Oil Company’s al-Sabah central station in Sanaa and then bombed it, the Houthis hit an administrative building of Saudi Aramco in Dhahran, the company’s headquarters city in the Eastern Province. The Saudi Energy Ministry reported a fire but no casualties. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree declared Saudi oil facilities “a legitimate target,” told civilians to leave Aramco’s Dhahran headquarters, King Khalid International Airport and an Aramco-affiliated refinery in Riyadh, and promised a “harsh and painful” reprisal on Riyadh for any further strike on Sanaa. Saudi air defenses intercepted missiles fired at the capital. Falling debris injured four people and damaged 15 homes.

Trump told reporters the Houthis were “behaving very badly” and said he is considering joining the Saudi-backed ground counteroffensive to push them off the Red Sea coast and the Bab al-Mandab. He also floated asking Turkish President Erdogan to intervene. A Houthi leader warned the US of a “swift and painful” response if it does. The danger is a US-led war in the Gulf spreading to the Arabian Peninsula, centered on the world’s largest oil company.

Key facts: - Coalition strikes: Beyond al-Sabah, coalition aircraft hit oil storage tanks at Ras Isa port and an oil station in Hodeidah province. Spokesman Maj. Gen. Turki al-Maliki said the Houthis used the site’s underground depots to store missiles and drones. The Yemeni Oil Company said its tanks were empty, but cars queued at Sanaa gas stations. - Airlines: Air France-KLM, Air India, Etihad, Lufthansa, Delta, Gulf Air and Qatar Airways have suspended flights to Saudi Arabia and neighboring states. - Ground war: The government’s “Dawn of Yemen” counteroffensive carried out nine airstrikes in Taiz governorate. The Yemeni Network for Rights and Freedom says Houthi fire killed at least five civilians in Taiz in 24 hours, including a five-year-old girl, and wounded 17. A hospital in the encircled city was hit, and residents are rationing food. - Saudi cabinet: The cabinet, chaired by Mohammed bin Salman, said the Houthis will face “consequences.” The US Embassy says Americans have been arrested in Riyadh for photographing the aftermath of strikes. - Toll: The UN counts at least 46 civilians killed and more than 130 injured in Yemen since August. ICRC President Mirjana Spoljaric urged de-escalation.

2. IMF forecasts outright contraction across the Gulf as it warns Big Tech’s AI debt could trigger a crisis

The International Monetary Fund’s October World Economic Outlook, titled “Resilience under Strain, Urgent Choices,” was released at the annual meetings in Bangkok. It raised global growth slightly to 3.1%, from 3.0% in the previous outlook. But it projects inflation rising to 4.7% after the oil shock from the war on Iran. The Middle East and Central Asia is now forecast to shrink 0.3% in 2026, down from 1.9% growth projected in April, the only region in the world expected to contract. The Fund describes a “split global economy.” A 29% jump in AI-related trade lifts total world trade growth to 8.3%, while energy importers face higher bills and weaker demand. It warns that the expansion depends on “a small number of firms, sectors and economies,” and that the global economy could “soon test the limits of resilience.”

The IMF’s Global Financial Stability Report, released Tuesday, singles out the debt-financed AI buildout as a systemic risk. It warns that “circular” financing among AI firms creates “significant ‘wrong-way risk’” if a key firm fails. Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva said global debt-to-GDP is set to exceed 100%. She warned that high debt in the advanced economies limits governments’ ability to address the cost of living and could produce unrest “maybe on the street,” and she called for “consolidation plans.” As Nick Beams wrote on the WSWS, the IMF’s prescription for the major economies is the austerity it has imposed on poor countries for decades.

Key facts: - Gulf collapse: Projected 2026 GDP is Kuwait −16.7%, Iraq −16%, Qatar −15.6%, Bahrain −11.3%, Iran −7.6% and Saudi Arabia −3.3%. The Saudi decline is partly offset by exports through the East-West pipeline. - Major economies: US +2.3%, China +4.5%, eurozone +1.1%, emerging Asia +5.1%, sub-Saharan Africa +4.5%, Latin America +2.3%. - AI leverage: Analyst Bret Jensen, quoted by Common Dreams, puts the debt of the five largest hyperscalers (Oracle, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, Alphabet) at more than $1.3 trillion on balance sheet, plus about $3 trillion in off-balance-sheet commitments. - Austerity on the poor: An Oxfam report released Monday finds that 24 of 28 countries with IMF loans have been required to impose austerity. Bolivia must cut up to 8.5% of GDP, roughly twice its health budget. Sierra Leone’s required adjustment is more than five times its annual health budget. - Bond market: Pimco’s CIO Dan Ivascyn calls a 6% US 10-year yield “feasible.” The US now spends roughly one dollar in five of federal revenue on interest.

3. Missile hits Kyiv power plant without warning as drones sink a ship off Bulgaria

Trump’s “energy ceasefire” has not stopped the strikes. On Tuesday evening a Russian ballistic missile hit Kyiv’s CHP-4 combined heat and power plant. Mayor Vitali Klitschko reported at least two dead and about 20 wounded, including in a residential building in the Desnianskyi district. Explosions were heard before the air-raid sirens sounded, which is rare. Zelensky wrote: “Once again, no early warning from our partners.” The comment points to Kyiv’s dependence on US intelligence at a moment when Trump has attacked Zelensky over strikes on Russian refineries and said it may be “time for Ukraine to get a new president.” Russian strikes also injured dozens at a university in Rivne.

In the Black Sea, drones struck two cargo ships off Bulgaria, a NATO member, around 1:30 a.m. Tuesday. The Guinea-Bissau-flagged HS Dignity sank off Krapets, near the Romanian border. Five crew were rescued and 11 are missing. The Ukrainian-owned, Maltese-flagged Chelsea 3 was hit by three drones 18 nautical miles offshore. All 15 crew (seven Ukrainians and eight Azerbaijanis) were rescued. It is the second deadly attack off Bulgaria in a week. The 10 crew of a ship sunk last week remain unaccounted for.

Key facts: - Sofia’s caution: Prime Minister Rumen Radev summoned the Russian ambassador but said: “We do not possess solid, clear evidence to level an accusation against any specific party.” - Brussels and NATO: Von der Leyen said “The Black Sea has become another front of escalation of the Russian war in Ukraine.” NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte said the alliance would adjust its posture “as needed.” - Europe arms up: Poland’s defense minister called for Europe to switch to “war-mode” production of ammunition and weapons.

4. Bipartisan senators and Democratic leaders demand Trump scrap Russian diesel deal

After a phone call with Putin on Friday, Trump said Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel. Treasury General License No. 135, valid until April 7, 2027, authorizes Russian diesel sales and imports, including into the United States, which has banned Russian energy imports since 2022. The fuel crisis behind the deal comes from the war on Iran: diesel sells for $6.28 a gallon, against $3.65 a year ago. Delta has already cut its profit outlook over a roughly $6 billion rise in annual fuel costs.

The opposition in Washington is coming from the war hawks. A bipartisan group of senators, including Republicans Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski, Thom Tillis and John Curtis, says the deal violates the 2022 law and is designed to lower pump prices before the November 3 midterms: “The United States should not provide Putin with a financial lifeline while Russia continues to kill Ukrainian civilians.” Senate Democrats Chuck Schumer, Jeanne Shaheen and Elizabeth Warren called it a “total betrayal of Ukraine, our European allies and American national security,” and insisted “Putin only responds to strength.” Trump replied on Truth Social: “I made the right choice for the American People — ALWAYS, AMERICA FIRST!” As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, the Democrats are attacking Trump from the right, as insufficiently aggressive toward Moscow.

Key facts: - Twelve-senator letter: Six Democrats and six Republicans called the deal “a gift to our adversary and a betrayal of our Allies.” They demand that License No. 135 be rescinded and that the Graham sanctions law, which mandates tariffs of up to 100% on the largest buyers of Russian oil, be enforced, aimed above all at China. - Tillis: He called Russia “the aggressor” and said the response should extend “back to Crimea.” - Wall Street Journal: Its editorial board argues the sanctions relief directly funds Putin’s war.

5. Iran claims third tanker strike in two days as MI5 says Tehran is targeting UK military sites

The UK Maritime Trade Operations Center reported that an oil tanker was hit by a projectile while leaving the Strait of Hormuz on Monday. The crew is reported safe. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed the attack, its third on shipping in two days. There have been at least 18 attacks on vessels in or near the strait since the start of October, and the IRGC says it is targeting what it calls a “US-UAE” oil shuttle moving crude out of the Gulf. Euronews reports further Iranian attacks on two vessels using “new tactics” to target shipping. Brent rose 0.5% to $106.24 in early European trading, and Dutch TTF gas futures traded above €86 per megawatt-hour, near a four-year high.

In London, MI5 Director General Ken McCallum used his annual threat speech to say Iran has made a major shift this year, with “Iranian ambitions to come at harder targets, like military sites.” He said the authorities had made 50 arrests over the past year to disrupt Iran-linked operations. He also named Russia “aggressor number one” in Europe and said its hybrid attacks are increasing.

Key facts: - Bases: On October 4, after a security breach, the US withdrew its B-1 and B-52 bombers from RAF Fairford, which it uses for strikes on Iran. No one has been charged over the Fairford incident. Two Latvian men were separately arrested for trespassing at RAF Molesworth, a US and NATO intelligence base. - McCallum’s caveat: He doubted Iran’s UK operatives could mount complex attacks such as explosive-drone strikes, but said the IRGC has “quite deep experience” with hybrid technology closer to home.

6. Pope Leo XIV has part of his lung removed after cancer is confirmed

Pope Leo XIV, the first US-born pontiff, underwent thoracic surgery at Rome’s Gemelli Hospital on Tuesday afternoon. Surgeons removed the lower lobe of his right lung by video-assisted surgery “to ensure a radical resection.” A histological exam during the operation confirmed lung adenocarcinoma, as pre-operative tests had suggested. Before admission the pope had described a “localized nodule” at “a very early stage.”

Key facts: - Operation: About three hours in the operating room, with the procedure itself lasting about 90 minutes. Doctors reported no complications. - Next steps: Pathology results on the removed tissue are expected in the coming days. The planned one-week hospital stay is unchanged, and the Press Office will issue an update Wednesday morning. - Statement: Issued by Dr. Luigi Carbone and lead surgeon Prof. Stefano Margaritora.

Ukrainian ex-officer goes on trial in Hamburg for Nord Stream sabotage

The first public trial over the September 26, 2022 destruction of the Nord Stream pipelines opened Wednesday at the Hamburg Higher Regional Court. The sole defendant, a 50-year-old former Ukrainian officer identified as Serhii K., is accused of leading a team of divers and an explosives expert. Prosecutors say the team sailed a yacht hired in Rostock to Bornholm and attached timed military-grade charges for unidentified “Ukrainian state entities.” Nord Stream 1 had supplied about half of Germany’s gas imports. Prosecutors say an attack of this kind on civilian infrastructure would amount to a war crime. The defendant denies involvement, and so does Kyiv. A second suspect, Vladimir Z., is being extradited from Croatia. The trial runs into February. Berlin, Ukraine’s second-largest backer, is prosecuting a Ukrainian state operation that destroyed its own energy infrastructure, and the case has strained relations between Berlin and Kyiv.

Russia’s 2027 budget sends 44% of federal spending to the military

Russia’s draft 2027–2029 budget gives defense and security 44% of federal spending, a record and double the share for social programs. Social spending falls 7%, to 6.65 trillion roubles (€71 billion). Over three years the military gets 50 trillion roubles (€533 billion), against 28.6 trillion for health care. This year’s military outlay reached 17 trillion roubles, against 12.1 trillion budgeted.

  • Health cuts: Ambulance funding falls by more than a quarter, the regional outpatient clinic program is halved, and the national cancer program drops from 44.9 billion roubles to 3.9 billion.
  • “Coffin money” cut: Regional death payments to soldiers’ families have been slashed since autumn 2025. Ulyanovsk cut its payment from 1 million roubles to 50,000, and Bashkortostan from 2 million to at most 100,000.
  • Inflation: Official inflation has topped 8% for most of two years. The Duma takes up the budget October 22.

French student protests draw up to 480,000 as Paris clashes put 11 police in hospital

Tuesday’s nationwide day of action by high school and university students drew 161,500 people by police count, 41,500 of them in Paris. Unions put the national figure near 480,000. At Place Vauban near Les Invalides, police used water cannon, tear gas and stun grenades. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said 11 officers were hospitalized. The government has suspended one type of stun grenade after a 15-year-old lost his hand outside a school earlier this month. The movement began in the Paris suburbs in mid-September over teacher shortages, decaying buildings and overcrowded classes. As the National Assembly opened its budget debate, Greta Thunberg joined students in Saint-Denis. Separate mass demonstrations against the cost of living and fuel prices are called for Saturday, and many students hope the two movements will merge.

China sentences three to death over Myanmar scam compounds

The Quanzhou Intermediate People’s Court in Fujian sentenced Wei Huairen, Chen Dawei and Liao Jingfang of the “Wei family criminal gang” to death. Three others received life terms and 55 were jailed for between three and 24 years. The gang’s 14 compounds in Myanmar lured “thousands” of Chinese nationals with promises of high wages and earned more than $1.8 billion from fraud, drug trafficking, prostitution and casinos. Its crimes included the murder of at least two Chinese nationals. The IOM estimates that Southeast Asian scam centers employ as many as 300,000 people, many of them trafficked and held in prison-like conditions.

China and North Korea open the long-stalled New Yalu River Bridge

Xinhua says the New Yalu River Bridge between Dandong and Sinuiju opens to traffic Thursday. China finished its side in 2014, but Pyongyang left its side incomplete after Beijing backed UN sanctions over its nuclear tests. The opening follows Xi Jinping’s June visit to Pyongyang, his first since 2019, and a Russia–North Korea road bridge opened in September. Chinese exports to the North hit a six-year high of $2.3 billion last year. North Korea has sent thousands of troops to fight for Russia in Ukraine.

Colombia’s peace tribunal charges 10 ex-FARC leaders over crimes against Indigenous peoples

The Special Jurisdiction for Peace (JEP) charged 10 former FARC members, including three from its last secretariat and ex-senator Julian Gallo, with war crimes and crimes against humanity against five Indigenous peoples in southeastern Colombia between 1996 and 2016. The charges cover torture, murder, forced displacement and the planting of landmines on Indigenous land. Magistrate Catalina Diaz said: “Today we are delivering justice to the most forgotten peoples of a segregated Colombia.” The JEP can impose only non-custodial sentences. Allies of far-right President Abelardo de la Espriella, a former lawyer for right-wing paramilitaries, are pushing a cut of nearly 17% to its 2027 budget. The Truth Commission counted 450,664 killed in the conflict between 1985 and 2018, most of them by paramilitaries and state forces.

UnitedHealth’s adjusted EPS of $4.69, against $4.06 expected, led it to raise full-year guidance to at least $20.50, extracted from patients and the government.

Corporate press, DSA falsify UAW election, invent “mandate” for “president 6 percent” Shawn Fain — Tom Hall

Hall shows that the 2026 UAW presidential election was a mass boycott. Ballots were counted for only 9.06% of roughly 1.1 million eligible members, the lowest turnout in any national US union election and below the 2022 vote. Fain’s 68.1% of ballots cast is about 6.1% of the membership, “roughly one member in sixteen,” and that figure includes staff and appointees whose jobs depend on the apparatus. Hall gives three causes: many workers again never received ballots; the monitor’s rules restricted Will Lehman’s campaign, barring WSWS endorsement and removing his campaigners from plants while officials campaigned openly; and, most decisively, workers “regard the UAW bureaucracy not as their organization but as an instrument of management and the state.”

The Wall Street Journal, Detroit Free Press, Guardian, Common Dreams and Labor Notes all reported a Fain victory and treated turnout as a footnote. Hall’s sharpest fire is aimed at the DSA. At Harvard, after a 40-day strike, 53 ballots were cast. At Columbia, about 29 came from some 3,000 members. Brooks, who was ousted after the monitor’s corruption probes, is seeking to return to a job that paid $238,294 in 2025.
