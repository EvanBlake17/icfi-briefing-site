1. Russian missile and drone barrage kills 17 in Kyiv region

At least 17 people were killed and 44 wounded in a massive overnight Russian missile and drone attack on Kyiv and the surrounding region, President Volodymyr Zelensky said Wednesday. Russian forces fired 24 ballistic missiles, four Zircon and Oniks cruise missiles and 115 drones. Ukraine’s air force reported intercepting none of the missiles, a failure Ukrainian officials attributed directly to a shortage of interceptor stocks.

The strikes hit civilian logistics infrastructure, including a Nova Poshta parcel-delivery hub in Kyiv’s Zhuliany district and Novus supermarket warehouse facilities. Ukraine responded with strikes on warehouses belonging to the Russian online retailer Wildberries. A separate attack on Sumy killed an elderly woman and two children.

Key facts:

  • Zelensky: “Ballistic missile interceptors — these are what could have saved the lives of those who died today… delays in [partners’] supply or a reluctance to provide anti-ballistic systems leads to precisely these horrific casualties and destruction.”
  • The zero interception rate against ballistic missiles marks a sharp deterioration from earlier phases of the war, when Patriot batteries downed a substantial share of incoming ballistic weapons.
  • The targeting of parcel-delivery and grocery warehousing on both sides reflects the war’s turn toward systematic destruction of civilian logistics and food distribution.
  • The exchange comes as US and European stockpiles of air-defense interceptors are being drawn down simultaneously by the Ukraine war and the widening US-Iran conflict in the Gulf.

2. Israel escalates Gaza strikes, rejects Board of Peace withdrawal terms

Israeli forces killed 19 Palestinians on Saturday and 26 over the weekend, including women and children, while Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu publicly declared that Israel “didn’t agree” to the disarmament plan advanced by Trump’s “Board of Peace.” Netanyahu said the Israeli military will not withdraw “from our current lines” until Hamas is “completely disarmed.”

The Board of Peace, for its part, confirmed that Israeli withdrawal beyond the so-called Yellow Line — which leaves the IDF in control of roughly 52% of the Gaza Strip — will occur “only once decommissioning is complete… light weapons, heavy weapons, and the tunnels alike.” The framework therefore places no timetable on Israeli withdrawal and no constraint on continued strikes, while the blockade and destruction of aid infrastructure continue.

Key facts:

  • Analyst Mtanes Shehadeh said Israel “seeks to continue killing, starvation and siege… without explicitly announcing rejection” of the deal — using the diplomatic process as cover for open-ended military operations.
  • Former Israeli prime minister Naftali Bennett attacked Netanyahu from the right, charging that he “gave the Qataris the keys to Gaza” while Hamas “rehabilitated itself.”
  • The “Yellow Line” arrangement institutionalizes a partition of Gaza, with the majority of the territory’s land area under permanent Israeli military control pending conditions Israel itself defines as unmet.
  • Aid access remains severely restricted; the starvation crisis engineered over the past two years is being displaced from headlines by coverage of the negotiating process.

3. US-Iran war widens across the Gulf as Washington signals interim deal

The US-Iran war that began in February has metastasized into a region-wide conflict. Two tankers were struck in separate incidents near the Strait of Hormuz on August 1 — one hit by a projectile that damaged its engine room, another near Khasab, Oman, struck by an explosion. Kuwait’s military said it detected and destroyed multiple Iranian drones over its territory beginning at dawn the same day, with falling shrapnel hitting a government facility in northern Kuwait and civilian property on Bubiyan Island. US and Saudi fighter jets struck multiple sites inside Iraq after drones launched by Iranian-aligned militias were fired toward Saudi Arabia.

Trump said he was preparing to hit Iran “very hard” before announcing a pause, saying Iran and other regional states had asked him to hold off. By Wednesday, reports of an imminent US-Iran interim agreement to reopen the Strait of Hormuz drove a global equity rally and a sharp fall in crude prices.

Key facts:

  • The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly a quarter of the world’s seaborne oil; disruption there transmits directly into global fuel and food prices.
  • Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi warned Tehran would “respond forcefully to any further American aggression.”
  • The State Department has issued warnings to US citizens across the Middle East.
  • The strikes on Iraq mark the formal extension of the war onto a third state’s territory, with Saudi Arabia now flying combat missions alongside US aircraft.
  • Millions of South Asian and African migrant workers across the Gulf states — the region’s most exploited layer — are directly exposed to drone and missile fallout with no evacuation options and no political representation.

4. Sudanese army drone strike kills at least 37 at Darfur civil court

A Sudanese Armed Forces drone strike on August 2 killed at least 37 people gathered at a civil court in an RSF-occupied village in North Darfur. The atrocity received minimal international coverage, consistent with the near-total marginalization of a war that has produced what the UN describes as the world’s largest humanitarian crisis.

Key facts:

  • Approximately 5 million people in Sudan now face emergency or catastrophic levels of hunger.
  • The RSF controls much of Darfur and has besieged El-Obeid; the city’s fall would open a direct corridor toward Khartoum.
  • Doctors Without Borders suspended operations at Zalingei hospital in central Darfur following an armed attack, in the midst of an active cholera outbreak.
  • The EU adopted targeted sanctions in July against Abu Aqla Keikel, leader of the RSF-aligned Sudan Shield Forces — a measure with no practical effect on the conduct of the war.
  • Both belligerents are sustained by rival external sponsors, with the UAE, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Russia and Western powers all pursuing competing interests in Sudanese gold, agricultural land and Red Sea port access.

5. 25 Democratic-led states sue Trump over “forced-labor” tariffs

Twenty-five Democratic-led states filed suit in the US Court of International Trade challenging the Trump administration’s new Section 301 tariffs of 10–12.5% imposed on 60 trading partners — levies that now affect more than 99% of US imports by value. The states argue the tariffs are a legal pretext for reviving the tariff regime the Supreme Court struck down in February 2026 in Learning Resources v. Trump, which held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not authorize them.

Key facts:

  • New York Attorney General Letitia James: “After losing at the Supreme Court, the administration is once again trying to illegally raise taxes on families and businesses with a new round of tariffs.”
  • White House spokesman Kush Desai defended the measures, arguing that countries’ “failure to impose and effectively enforce a prohibition on the importation of goods produced with forced labor is unreasonable and burdens US commerce.”
  • Plaintiff states include New York, California, Illinois, Michigan and Pennsylvania.
  • The “forced labor” pretext is a significant escalation in the ideological framing of trade war — human-rights language deployed to justify tariffs whose cost falls on working-class consumers on both sides of the border.
  • The Section 301 tariffs cover roughly $949 billion in imports and were imposed July 24; separate 50% tariffs on Canadian agriculture, dairy, furniture and alcohol (about $16 billion in goods) take effect August 19.

6. BMW to cut 8,000 jobs in Germany

BMW confirmed plans to eliminate approximately 8,000 jobs — roughly one in ten of its domestic positions and about 5% of its global workforce — through a “voluntary” redundancy program running from October 2026 through the end of 2027. Around 40,000 of BMW’s roughly 85,000 permanent German employees will be eligible to receive severance offers. The company is targeting €1 billion in annual savings.

The cuts follow a June profit warning driven by collapsing demand in China, competition from BYD and other domestic Chinese manufacturers, US tariffs and what BMW management calls “geopolitical uncertainty.” The company says production jobs will be spared, concentrating cuts in administration and R&D — a distinction that IG Metall has used to justify accepting the program rather than resisting it.

Key facts:

  • The cuts extend the wave of destruction across German auto: Volkswagen, Bosch, ZF, Continental and Ford have all announced major reductions over the past 18 months.
  • Framing the program as “voluntary” allows the works council and IG Metall to process the destruction of 8,000 livelihoods without a single strike vote.
  • Writing on the WSWS, Dietmar Gaisenkersting locates the cuts within the wider crisis of German industry and identifies IG Metall’s role as facilitating restructuring rather than opposing it.

7. South Korea records hottest temperature in its history

Yangsan, in South Gyeongsang province, reached 42.5°C (108.5°F) on August 2 — the highest temperature ever recorded in South Korea since record-keeping began. It was the fifth consecutive day the city exceeded 40°C.

Key facts:

  • More than 20 localities received emergency heat alerts; authorities urged residents to avoid all outdoor activity.
  • The government raised its Central Disaster and Safety Countermeasures Headquarters response to Level 2, with expanded field inspections and financial support for affected residents.
  • Outdoor construction, delivery and agricultural workers bear the overwhelming burden of such events; South Korea has no binding national heat-stoppage standard with enforcement teeth, and heat-related occupational deaths are chronically undercounted.
  • The record is part of a global pattern of extreme heat events this summer, with simultaneous heat emergencies reported across East Asia, southern Europe and North America.

Indonesian ferry fire leaves 5 dead, 41 missing

The ferry Mutiara Sentosa 2 caught fire between 6 and 7 a.m. local time en route from Surabaya, East Java to Makassar, South Sulawesi. There were 271 people aboard — 232 passengers and 39 crew. At least five are confirmed dead and 41 remain missing. Indonesia’s National Search and Rescue Agency (Basarnas) says more than 200 people have been rescued. The cause of the fire is under investigation.

Indonesia’s inter-island ferry network, which carries tens of millions of working-class passengers annually, has an extensive record of fatal fires, capsizings and overloading incidents. Operators routinely exceed manifested capacity, and enforcement of safety standards has been systematically weakened by deregulation and the fragmentation of the sector among small private operators.

Russian soldier kills four in shooting spree near Sevastopol

A Russian serviceman shot and killed four people — one fellow soldier and three civilians aged 71, 64 and 59 — in the village of Khmelnytske near Sevastopol, occupied Crimea. Four more were wounded. According to accounts relayed by Sevastopol’s Moscow-installed governor Mikhail Razvozhayev, the soldier first opened fire on fellow servicemen after allegedly refusing an order, then shot at civilians before fleeing into woodland. He was later detained. The independent outlet Agentstvo tentatively identified the suspect as a 21-year-old from Saratov region.

The incident is one of a growing number of shootings, desertions and violent breakdowns within the Russian armed forces in the fourth year of the war — an index of the accumulating strain on a conscript and contract army held together by coercion and cash bonuses.

Norwegian oil service workers end month-long strike

A strike by Norwegian oil service workers that lasted a full month has ended. The dispute affected operations in Norway’s offshore energy sector, Europe’s largest single source of pipeline gas since the severing of Russian supply.

Norway’s industrial relations framework permits the government to impose compulsory arbitration (“tvungen lønnsnemnd”) whenever a strike is deemed to threaten “life, health or vital societal interests” — a standard applied with near-automatic regularity to the oil and gas sector. The result is that Norwegian energy workers possess a formal right to strike that is negated in practice the moment it becomes economically effective. As Kim Nachtweid notes on the WSWS, this demonstrates that the vaunted “Nordic model” rests on state power to contain workers’ struggles in strategic industries.

Japanese PM makes brief visit to Kumamoto quake zone

Japan’s prime minister made a short visit to the Kumamoto earthquake zone. The government has formally designated the disaster “extremely severe” — a classification that unlocks enhanced central government reconstruction funding — yet actual aid disbursement to date has been limited, and the visit was largely ceremonial.

The pattern is familiar from the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake, where thousands of residents remained in temporary accommodation years after the event while reconstruction budgets were absorbed by infrastructure contracts. Peter Symonds writes on the WSWS that the gap between the “extremely severe” designation and the material support actually delivered exposes the priorities of a government simultaneously executing the largest military build-up in Japan’s postwar history.

WestJet flight attendants strike, then accept tentative deal

More than 4,000 WestJet flight attendants — represented by the Canadian Union of Public Employees — struck at 12:01 a.m. Mountain Time on Sunday, August 2, after CUPE issued a 72-hour strike notice and WestJet responded with a simultaneous 72-hour lockout notice. Some 309 flights were cancelled and thousands of passengers stranded.

The central issue was unpaid ground work: flight attendants are compensated only for “credit hours” in the air, leaving boarding, deplaning and delays entirely unpaid. The prior contract expired December 31, 2025. CUPE and WestJet reached a tentative agreement on August 3 — within roughly 48 hours of the walkout — and both the strike and lockout notices were withdrawn. Terms have not been made public to members’ satisfaction, and the speed of the settlement follows the pattern of the 2025 Air Canada dispute, in which a militant walkout was rapidly channelled into a leadership-brokered deal.

Canada’s “anti-scab” law used to break strikes

The Canada Industrial Relations Board has issued rulings against workers in a strike involving the Bank of Canada, in which legislation banning replacement workers — hailed by the Canadian Labour Congress and Unifor as a historic victory when it passed — has now been invoked twice in ways that undercut strikers rather than protect them. The law’s “maintenance of activities” provisions require unions to keep designated operations running, and the Board has used these to define ever-broader categories of struck work as essential.

Queensland teachers face extended pay and conditions freeze

Teachers in Queensland, Australia, face a two-year freeze on pay and working conditions, imposed despite prior strike action by educators across the state. The freeze arrives amid persistent class-size increases, unfilled vacancies and escalating workload. In real terms, with Australian inflation running well above the frozen nominal rate, the settlement constitutes a substantial pay cut for tens of thousands of education workers — negotiated and enforced through the Queensland Teachers’ Union.

Senate Judiciary advances Todd Blanche for attorney general

The Senate Judiciary Committee voted 12-10 on strict party lines to advance Todd Blanche’s nomination as attorney general to the full Senate, where Republicans hold 53 seats. Senator Susan Collins (R-ME) announced she will vote against confirmation on the floor — described by Senate observers as a critical blow — and Senator Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) has not committed.

The committee vote followed a standoff over a proposed “anti-weaponization fund,” a mechanism to compensate individuals the administration deems victims of politicized prosecution, including Trump himself. Blanche struck a deal with Senators John Cornyn and Thom Tillis to end the fund, clearing the path to a committee vote.

Blanche, formerly Trump’s personal criminal defense attorney, would head a Justice Department that has been systematically reorganized around the president’s political interests. The dispute among Republicans concerned the crudeness of the payout mechanism, not the underlying transformation of the DOJ into an instrument of executive vengeance.

ICE fits Haitian workers with GPS ankle monitors after stripping status

Immigration and Customs Enforcement has begun fitting Haitian immigrant workers with GPS ankle monitors after revoking their legal status — workers who entered the country lawfully, obtained work authorization and have been employed for years in meatpacking, poultry processing, warehousing and elder care across the Midwest and Southeast.

The monitors are administered through ICE’s “Alternatives to Detention” program, operated under contract by BI Incorporated, a subsidiary of the private prison firm GEO Group. Enrollment in the program has expanded dramatically, converting hundreds of thousands of workers into a permanently surveilled, hyper-exploitable labor force that employers can discipline with the threat of a single phone call.

Writing on the WSWS, Jacob Crosse draws an explicit comparison to historical fascist methods of marking and tracking targeted populations, arguing that the electronic shackling of workers based on national origin is not immigration administration but the construction of an apparatus of mass repression whose eventual targets extend far beyond immigrants.

FBI raids Chinese-American media executive

The FBI has raided a Chinese-American media executive as part of what the WSWS characterizes as an expanding campaign of anti-China political persecution and racial profiling. First deaths recorded in largest US cyclosporiasis outbreak

Michigan reported the first two deaths in what is now the largest recorded US outbreak of cyclosporiasis, a parasitic intestinal infection. Both individuals had underlying health conditions aggravated by severe diarrhea, vomiting and dehydration. As of August 3, the Michigan Department of Health and Human Services had recorded 11,234 cases statewide and 193 hospitalizations. The CDC is aware of more than 18,000 confirmed or suspected cases nationally, though state-level reports put the figure above 20,000 across 45 states.

Federal officials have identified lettuce imported from Mexico and served at Taco Bell locations in nine states as a source. Benjamin Mateus notes on the WSWS that the outbreak is a product of a food-safety inspection regime hollowed out by decades of budget cuts and industry self-certification: Cyclospora cayetanensis is transmitted through produce contaminated with human waste, and its appearance at this scale points to failures in irrigation water sanitation and field hygiene that no amount of downstream testing can remedy.

H5N1 kills seabirds in Australia’s first mass mortality event

Australia confirmed its first mass mortality event from H5N1 avian influenza, with roughly 50 greater crested terns found dead on the coast south of Adelaide. The virus was first detected in Australia in June 2026, ending the continent’s status as the last major landmass free of the panzootic H5N1 clade. There is as yet no evidence of the virus on Australian farms, many of which have locked down poultry flocks.

Globally, A(H5) remains widespread in wild birds and continues to cause outbreaks in poultry and US dairy cattle, with sporadic human infections among dairy and poultry workers. The World Health Organization has reported an additional H5N1 death among this year’s variant zoonotic influenza cases.

US measles elimination status under review

US health officials are preparing to review the country’s measles elimination status following 37 new outbreaks recorded this year. Elimination status is lost when continuous transmission of a single chain persists beyond 12 months. Cases continue to rise both domestically and globally against a backdrop of falling vaccination coverage, driven in the US by the dismantling of federal vaccine advisory structures and the promotion of anti-vaccine positions from within the Department of Health and Human Services.

EU and Colorado AI disclosure rules take effect

  • The European Union switched on the first continent-wide rules requiring AI systems to identify themselves to the humans they interact with, effective August 2 — the transparency obligations of the AI Act.
  • Colorado’s Chatbot Safety Act took effect, requiring conversational-AI operators to estimate user ages, disclose the artificial nature of interactions, implement safeguards against sexually explicit content and simulated emotional dependence for minors, maintain suicide and self-harm response protocols, and file annual reports with the state attorney general.
  • Both regimes impose disclosure requirements while leaving untouched the underlying question of ownership: the models, the data centers and the training corpora remain the private property of a handful of firms whose valuations now anchor global equity markets.

Arts and culture

Nolan’s The Odyssey becomes a mass cultural event

Christopher Nolan’s adaptation of Homer’s Odyssey has reached $320.5 million domestically and $407.4 million internationally for a global total of $727.9 million, crossing $320 million in twelve days — faster than The Dark Knight Rises, which took fifteen. It is on pace to pass $1 billion within a week and to become Nolan’s highest-grossing film, all before its August 14 release in China across nearly 800 IMAX screens.

The film was shot entirely on IMAX film cameras, a first for a feature-length narrative production. More significant than the technical achievement is the response: booksellers report surging sales of Homer in translation, and the film has generated sustained public argument about a text nearly three thousand years old.

David Walsh writes on the WSWS that this response should be understood as evidence of a genuine and widely felt hunger for serious, epic storytelling and for the great works of classical culture — a hunger the film industry has spent decades insisting does not exist. The commercial logic of the studios has been organized around the assumption that mass audiences want only franchise product; the reception of The Odyssey indicates something quite different. The question Walsh poses is what social experience underlies this receptivity to a narrative of war’s aftermath, of a decade lost to combat, of homecoming into a house occupied by parasites.

The Revolutionary Communist Party’s communist.red published its own review, headlined “Nolan’s The Odyssey: An Anti-War Epic,” which treats the film’s anti-war content in moralist and humanist terms — “lessons of the past” — while conspicuously declining to draw any connection to the actual wars underway in Ukraine, Gaza and the Persian Gulf.

Saudi state capital takes control of Electronic Arts

The completed buyout of Electronic Arts places one of the world’s largest interactive entertainment producers under the majority ownership of the Saudi Public Investment Fund, the personal instrument of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, with a minority stake held by Jared Kushner’s Affinity Partners. EA’s studios produce games played by hundreds of millions of people, including franchises with substantial narrative content.

The transfer of a major cultural producer into the hands of an absolute monarchy — one that jails and executes critics — establishes editorial control over creative output at the level of ownership. It follows the same fund’s acquisitions across sport, film and music, and it proceeds without meaningful objection from any quarter of the American political establishment, in part because the deal’s beneficiaries include the president’s son-in-law.

Media consolidation and cultural deaths

  • Paramount’s $110 billion merger has drawn a legal challenge from a coalition of state antitrust enforcers. The combination would further concentrate American film and television production and distribution into a handful of hands, with predictable consequences for what gets made.
  • Monika Helfer, the Austrian novelist, died August 2 at 78. Her late trilogy — beginning with Die Bagage — reconstructed the lives of her impoverished Vorarlberg forebears across two world wars, treating rural poverty, illegitimacy and village cruelty with unsentimental precision. She was among the most serious German-language writers of her generation to take working-class family history as her subject.
  • Glenn A. Baker, the Australian music journalist, historian and broadcaster, died July 31. Over five decades he documented Australian popular music with a scholar’s rigor.
  • Melaine Favennec, the Breton singer and songwriter, died at 75. He was central to the postwar revival of Breton-language song, a body of work bound up with resistance to the French state’s long suppression of regional languages.
  • War propaganda in Australian schools: Frank Gaglioti’s WSWS review of the television program Ultimate Classroom, shown in Australian schools, identifies it as military recruitment material dressed as education — produced with Australian Defence Force cooperation and aimed at reversing the deep anti-war sentiment among young people. The mobilization of the education and broadcast systems to normalize military service is a direct cultural expression of war preparation.

Stellantis worker James Brasher killed on the job — Jerry White

James Brasher, 57, with more than 30 years of seniority, fell 25 feet through a second-floor roll-up door into a trash dumpster at the Mopar Parts Distribution Center in Center Line, Michigan on August 1 and died of his injuries. A coworker’s tribute is quoted: “The headline calls him an auto worker. We knew him as James—a husband, a father, a coworker, a friend, and a human being.”

White establishes that Brasher is the fourth Stellantis worker and at least the sixteenth UAW member killed on the job since 2022, and traces the conditions directly to the 2023 UAW-Stellantis contract that Shawn Fain called “brilliant.” That agreement sanctioned the closure of 19 US facilities, including roughly half of all Stellantis parts distribution centers nationally. The Center Line building has already been sold and is being leased back from its new owner while operations wind down ahead of a planned 2027 shift to a $388 million automated “Mega Hub” in Detroit — a facility that will eliminate hundreds of jobs. White’s central argument is that plants slated for closure are systematically starved of maintenance and safety investment in their final years.

He cites the International Workers Alliance investigation into the earlier death of Ronald Adams Sr., which documented management distributing “cheater keys” enabling supervisors and workers to bypass lockout/tagout protections. He draws a parallel to the July 2026 Detroit wildfire smoke crisis — Air Quality Index reaching 698, the worst ever recorded in a major US city — when the UAW likewise failed to force protective measures, producing “not one finding, not one prosecution, not one change.”

Will Lehman, the Mack Trucks worker and socialist candidate in the ongoing UAW presidential race, supplies the political counterpoint: “It was not a real strike. It was a disaster. It was a Hollywood strike.” And: “Every year in this country, more than 5,000 workers die on the job because safety is subordinated to profit… What we need is a strategy and an organization, class struggle instead of class collaboration.” The piece closes with a call for rank-and-file safety committees with independent power to halt production, and an offer of anonymity to workers reporting violations.

Nicaragua moves to ban “Yankee” agents from elections as Trump policies hit economy — Andrea Lobo

Lobo analyzes a Nicaraguan constitutional reform excluding US-funded parties and political figures from participation in elections. The measure is presented by the Ortega-Murillo government as an anti-imperialist defense of sovereignty. Lobo’s argument is that the nationalist posturing operates as cover for the opposite process: the same government is making substantial economic concessions to Washington under pressure from Trump-era trade and remittance policy, while using “anti-Yankee” rhetoric to criminalize domestic opposition and suppress the working class.

The analysis is a case study in the WSWS’s treatment of bourgeois nationalism in the semi-colonial countries: the FSLN, having long since abandoned any social program, retains the anti-imperialist vocabulary of the 1979 revolution as an instrument of authoritarian rule while integrating Nicaraguan capitalism ever more tightly into US-dominated circuits of production and finance. The reform’s practical effect is to eliminate electoral competition entirely while leaving the material relationship to US capital untouched. Lobo situates this within the broader Latin American pattern in which governments across the political spectrum — from Ortega to Sheinbaum to Lula — accommodate Washington’s escalating economic and military pressure while offering rhetorical resistance to their own populations.

The War in Ukraine and the Struggle for Socialism: The Case of Bogdan Syrotjuk — Mehring Verlag

Mehring Verlag announced the availability of a volume documenting the case of Bogdan Syrotjuk, the young Ukrainian Trotskyist and member of the Young Guard of Bolshevik-Leninists arrested by the Zelensky government’s SBU in April 2024 on charges of “justifying Russian aggression” — charges arising entirely from his socialist opposition to both the Putin regime and NATO.

The book collects the documents of the international campaign for Syrotjuk’s freedom conducted by the ICFI, together with the political analysis underlying it: that opposition to the war requires the unified struggle of Ukrainian and Russian workers against both their own ruling classes and NATO imperialism, and that this position is intolerable to all the belligerent governments precisely because it is the only one that threatens them. The volume’s publication makes the case’s documentary record available in German for the first time in collected form.

BMW cuts 8,000 jobs in Germany — Dietmar Gaisenkersting

Gaisenkersting’s account adds detail beyond the corporate announcement: the cuts are the German auto industry’s response to a structural crisis it cannot resolve — simultaneous loss of the Chinese market to domestic Chinese producers, exposure to US tariffs, and the enormous sunk costs of the electric transition. He documents IG Metall’s now-standardized method: negotiate a “voluntary” program with severance packages, declare that production jobs are protected, and thereby prevent any collective response from emerging.

The political conclusion is that the defense of jobs in the German auto industry cannot proceed through IG Metall or the works councils, which function as co-managers under the co-determination system, but requires rank-and-file action committees linked internationally to workers at Stellantis, Volkswagen, Ford and the Chinese manufacturers now being counterposed to them.

Canada’s ruling class exploits “anti-scab” legislation — Carl Bronski

Bronski’s central contribution is the documentation that the Canada Industrial Relations Board has now applied the anti-replacement-worker law against strikers on two separate occasions. He identifies the mechanism: the union bureaucracy’s investment of years of political capital in securing this legislation, and its subsequent inability to oppose the Board’s rulings without repudiating its own strategy. Having presented the law as the substitute for industrial militancy, Unifor and the CLC have no answer when the state turns it around.

1-month-long strike by Norwegian oil service workers ended — Kim Nachtweid

Nachtweid’s specific target is Norway’s compulsory arbitration statute and the role of LO, the Norwegian trade union confederation, in accepting it as a legitimate framework. The article’s conclusion is that the Norwegian “model” — held up internationally as proof that social partnership works — depends on the systematic legal negation of the right to strike wherever it would carry economic weight.

Japanese PM makes brief visit to Kumamoto quake zone — Peter Symonds

Symonds contrasts the resources allocated to Kumamoto reconstruction with the record military budget passed by the same government, and connects official indifference to the residents of the quake zone with the wider abandonment of Japan’s working population — stagnant real wages, an aging population without adequate care provision, and a political establishment reorienting the state around remilitarization.

Australia: Queensland teachers face longer freeze on pay and conditions

The article documents that the Queensland Teachers’ Union called limited strike action, then accepted the freeze — the standard sequence by which industrial action is used to dissipate rather than mobilize opposition. The WSWS calls for the formation of independent educators’ rank-and-file committees across Australian states, linked to teachers internationally.

ICE shackles Haitian workers with GPS monitors — Jacob Crosse

Crosse’s argument extends beyond immigration policy: he presents the GPS monitoring program as a prototype for population control technologies whose logic is not confined to non-citizens, and links it to the Trump administration’s parallel expansion of domestic surveillance authority and deployment of federal forces in US cities.

Mateus places the outbreak within the WSWS’s sustained critique of the dismantling of US public health infrastructure, arguing that the FDA’s produce safety rule has never been meaningfully enforced against importers and that the agency’s inspection capacity has been further reduced by the current administration’s staffing cuts.

Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey — David Walsh

Walsh’s review, beyond the discussion of the film’s reception, engages with Homer’s text itself — the poem’s treatment of the returning soldier, of a social order decayed in the warrior’s absence, and of the human capacity for endurance. He warns against the reduction of the epic to spectacle and assesses where Nolan’s adaptation succeeds and fails in rendering the poem’s more difficult and less heroic content.

Jacobin / DSA

“Socialists Are Out-Organizing the Democratic Establishment” defends DSA members running and organizing as Democratic Party candidates and primary participants as a matter of superior tactics and organizing capacity. The argument is entirely instrumental — the Democratic Party is treated as a neutral terrain on which better organizers win — evading the class character of the party as an instrument of American capitalism and imperialism.

“New York’s Socialist Victories Can’t Be So Easily Dismissed” presents DSA’s New York City ballot-line wins as empirical proof that electoralism works, engaging with turnout and gentrification data while never raising the question of what a socialist administration can actually do within the capitalist state. The framing treats the ballot box as the vehicle of social transformation rather than as an arena in which the working class can only intervene independently.

“Mexican Sovereignty in the Era of the ‘Donroe Doctrine’” criticizes US imperialism rhetorically but locates the resistance in Mexican state diplomacy and “rules-based” interstate alliances. This is the substitution of bourgeois nationalism for international working-class struggle: the Mexican bourgeoisie, whose relationship to its own working class is one of brutal exploitation, is recast as the anti-imperialist agent.

Assessment: Jacobin’s output today is a sustained argument for the subordination of socialist politics to the Democratic Party, now advanced with the confidence of a tendency that believes recent electoral results have vindicated it.

Left Voice

“The Michigan Senate Race Is a Microcosm of National Politics” explicitly rejects channeling movement energy into Democratic primaries and lesser-evilism, calling instead for independent candidates and street organization. “Sanae Takaichi Cannot Resolve Japan’s Economic Crisis” situates Japan as subordinate to US imperialism and criticizes the militarist scapegoating of China. “Ceuta: Against Militarization, Deportations, and ‘Fortress Europe’” treats far-right and social-democratic border policy as fundamentally continuous, demanding open borders and migrant self-organization.

Assessment: Left Voice’s formal positions on the Democratic Party and on borders are closer to Marxism than those of the other tendencies surveyed. The limitation is programmatic: the alternative offered is “independent candidates” and “street organization” — a movementist substitute for the construction of a revolutionary party on the basis of the historical continuity of Trotskyism, which the Morenoite tradition to which the PTS belongs specifically repudiated.

Liberation News / PSL

“Study, Fast, Train, Fight: The Roots of Black August” rejects “Black faces in high places” — Black Democratic officials, police and billionaires — in favor of what it terms revolutionary rather than assimilationist strategy. “Six Months Later, the Lessons of January in Minnesota Cannot Be Forgotten” warns explicitly against 2020-style militancy being “funneled into voting for the Democratic Party,” calling for strikes and mass mobilization.

Assessment: The domestic line in this batch is sharper than usual, and the campist framing on Russia, China and Syria that ordinarily characterizes PSL output did not appear. The programmatic content remains a radical-democratic mass-action perspective rather than a class analysis; the PSL’s opposition to the Democratic Party has repeatedly proven tactical rather than principled, and its international line — support for capitalist states in conflict with Washington — is the negation of the internationalism it invokes domestically.

Socialist Alternative

“Support Left Independent Candidates—Build an Alternative to the Two Corporate Parties” formally rejects both major parties while touting SAlt’s past support for Bernie Sanders and Zohran Mamdani — both of whom ran inside the Democratic Party. The rhetoric of a break is wrapped around continued tailing of Democratic-aligned figures, folded into a national-reformist “new labor party” program that presupposes the trade union bureaucracy as its instrument.

“Zohran Says Netanyahu Not Welcome in NYC: Kick Him Out with Mass Action!” implicitly legitimizes the Mamdani administration as a vehicle for the anti-genocide movement rather than politically breaking from a Democratic mayor who administers the largest municipal police force in the United States.

Assessment: SAlt continues to occupy the position it has held for a decade — verbal independence from the Democrats combined with practical orientation to whichever Democratic figure has movement credibility at the moment.

Socialist Workers Party (UK)

“Restore Britain Seizes an Opening on the Far Right” attacks Labour Prime Minister Andy Burnham for “conceding to the right” on immigration. The piece does not call for a Labour vote, but frames anti-fascism through broad mobilization rather than an independent revolutionary program — the SWP’s decades-old united-front method, which subordinates the fight against fascism to coalitions with the very Labour and trade union forces whose policies create the openings for the far right. “United March in Sheffield for Trans+ Rights” frames trans liberation as requiring “class mobilisation” while leaning into movement-unity rhetoric linking Palestine, refugees and anti-fascism without a distinct program.

Assessment: The SWP’s criticism of Burnham from the left is not accompanied by any perspective for building an alternative to Labour.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

“From Kashmir, to India, to Britain… Workers and Youth, Unite!” frames corruption as inherent to capitalism and calls for building a “revolutionary communist” cadre party, with only a passing, undeveloped mention of the Iran war. “Nolan’s ‘The Odyssey’: An Anti-War Epic” offers anti-war film criticism in moralist and humanist terms while failing to articulate an anti-imperialist position on any war currently being fought.

Assessment: The RCP’s party-building rhetoric is not matched by programmatic content on the central question of the moment. A publication capable of writing an anti-war film review during a widening US-Iran war, without naming that war, has established what its “revolutionary communism” consists of.
