1. US reimposes naval blockade and resumes bombing of Iran as ceasefire collapses

US Central Command reimposed its naval blockade of Iranian ports and coastal waters at 20:00 GMT on July 14 and launched a fourth consecutive night of strikes against Iranian missile, drone, and coastal-defense sites, marking the definitive collapse of the interim “Islamabad memorandum” that had briefly paused the war. Iran’s Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the blockade “has, in a way, dismantled” the deal.

President Trump, who a day earlier had floated a 20 percent transit fee on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, reversed the proposal and adopted open threats: “They shot first, and that was a big mistake… We have been knocking the hell out of them.”

The escalation confirms the analysis advanced repeatedly on the WSWS that the “ceasefire” was a pause for regrouping, not peace, and that US imperialism is driving toward a wider Middle East war to reorder the region.

Key facts: - Iran’s army says at least 7 personnel were killed when 13 missiles struck a guesthouse and accommodation facilities at a military base near Iranshahr. - At least 2 seafarers were killed in related attacks in the Strait of Hormuz. - Iran’s Revolutionary Guard struck “two non-compliant” supertankers and fired missiles and drones at US military infrastructure in Bahrain and Jordan.

Sources Al Jazeera US strikes new targets in Iran as Tehran hits Gulf states, Hormuz · CNN Iran war live updates · NPR US-Iran updates

2. Iran strikes Gulf states, drawing regional condemnation and airspace closures

Iran’s retaliation extended across the Gulf, prompting interceptions over Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan and forcing the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) to direct airlines away from Gulf airspace. Gulf Arab states issued a joint condemnation while renewing calls for a return to diplomacy — an appeal that underscores the fear among the region’s rulers that the war will engulf them.

Key facts: - Kuwait’s air defenses intercepted 4 missiles and 10 drones; Bahrain’s capital Manama sounded air-raid sirens before interceptions. - Jordan’s military intercepted three ballistic missiles that entered its airspace from Iranian territory. - Bahrain’s Defence Force accused Iran of a “systematic aggressive approach” through missile and drone strikes. - EASA directed airlines to avoid airspace over Bahrain, Qatar, Kuwait, the UAE, and parts of the Gulf of Oman.

3. Israel controls nearly 70% of Gaza nine months into “ceasefire”

Israeli forces continue to expand their occupation of Gaza, now controlling nearly 70 percent of the territory — well beyond the original ceasefire demarcation lines — as daily killings continue. Three Palestinians were killed and 15 wounded in Israeli attacks across the enclave as forces enlarged the so-called “Yellow Zone.”

Key facts: - Gaza’s Ministry of Health says “ceasefire” violations since October have killed 1,108 Palestinians and wounded 3,578 others. - Concrete demarcation blocks were moved to within roughly 200 meters of Salah al-Din Street in Shujayea, Gaza City’s main north-south route. - A UN Deputy Special Coordinator urged the Security Council to “consolidate the Gaza ceasefire” and halt escalating West Bank settlement violence.

4. Andy Burnham set to become UK prime minister unopposed

Andy Burnham, the Greater Manchester mayor, is on course to become Britain’s prime minister without facing a membership ballot, after securing the backing of 349 of Labour’s 403 MPs — the only candidate able to clear the 81-MP nomination threshold. MP nominations closed July 15, with trade union and affiliate nominations open July 15–16. If unopposed, a special leadership conference on July 17 could confirm him as leader outright, making him Britain’s seventh prime minister in a decade.

The succession follows Keir Starmer’s June 22 resignation after Labour’s rout in the May local elections. The manner of Burnham’s likely coronation — an internal MP stitch-up producing a head of government with no popular mandate — exemplifies the profound crisis of bourgeois democratic legitimacy the WSWS has tracked across Britain’s revolving-door premierships.

Key facts: - Candidates need at least 3 affiliate nominations, including 2 unions, to appear on any membership ballot. - Starmer resigned June 22 following poor May 2026 local election results and internal party discontent.

Sources Al Jazeera UK Labour leadership nominations begin: Who’s running and how it works · China Daily Burnham poised to lead Labour

5. DOJ defies court order, refuses to release unredacted Epstein files

The Justice Department on July 14 refused to release additional unredacted records from the Jeffrey Epstein investigation, telling federal Judge Emmet Sullivan it had already “adequately complied” with the Epstein Files Transparency Act — in open defiance of the court’s order to either unredact the files or show cause why it cannot. Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche said the department had reviewed “more than 6 million documents.”

The withheld material is explosive: disputed documents include at least eight email exchanges referencing a “torture video” and sexual activity with minors, along with interviews with a woman alleging abuse by Trump as a minor. The stonewalling reinforces the WSWS characterization of the Epstein affair as a window into the criminality and mutual blackmail binding sections of the ruling class and state apparatus.

6. ICE agents kill immigrants in Maine and Texas amid detention buildup

Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents shot and killed a man in Biddeford, Maine, days after killing another in Houston, with a third man dying during an ICE pursuit in Florida — a wave of killings that has drawn national coverage and protests. The victim in Maine has been identified as Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero, a 26-year-old Colombian who state officials say was not the intended target of the operation; the Houston victim, Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, was a Mexican father of three who had lived in the US for decades and was killed on his way to work. Following outcry, ICE announced it was pausing most traffic stops and expanding body-camera use.

The deaths come amid a $45 billion detention expansion and the deployment of more than 2,600 National Guard troops in Washington, D.C. In a statement, the Socialist Equality Party called the killings “state terror” and demanded independent workplace committees to organize mass action, up to and including general strikes, against “ICE terror and dictatorship.”

7. Bangkok music bar fire kills 30 amid negligence probe

The death toll from the July 14 blaze at the Rong Beer Na Ladprao bar in Bangkok’s Chatuchak district rose to 30, with more than 70 injured and 24 still in critical condition. Most victims died of smoke inhalation, many trapped in windowless bathrooms near a rear exit. Police suspect an electrical short circuit in a ceiling air conditioner, and investigators are examining possible negligence, including obstructed emergency exits and signs that some doors were locked shut. The venue had passed a safety inspection in April.

The disaster follows a grimly familiar pattern of mass-casualty fires in profit-driven nightlife and workplace settings where safety regulation is nominal. As the WSWS noted, the tragedy reflects the subordination of human life to profit in Thailand’s $5.5 billion nightlife industry.

Sudan’s drone war drives record civilian deaths

Sudan’s civil war, now past 1,000 days, has entered a phase of intensified drone warfare that is killing civilians at record rates. Between January and May 2026, the UN human rights office (OHCHR) documented over 1,000 civilians killed in drone strikes — roughly 80 percent of all conflict-related civilian deaths recorded this year. UN monitors report the war has “triggered an unprecedented wave of sexual violence used systematically as a weapon of war.”

  • The conflict has displaced more than 11 million people and left 28 million facing acute hunger.
  • Peace-plan efforts continue at the UN Security Council, but the great-power and Gulf-state backers of the rival factions continue arming them.

DRC conflict intensifies in South Kivu as UAE backs Kinshasa

Hostilities between the Democratic Republic of Congo’s armed forces and the Rwanda-backed M23 have intensified in South Kivu’s Fizi and Mwenga territories over the past two weeks, with both sides deploying armed drones and heavy artillery in populated areas. UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk “deplored” the civilian toll of clashes around Mulima village on July 4–5 and urged de-escalation.

Ten years since the failed Turkish coup against Erdoğan

Turkey marked the tenth anniversary of the July 2016 coup attempt against President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan with nationwide commemorations, including a march across Istanbul’s July 15 Martyrs Bridge under the slogan “Our Will, Our Victory.” The 2016 coup — in which more than 8,600 soldiers were involved and roughly 250 people were killed — remains a decisive turning point: in its aftermath Erdoğan purged some 40 percent of Turkey’s generals and dismissed roughly 125,000 public employees, constructing an authoritarian presidential state. On July 13, Turkish authorities launched coordinated operations across all 81 provinces targeting nearly 1,000 suspects over alleged FETO links. As the WSWS analysis argues, the coup arose from imperialist conflicts over Turkey’s geopolitical alignment rather than a simple Erdoğan–Gülen rivalry, and Western powers turned a blind eye despite their professed defense of democracy.

Germany expands surveillance and police-state powers

Germany’s “Security Package 2.0,” passed by the federal cabinet in late April 2026, bundles three draft laws authorizing the Federal Criminal Police Office (BKA) and Federal Police to carry out automated data analysis, biometric internet matching, and real-time facial recognition of “wanted persons” at railway stations and airports. Police in three federal states already use Palantir’s “Gotham” software, with a fourth set to roll it out and potential nationwide expansion. A petition against the proposals, name-checking Palantir, has surpassed 150,000 signatures, and more than a dozen civil-society groups warn the laws threaten anonymity in public space and may conflict with the EU AI Act. The WSWS argues the measures are an authoritarian response to mounting popular opposition to rearmament and austerity — effectively criminalizing dissent.

Australia threatens to defund universities over pro-Palestinian dissent

Australia’s Labor government, through Education Minister Jason Clare, has imposed by ministerial regulation new “Threshold Standards” requiring universities to adopt formal definitions of antisemitism and other forms of racism, demonstrate ongoing preventive action, and establish complaints processes for those who feel “threatened.” Clare has introduced legislation expanding the powers of the regulator TEQSA to fine or sanction non-compliant institutions, with public universities required to comply from January and facing restriction or total cutoff of federal funding for failure to “monitor compliance.” The measures flow from an Antisemitism Education Taskforce interim report. The WSWS likens the crackdown to Trump administration tactics and calls for independent rank-and-file committees of students and workers to defend pro-Palestinian speech.

New heat wave endangers workers across the US

A record-shattering heat wave has spread across the United States, with more than 100 million Americans under heat alerts from Los Angeles to Boston. Salt Lake City hit an all-time record 109°F on July 12, and Billings, Montana reached 111°F, with Miles City, Montana touching a preliminary 115°F. The extreme heat is exposing workers to life-threatening conditions in un-air-conditioned plants and outdoor worksites, yet OSHA still lacks a binding federal heat-safety standard. The WSWS reports more than 48 heat-related worker deaths occur annually — including postal workers Wendy Johnson and Jacob Taylor — and calls for independent rank-and-file safety committees, arguing that employers and unions alike subordinate worker safety to production quotas.

First strike in PECO’s 145-year history hits Philadelphia

Some 1,600 unionized PECO utility workers (IBEW Local 614) walked off the job — the first strike in the company’s 145-year history — after six months of failed bargaining. The walkout, which began during a Fourth of July heat wave, raises the stakes for power reliability across Philadelphia and reflects the mounting militancy of utility and infrastructure workers confronting stagnant wages and rising benefit costs.

Brigham nurses return without a contract after five-day lockout

More than 4,000 Mass General Brigham nurses returned to work on July 13 after a one-day strike (authorized by 99.6 percent) that management converted into a five-day lockout, hiring roughly 1,300 replacement nurses. The nurses won no gains on wages, health insurance, or travel-nurse staffing. The WSWS contrasts the system’s CEO pay of $8.4 million with entry-level nurses’ $86,700 salaries and blames the Massachusetts Nurses Association’s isolated one-day-strike strategy, calling for independent rank-and-file committees.

Video game strikes hit Bethesda and Ubisoft Barcelona

Unionized Bethesda/ZeniMax workers — 440 affected — began striking July 15 over Xbox-linked mass layoffs, while Ubisoft Barcelona workers struck over cuts affecting 28 percent of the studio. The video game sector has shed an estimated 45,000 jobs since 2022 despite record industry revenue, a stark expression of the drive to boost profit margins at labor’s expense even in booming sectors.

Unifor pushes early Ford Canada tentative deal

Unifor announced a tentative three-year Ford Canada contract covering 5,150 workers — with ratification votes July 17–19 — more than two months before the current deal expires. The agreement, reached July 11, covers members at the Oakville Assembly Complex, Windsor Annex and Essex Engine plants, and parts distribution centers, and received the unanimous endorsement of the master bargaining committee; specifics are being withheld until ratification meetings. Ford is set as the pattern for more than 10,000 Stellantis and GM workers. The WSWS calls the maneuver anti-democratic and urges workers to reject the deal and build rank-and-file committees uniting Canadian, US, and Mexican autoworkers against the nationalist policy of the union apparatus.

Science, technology, and public health

  • Record cyclosporiasis outbreak sickens nearly 7,000 — 34 states affected, Michigan hardest hit with 3,309 cases, exceeding the 2019 record with six weeks left in the season.
  • H5N1 bird flu remains widespread with sporadic human cases — 71 US human cases and 2 deaths since 2024; 12 additional cases abroad since August 2025, with 3 deaths.
  • Lancet Countdown warns of surging European heat deaths — 99.6% of monitored European regions saw more heat-attributable deaths in 2015–24 than in 1991–2000.
  • DRC Ebola outbreak triggers new US travel restrictions — US now requires travelers from Congo to spend 21 days elsewhere before entry.

A record cyclosporiasis outbreak has sickened nearly 7,000 people across 34 states, with Michigan the hardest hit at 3,309 cases — already exceeding the 2019 record with six weeks left in the season. As Evan Blake reports on the WSWS, the surge follows Trump–Kennedy administration cuts that gutted federal foodborne-disease surveillance, eliminating some 3,500 FDA and 2,400 CDC jobs. The outbreak, driven by contaminated fresh produce, is a direct consequence of dismantling public-health infrastructure in the interests of agribusiness profit; the WSWS calls for socialized public health and the expropriation of the agribusiness monopolies.

Disease surveillance: - H5N1 avian influenza: The CDC reports 71 human cases and 2 deaths in the US from 2024 to May 2026, with no confirmed person-to-person spread; globally, 12 additional human cases (3 fatal, in Bangladesh and Cambodia) were reported from August 2025 to June 2026 across Bangladesh, Cambodia, and India. The virus remains widespread in wild birds, US poultry, and dairy cattle. - Measles: The US recorded 164 confirmed cases in 2025 across nine states, with continued clusters driven by declining vaccination rates and the erosion of federal public-health capacity. - Ebola (DRC): US health officials now require citizens who have traveled in the Democratic Republic of Congo to spend 21 days in a third location before entering the US amid a growing outbreak — a punitive measure that does nothing to address the underfunded state of Congo’s war-ravaged health system.

Climate and environment: - The 2026 Europe report of the Lancet Countdown on health and climate change finds that nearly all monitored European regions (823, or 99.6 percent) saw increased heat-attributable deaths in 2015–24 compared with 1991–2000, warning of a “narrowing window for decisive health action.” - New analyses in Nature Climate Change document multidimensional intensification of global heat stress since 1950 and show, across more than 5,100 species, that climate change has caused more frequent local extinctions among temperate than tropical species.

The convergence of these findings — accelerating heat mortality, spreading zoonotic disease, and the deliberate defunding of surveillance — underscores the WSWS argument that the response to biological and climatic threats is being sacrificed to austerity and private profit.

Arts and culture

The death of Sam Neill, the New Zealand actor, dominated cultural news. Neill died in Sydney aged 78, in a passing described as “sudden and unexpected,” months after he said in April that he remained cancer-free following treatment for stage-three blood cancer. Neill’s five-decade career resists the reduction of screen acting to blockbuster spectacle. Though best known internationally as Dr. Alan Grant in Jurassic Park, he built a body of work rooted in the independent and art cinema of Australia and New Zealand — from Gillian Armstrong’s My Brilliant Career (1979), a landmark of the Australian film revival engaging questions of women’s independence and colonial society, to Jane Campion’s The Piano (1993). His willingness to embody menace and moral ambiguity, in films from Possession to Dead Calm, reflected a serious craftsman’s temperament increasingly rare in an industry dominated by franchise product. His obituaries have rightly stressed the substance of his work over its box-office scale.

In new releases, Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey — an adaptation of Homer starring Matt Damon, Tom Holland, Anne Hathaway, Robert Pattinson, Zendaya, Charlize Theron, and Lupita Nyong’o — anchors the summer film calendar. A large-scale studio treatment of one of the founding works of Western literature carries the potential to bring the epic’s themes of war, exile, and homecoming to a mass audience, though it also raises the perennial question of whether the spectacle apparatus of contemporary Hollywood can do justice to the poem’s tragic and humanist depths.

Roundup: - The Odyssey (film): Nolan’s ensemble adaptation of Homer arrives as the season’s most anticipated release, testing whether classical literature can survive translation into franchise-scale cinema. - Booker Prize: The 2026 “Booker Dozen” longlist will be announced July 28 — an occasion to assess the state of literary fiction against the pressures of a concentrated publishing industry. - Fraenkel Film Festival: The expanded 2026 edition opens with artist Christian Marclay’s choice, Brian De Palma’s Carrie, and marks the 40th anniversary re-release of Ross McElwee’s Sherman’s March, a foundational work of personal documentary cinema. - Repertory and revival culture — the festival re-releases and artist-curated screenings — point to a continued appetite for cinema history that runs counter to the streaming platforms’ erasure of the film heritage.

These developments should be understood against the broader assault on culture — defunding, censorship of anti-war and pro-Palestinian expression, and the subordination of artistic production to corporate concentration — that the WSWS has documented, and against which genuine artistic achievement, wherever it appears, must be defended.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “A revealing comment from former IMF chief economist” by Nick Beams — read in full on wsws.org

Beams takes as his point of departure a New York Times op-ed by Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, who stepped down in June as the IMF’s chief economist and, freed from institutional constraints, offered an unusually candid diagnosis of the crisis of world capitalism. Beams’ central argument is that Gourinchas’s framing of the world economy as caught between two opposing “forces” — the destruction of war and the supposedly countervailing boost of AI investment — is fundamentally misleading: AI under capitalism does not offset the logic of war and fragmentation but intensifies the same contradictions, being deployed to destroy jobs and raise profit rates. The two “forces” are two expressions of a single crisis of the profit system.

Beams treats Gourinchas’s own words as significant admissions from within bourgeois orthodoxy. Gourinchas warned of “a dangerous feedback loop” as “countries seek to insulate themselves from perceived risks” and “risk fragmenting the global economy further still,” and observed that “the world’s superpowers are searching for strategic advantages, identifying choke points, adopting inward-looking policies and increasing military expenditures.” Most strikingly, Beams highlights Gourinchas’s explicit analogy to the pre-1914 period: “We have seen such dynamics before… What followed was a period of intense deglobalization, coinciding with rising nationalism and militarization — and two world wars.” Beams reads this as an unintentional concession that global capitalism is retracing the slide into inter-imperialist war. Gourinchas, he concludes, “could offer no solution” beyond a “vacuous appeal” to IMF cooperation; the only answer is “the perspective of socialist revolution.”

Chilean state terrorist and death squad leader freed from ICE custody — Bill Van Auken

Armando Fernández Larios — a former Chilean intelligence officer involved in the 1976 Letelier-Moffitt car bombing in Washington, D.C., and decades of Pinochet-era torture and assassinations — was released from ICE custody on “humanitarian parole” after five months, despite having been labeled among the “worst of the worst.” Van Auken details Fernández Larios’s role in the assassination of former Chilean foreign minister Orlando Letelier and his American aide Ronni Moffitt on US soil, and his later cooperation with US authorities. The article argues his quiet release — while immigrant workers with no criminal records are killed and deported — exposes the class and geopolitical selectivity of the US state’s rhetoric, and the enduring protection Washington extends to Cold War-era agents who served American strategic interests in Latin America. It ties the case to the ongoing exposure of US complicity in the crimes of the Pinochet dictatorship, installed with CIA backing in the September 11, 1973 coup.

Pseudo-left press review

  • Jacobin / DSA — Anti-imperialist critique of Trump’s Iran war stops short of independent working-class action; calls for a “left foreign policy” won through domestic coalitions.
  • Left Voice — Emphasizes rank-and-file action (UAW Israel-bond divestment, general strike) while criticizing AOC/DSA electoral illusions from the left.
  • Liberation News / PSL — Campist anti-NATO solidarity with the Turkish CP; condemns SCOTUS TPS ruling. No fresh 24-hour output.
  • Socialist Alternative — Calls to build a fightback against ICE and a “new party” independent of the Democrats.
  • Socialist Worker / SWP (UK) — Rank-and-file trade union militancy and street anti-fascism; direct opposition to Labour’s refugee-camp plan.
  • Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT) — Anti-imperialist reading of Trump’s Iran war and pointed attacks on liberal anti-racism.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s coverage of the Iran war, “Unable to Accept Defeat, Donald Trump Presses On in Iran,” frames the escalation as Trump’s stubborn refusal to accept a strategic defeat, emphasizing Iran’s leverage over the Strait of Hormuz. The anti-imperialist framing is directed at Trump’s personal recklessness and US militarism, but it does not call for the independent mobilization of the working class against the war — leaving the “solution” implicitly with a more rational bourgeois foreign policy. This is made explicit in “The Left Needs Its Own Foreign Policy,” which argues the US left lacks a coherent program and implies that foreign policy is a domestic prize to be won through electoral coalitions — a national-reformist conception that substitutes influence over the American state for international class struggle. A third piece, “Why the French Revolution Matters,” offers competent popular-Marxist history of 1789 as a bourgeois revolution, disconnected from present tasks.

Assessment: Jacobin channels genuine anti-war sentiment into pressure for a reformed imperialist policy administered by the Democratic Party, never breaking from the framework of the American nation-state.

Left Voice

Left Voice reports the UAW’s divestment of $400,000 in Israel bonds as a rank-and-file victory forced onto the convention agenda by UAWD pressure, correctly crediting pressure from below rather than the leadership. “Promises, Promises: AOC, Israel, and the DSA” criticizes AOC and the DSA from the left for illusions in the Democratic Party, consistent with the group’s “no confidence in the Democrats” line, while “General Strike to Shut Down the Shutdown” calls for independent mass action rather than Democratic maneuvering.

Assessment: Left Voice remains the pseudo-left tendency closest to a class-independent posture, but its “from below” activism is not anchored in a struggle to build a revolutionary party, leaving its militant calls without a strategic road forward.

Liberation News / PSL

No output within the strict 24-hour window; the most recent indexed articles are from July 10. “SCOTUS greenlights Trump Administration’s assault on Temporary Protected Status” condemns the ruling ending TPS for some 330,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians within PSL’s anti-racist framework. The “Solidarity Statement with the Communist Party of Turkey (TKP) on the anti-NATO protests” exemplifies PSL’s campism — solidarity organized around opposition to NATO rather than an independent working-class internationalism, aligning the group with state and party actors positioned against Washington.

Assessment: PSL’s campist orientation subordinates the working class to a geopolitical bloc politics, obscuring the class character of the regimes and forces it defends.

Socialist Alternative

“ICE Murders Again—Build The Fightback From Houston To Maine!” calls for an organized mass fightback against the ICE killings, converging with the SEP’s factual account but framing the response around movement-building rather than a political struggle against the capitalist state. “The Democrats Won’t Save Us—The Case For A New Party” explicitly rejects reliance on the Democrats and argues for a new workers’ party, while “Middle East War: Capitalism Offers No Way Out” blames US-Israeli aggression for 10,000+ dead and calls for international working-class opposition.

Assessment: Socialist Alternative’s break from the Democrats is welcome, but its “new party” remains a broad-left electoral vehicle rather than a Marxist revolutionary party, and its perspective stops at protest.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

“Educators at ‘breaking point’ must lead the fight for pay and funding” frames education workers’ militancy as needing to break from union bureaucracy and Labour, while “Oppose Labour’s plans to turn army barracks into camps for refugees” directly attacks the Labour government — a sharper posture than the SWP’s usual accommodation to Labourism. “Anti-racist majority fights far right” reports street mobilizations outnumbering the far right in Walsall, Lincoln, and Portsmouth.

Assessment: The SWP’s direct opposition to Labour on refugees is notable, but its anti-fascism remains oriented to episodic street confrontation rather than the independent political organization of the working class.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

“Trump’s mad dash back to war” analyzes the renewed Iran war and the reclosure of Hormuz as symptoms of declining US imperial power, an orthodox anti-imperialist reading with no appeal to parliamentary solutions. “Five reasons why chanting ‘refugees are welcome here’ doesn’t work” sharply attacks the liberal slogan of Britain’s anti-racist movement as “devoid of class content,” and “Nigel Farage resigns in political stunt” dismisses Farage’s maneuver as populist theater.

Assessment: The IMT offers among the sharpest anti-liberal critiques of the day, but its recent “communist” rebranding remains disconnected from the international Trotskyist program and the fight against the union bureaucracy the WSWS insists upon.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. First strike in PECO’s 145-year history hits Philadelphia amid heat wave

Some 1,600 IBEW Local 614 utility workers walked out for the first time in the company’s history after six months of failed bargaining, with the strike beginning during a dangerous heat wave that raises the stakes for grid reliability. The action links directly to the WSWS’s heat-wave and rank-and-file coverage and warrants a report on the utility workers’ struggle and the role of the IBEW apparatus.

Recommended reading WHYY PECO workers strike starts amid Fourth of July heat wave

2. Video game workers strike at Bethesda and Ubisoft over mass layoffs

Unionized ZeniMax/Bethesda workers began striking July 15 over Xbox-linked layoffs, while Ubisoft Barcelona workers struck over cuts affecting 28 percent of the studio, part of an estimated 45,000 job losses across the sector since 2022 despite record revenue. This is a sharp example of layoffs and AI-driven “efficiency” in a profitable industry, ripe for WSWS analysis of tech-sector class struggle.

Recommended reading TechPowerUp Unionized Bethesda workers plan strike over Xbox mass layoffs

3. Andy Burnham’s uncontested path to Downing Street

The likely installation of Andy Burnham as prime minister by an internal MP process, without a membership ballot or general election, is a major expression of the disintegration of British bourgeois democracy — Britain’s seventh PM in a decade. The WSWS’s close tracking of Labour’s crisis makes a dedicated analysis of Burnham’s record and the anti-democratic character of the succession essential.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera UK Labour leadership nominations begin

4. DRC Ebola outbreak and punitive US travel restrictions

US health officials now require travelers from the Democratic Republic of Congo to spend 21 days in a third country before entering the US amid a growing Ebola outbreak. The measure exposes the collision between an underfunded, war-ravaged Congolese health system and the nationalist, quarantine-by-exclusion logic of US public-health policy — a subject fitting the WSWS’s critique of the abandonment of global disease control.

Recommended reading CBS News US citizens traveling in Congo must spend 21 days elsewhere before entering US

5. Sudan’s drone war produces mass casualties largely absent from Western coverage

OHCHR data show drone strikes now account for roughly 80 percent of conflict-related civilian deaths in Sudan this year — over 1,000 killed January–May alone — amid a war that has displaced 11 million and left 28 million facing acute hunger. The comparative silence of the Western press against the Iran and Ukraine wars is itself a story about imperialist priorities that the WSWS is positioned to expose.

Recommended reading Security Council Report Sudan, July 2026 Monthly Forecast

6. DOJ’s open defiance of a federal court over the Epstein files

The Justice Department’s refusal to comply with Judge Emmet Sullivan’s order to unredact Epstein records — including material referencing a “torture video” and abuse of minors — is a constitutional confrontation and a cover-up implicating the highest levels of the state. It deserves a dedicated WSWS analysis of the ruling-class stakes in suppressing the files.
