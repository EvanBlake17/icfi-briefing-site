1. US-Iran war escalates as Trump threatens new strikes and Hormuz blockade looms

The war launched by the United States and Israel against Iran on February 28 — which killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei in its opening hours — entered a sharper phase over the past 24 hours. President Trump declared on June 10 that Iran is taking “too long to negotiate a deal” and that “now they will have to pay the price,” after Tehran suspended peace talks and, together with the Axis of Resistance, threatened to “completely block” both the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb. The US struck Iranian coastal radar stations on June 6 in response to Iranian attack drones sent toward Hormuz — the latest breach of the fragile April 7–8 ceasefire.

The threat to the world’s most important oil chokepoint has become the dominant factor in global markets and was the principal driver of the OECD’s downward revision of world growth (see World economy). Iran has demanded a full end to Israeli operations in Lebanon and Gaza as a precondition for resuming negotiations.

Key facts: - The war began February 28 with US-Israeli air strikes; the US ran a naval blockade of Iranian ports from April 13 to May 29 - Trump’s June 9 threat to resume “kinetic strikes” — after Iran allegedly targeted a US helicopter — sent equities sharply lower intraday - Iran has paused all peace talks and conditioned their resumption on the cessation of Israeli operations in Lebanon and Gaza - Roughly one-fifth of global oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz; a sustained closure would constitute a systemic shock

2. Magnitude 7.8 earthquake kills at least 45 in southern Philippines

A 7.8 magnitude earthquake struck at 7:40 a.m. local time on June 8 off Maasim, Sarangani province, in southern Mindanao — the strongest quake to hit the Philippines since the 1990 Luzon earthquake. At least 45 people were killed and 490 injured, with 17 still missing. A landslide in Glan alone killed 14. Tsunami warnings were issued across the southern Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia, Palau, Japan, Taiwan, and Papua New Guinea, with the highest recorded wave reaching 1.4 meters.

The quake struck near the start of the school year. President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. activated emergency agencies and ordered school closures affecting 3.2 million students. The disaster reopens the question — for a country of 115 million on the Pacific Ring of Fire — of building-code enforcement and early-warning capacity.

Key facts: - Fatalities concentrated in Sarangani (18), General Santos (12), Davao Occidental (4), and South Cotabato (3) - Approximately 10,000 families were evacuated in Sarangani and Sultan Kudarat - 490 injured, 17 missing as of the latest count

3. US House passes $70 billion ICE and Border Patrol funding bill, 214–212

The US House of Representatives voted 214–212 on June 9 to pass a $70 billion immigration-enforcement package, ending a months-long standoff and sending the bill to Trump’s desk. The reconciliation measure — which bypassed the 60-vote Senate filibuster — allocates $38 billion to ICE, $26 billion to Border Patrol, and $5 billion in contingency funds, fully financing the deportation apparatus through the end of Trump’s term.

The standoff began when Democrats refused DHS funding following mass enforcement actions in Minneapolis and other cities, triggering the longest agency shutdown in US history. Their resistance collapsed: Democrats unanimously opposed the final bill but did not prevent its passage, while two Republicans crossed the aisle against it.

Key facts: - $38 billion for ICE, $26 billion for CBP, $5 billion contingency — funding locked in through 2029 - The vote ended the longest single-agency shutdown in US history - The package entrenches private prison contractors and surveillance-technology vendors as permanent beneficiaries of the enforcement build-out

Sources Washington Post ICE funding bill up for House vote · NPR House passes immigration bill with billions for ICE · CNN House passes ICE, Border Patrol funding

4. Israeli airstrikes hit Beirut suburbs; over 3,500 killed in Lebanon since March

Israeli airstrikes struck Beirut’s southern suburbs on June 7, killing at least two people and wounding 11 — the first such attack since the most recent US-brokered ceasefire renewal. Netanyahu’s office cited Hezbollah drone strikes on Israeli military positions as justification. Thousands of families fled the southern suburbs after Israel announced renewed strikes.

Hezbollah rejected the ceasefire framework agreed between Israeli and Lebanese authorities in Washington on June 4, demanding instead a full ceasefire combined with total Israeli withdrawal from Lebanon. More than 3,500 people have been killed by Israeli strikes in Lebanon between March 2 and June 4.

Key facts: - At least 2 killed, 11 injured in the June 7 strike on the southern suburbs - 3,500+ killed in Lebanon, March 2 – June 4 - Hezbollah’s withdrawal demand is now linked to Iran’s precondition for resuming nuclear talks

5. DRC-Uganda Ebola outbreak passes 550 cases with no vaccine for the strain

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Uganda has surpassed 550 confirmed cases. As of June 8, the DRC reported 550 confirmed cases and 101 deaths; Uganda reported 19 confirmed cases and three deaths. Africa CDC has warned the outbreak is “spreading at an unprecedented pace.”

The strain is Bundibugyo Ebola virus, for which there is no approved vaccine or specific treatment — only experimental candidates. The outbreak is compounding a pre-existing humanitarian catastrophe in eastern DRC, where conflict, mass displacement, and food insecurity are acute, and where MSF faces access constraints from ongoing armed conflict.

Key facts: - DRC: 550 confirmed cases, 101 deaths; Uganda: 19 cases, 3 deaths - Bundibugyo strain — no licensed vaccine or therapeutic exists - The absence of countermeasures reflects the pharmaceutical industry’s lack of profit motive for diseases of the poor

6. Peru presidential runoff too close to call

With 95% of votes counted, Peru’s presidential runoff remains tied: leftist Roberto Sánchez (Together for Peru) leads conservative Keiko Fujimori (Popular Force) by 50.10% to 49.90% — a margin of a few thousand votes. International ballots, expected to favor Fujimori, remain outstanding. The result mirrors the 2021 Castillo-Fujimori runoff, which produced an identical 50.1%–49.9% split.

Sánchez, a former psychologist and trade minister under the imprisoned Pedro Castillo, ran on anti-poverty measures, police reform, and a new constitution through “citizen participation.” Fujimori, running for a fourth time, pledged to “defeat terrorism” and impose a 60-day state of emergency. Sánchez said he is “confident and optimistic, but we’ll wait for 100 percent of the vote.”

Key facts: - Sánchez 50.10% vs. Fujimori 49.90% with 95% counted - Outstanding international ballots could decide the race - A Castillo-style outcome would raise the prospect of renewed institutional sabotage of a left government

Sources Al Jazeera Race tied in Peru’s presidential vote · AS/COA Poll tracker: Peru’s 2026 election

7. Pashinyan’s pro-Western party wins Armenian parliamentary majority

Armenian Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan’s Civil Contract party won 49.8% of the vote (727,160 votes) in the June 7 parliamentary elections, securing a parliamentary majority on 58.97% turnout. The pro-Russian Strong Armenia bloc, led by Samvel Karapetyan, came second with 23.29%, and ex-President Robert Kocharyan’s Armenia Alliance won 9.94%.

Crucially, the result fell short of the two-thirds supermajority needed to call a constitutional referendum — a condition Azerbaijan and Turkey attached to the peace deal and the resumption of border trade. Pashinyan declared a “historic victory” and is expected to accelerate Armenia’s pivot toward the EU and away from Russia’s CSTO. The vote is widely read as a referendum on whether Armenia will complete its break from Moscow after the 2020 and 2023 Nagorno-Karabakh defeats.

Key facts: - Civil Contract 49.8%; Strong Armenia 23.29%; Armenia Alliance 9.94% - No two-thirds majority — the Azerbaijan-Turkey peace roadmap remains blocked - A Western pivot brings IMF conditionality and NATO integration without security guarantees

Sources Al Jazeera Pashinyan’s party wins Armenia election · CNN Armenia PM claims win, poised to pivot from Russia · Armenpress Civil Contract secures majority

South Korea ballot shortage sparks conservative protests

Ballot papers ran out at more than a dozen polling stations during South Korea’s June 3 local elections for mayors and governors, amid higher-than-expected turnout. Hundreds of conservative protesters rallied in Seoul on June 8, accusing the National Election Commission of manipulation and blocking ballot boxes from leaving at least one station. The NEC apologized, extended voting hours in affected areas, and opened an investigation.

President Lee Jae-myung called for “fundamental measures,” saying the controversy raised questions about his own “sensitivity to sovereignty,” and nominated Han Seong-sook as prime minister — who, if confirmed, would be the first woman to hold the post in 20 years. The episode echoes the conspiracist “election fraud” agitation cultivated by the South Korean right since the failed December 2024 martial-law crisis.

Pope Leo XIV celebrates Mass at completed Sagrada Família

Pope Leo XIV celebrated Mass on June 10 at Barcelona’s La Sagrada Família, now the tallest church in the world following the completion of its highest spire. Antoni Gaudí’s basilica was under construction for more than 140 years. The papal visit — Leo XIV is the first non-European pope to visit the site since its completion — drew enormous crowds and extensive international media attention, foregrounding the Vatican’s effort to project continuity and global reach under the new pontificate.

DSA’s Nithya Raman advances to Los Angeles mayoral runoff

Los Angeles City Councilmember Nithya Raman, a DSA member, advanced to a November 3 runoff against incumbent Mayor Karen Bass after overtaking reality-TV figure Spencer Pratt in the slow count of the June 2 primary. CSX to cut human rail track inspections under federal waiver

Class I railroad CSX will reduce the frequency of human track inspections from July 1 under a five-year Federal Railroad Administration waiver, suspending one of two required weekly inspections on high-traffic main lines and replacing inspectors with Automated Track Inspection vehicles. The Brotherhood of Maintenance of Way Employes warns the move is primarily a cost-cutting measure. Derailments already occur roughly three times a day in the US. FRA administrator David Fink previously headed Pan-Am Railways before CSX acquired it in 2022 — a direct conflict of interest.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural event of the day is the convergence of the Metropolitan Opera’s production of El Último Sueño de Frida y Diego (The Last Dream of Frida and Diego) and MoMA’s companion exhibition Frida and Diego: The Last Dream. The opera, with music by composer Gabriela Lena Frank — who has just won the 2026 Pulitzer Prize for her symphony Picaflor: A Future Myth — and a libretto by Nilo Cruz, draws strong notices for a score integrating Andean folk traditions, pre-Columbian percussion, and European classical music. Soprano Isabel Leonard (Frida), baritone Carlos Álvarez (Diego), and soprano Gabriella Reyes (La Catrina) are praised.

The decisive point, as Fred Mazelis and Clare Hurley argue on the WSWS, is what the production omits. Both the opera and the exhibition almost entirely suppress the political lives of Rivera and Kahlo. There is no reckoning with Rivera’s role in securing Trotsky’s Mexican asylum in 1937, nor with the 1938 “Manifesto for an Independent Revolutionary Art,” co-authored by Rivera, Trotsky, and André Breton — one of the central artistic-political documents of the 20th century. The libretto reduces this relationship to romantic biography and physical suffering. The reviewers attribute this to identity-politics frameworks that have turned Kahlo into a marketable brand and recast Rivera as a “bad boy,” erasing both artists’ revolutionary commitments and their tragic later reconciliation with Stalinism. The MoMA show is described as partly a “co-branded marketing opportunity,” though it offers rare proximity to key works including Self-Portrait on the Border Between Mexico and the United States (1932).

The death of historian Gordon S. Wood at 92 — struck by a car in a Rhode Island parking lot, weeks before the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence — is also a major cultural loss. Wood’s The Radicalism of the American Revolution (Pulitzer, 1993) and The Creation of the American Republic (Bancroft Prize) defended the Revolution as a genuinely transformative break with the monarchical Ancien régime. He collaborated with WSWS-published historians in opposing the New York Times’ 1619 Project, demanding corrections to its racialist falsification of 1776.

Educators strike in Australian capital as public education crisis deepens — Kaye Tucker

More than 4,000 ACT public educators struck for 24 hours on June 10 against the ACT Labor government’s sub-3% nominal wage offer, set against 4.2% inflation; teachers earn roughly $4,000 less in real terms than in 2022. The Australian Education Union is criticized for blocking industrial action for 15 years and now bargaining without concrete wage demands. Tucker links the dispute to federal Labor’s $63.8 billion in social spending cuts and record $60 billion military budget, and calls for rank-and-file committees affiliated with the IWA-RFC.

Record One Nation polling underscores crisis of Australia’s two-party system — Oscar Grenfell

A new poll places One Nation’s primary vote (31%, up from 6.4% at the May 2025 election) ahead of a Labor Party fallen to a historic low. Roughly 70% of respondents in one poll said politics needed “a big shake-up.” Grenfell identifies mining billionaire Gina Rinehart and other ruling-class figures backing One Nation to pressure both parties rightward, arguing working-class discontent requires internationalist socialist expression, not right-wing nativism. The shared austerity record — the same $63.8 billion in cuts alongside the $60 billion military budget — underlies the collapse of both major parties.

May 8: How the Soviet Union’s victory over the Nazis is being erased from memory — Katja Rippert

Rippert documents the German government’s systematic erasure of the USSR’s decisive role in defeating Nazism, concurrent with German rearmament against Russia. Chancellor Merz’s May 8 statement omitted Soviet forces; officials refused to answer journalists asking who liberated Europe. Campaigns are underway to “recontextualize” Soviet memorials at Treptower Park and Schönholzer Heide (commemorating some 13 million Soviet dead). Rippert ties this revisionism to the rehabilitation of nationalist narratives, including the elevation of Nazi collaborator Stepan Bandera, and stresses that the Red Army defended the gains of the October Revolution, not any national cause.

IG Metall accepts closure of Mahle plant in Neustadt an der Donau — K. Nesan

IG Metall has agreed to the 2027 closure of the Mahle auto-components plant, eliminating 400 jobs, after workers voted 98.4% for indefinite strike action on May 21 and halted production for three days. The deal caps severance at €250,000 for 350 core workers but excludes roughly 50 agency workers entirely, while providing €3.25 million in union-membership bonuses. Nesan situates this in a pattern of IG Metall collaboration amid auto-sector job destruction — nearly 121,000 members have left the union in two years.

Will Lehman issues open letter to UAW members on eve of Constitutional Convention — Our reporters

Mack Trucks worker Will Lehman distributed an open letter to 300,000 UAW members ahead of the June 15 Detroit convention, calling on delegates to nominate him from the floor to “organize a rebellion against the dictatorship of the UAW apparatus.” Key figures for the board: the UAW holds $1.1 billion in assets and pays nearly 470 employees over $100,000; President Fain earns $270,000. In 2024 Federal Judge David Lawson ruled the Department of Labor acted “arbitrarily and capriciously” in dismissing Lehman’s 2022–23 election-irregularity complaints. Lehman proposes abolishing the bureaucracy and building international autoworker solidarity.

“Workers at Flint Assembly and Nexteer should stop production” — Sheila Brehm and Jerry White

At GM’s Flint Assembly, management receives roughly seven daily truckloads of axles from the struck American Axle plant in Three Rivers, enabling uninterrupted production — “six days a week building 380-plus trucks a shift” — with no UAW solidarity action. Nexteer workers in Saginaw, having rejected multiple union-backed contracts, want to strike alongside American Axle. Historical context for the board: the UAW permitted strikebreaking during the 2023 Clarios strike and the 2008 American Axle walkout, which cut wages from $29 to $14.50/hour. The article calls for rank-and-file committees to coordinate cross-plant action.

The “Big Shrink”: Over half of 50 largest US school districts facing deep cuts — Nancy Hanover

More than half of the 50 largest US school districts are cutting budgets or running deficits. Los Angeles has an $877 million deficit and 3,200 layoff notices; Boston, Chicago, and Philadelphia are similarly hit. Hanover argues this is a deliberate, bipartisan privatization drive accelerated by the expiry of pandemic relief — Democratic-run districts implementing Trump’s education agenda — and criticizes the AFT and NEA for channeling anger into the Democratic Party. She calls for nationally coordinated rank-and-file educator committees and links the defense of public education to socialist transformation.

A tribute to Gordon S. Wood (1933–2026) — Tom Mackaman and David North

(Wood’s death and his work are covered in Arts and culture above.) Unique to the WSWS tribute: detailed treatment of Wood’s working-class origins in Worcester, his study under Bernard Bailyn, and his career at Brown (1969–2008); quotations from colleagues Carol Berkin, James Oakes, Mary Beth Norton, and Timothy Breen; and the explicit account of Wood joining WSWS-published historians to demand corrections to the 1619 Project. North and Mackaman argue Wood was among the last representatives of an objective, idea-centered historical tradition whose work will outlast the “postmodernist evasions” he combatted.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s lead pieces over the past 24 hours include “His Rivals Wrote Him Off, but Jean-Luc Mélenchon Is Back,” which offers sympathetic coverage of the France Insoumise leader’s resurgence among working-class voters, and “Everyone Loves the Knicks. Everyone Hates James Dolan,” which advocates “public ownership” of the franchise as an alternative to billionaire control. A third, “Donald Trump Is Accidentally Speeding the Green Transition,” argues Trump’s foreign policy is inadvertently accelerating renewables.

The political line is consistent left-Democratic reformism. The Mélenchon piece promotes a bourgeois “left populist” as the vehicle for working-class aspirations, obscuring France Insoumise’s record of containing and demobilizing struggle. The Dolan article reduces “socialism” to a municipal ownership gimmick within capitalism. The climate piece is the clearest tell: presenting the imperialist war drive and tariff chaos as a silver-lining “green transition” launders Trump-era disorder into a story of progress, evading the catastrophe being inflicted on the working class. The DSA’s own electoral vehicle is on display in Los Angeles, where DSA member Nithya Raman — censured by DSA-LA over Gaza and a repeat backer of LAPD budget increases — has advanced to the mayoral runoff (see United States). Overall orientation: managing working-class discontent back into the Democratic Party and its European analogues.

Left Voice

Left Voice (PTS/Trotskyist Fraction) continues its extensive Iran coverage with pieces such as “The War Against Iran Shows the Failure of Western ‘Liberation’” and “Defend Iran, but Don’t Support the Theocracy.” These correctly identify the war as imperialist and oppose regime change. But the recurring “defend Iran, but don’t support the theocracy” formula functions as a moral disclaimer in place of a worked-out program for the independent mobilization of the Iranian and international working class against both imperialism and the bourgeois-clerical regime. The companion piece “How the War in Iran Is Shaking the World Economy and Global Order” provides useful economic description but, characteristically, the Morenoite tradition substitutes movementist appeals and propaganda blocs for the building of a section of the Fourth International. Overall orientation: anti-imperialist in rhetoric, but evading the question of revolutionary leadership and the ICFI’s analysis of the war.

PSL / Liberation News

Liberation News maintains heavy immigration coverage in the wake of the $70 billion ICE funding bill, framing the fight against ICE around protest mobilizations and “know your rights” defensism. The PSL’s line couples militant-sounding anti-ICE agitation with orientation toward broad, Democratic-aligned coalition politics, and abroad pairs it with uncritical “anti-imperialist” cheerleading for the Iranian state and the Axis of Resistance. The class question — that the deportation machine targets workers regardless of status and is bipartisan in origin — is subordinated to a campist framework that treats bourgeois nationalist regimes as progressive allies. Overall orientation: street-protest populism that leaves the working class politically dependent on sections of the existing order.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP’s recent output includes “Alex Callinicos: Trump hesitates as risk of war heightens,” “Far right thugs seize on Belfast attack to whip up racist violence,” and “Mexican teachers deliver a lesson in class struggle.” Callinicos’s framing of the imperialist assault on Iran as Trumpian “hesitation” personalizes and minimizes a systemic war drive, the better to avoid a settling of accounts with the SWP’s own record of adapting to imperialist “humanitarian” narratives. The Belfast piece tails a spontaneous anti-racist mobilization without advancing an independent socialist program. Overall orientation: activist militancy decoupled from any struggle for political power, consistent with the IST’s long abandonment of the fight for a revolutionary international.

Socialist Appeal / RCP-IMT

The RCP (Britain) leads with “The Epstein scandal and the crisis of the British state” and “Who governs Britain?,” arguing the scandal has “obliterated public trust” in core institutions and that the state is a “dictatorship of capital.” A third, “Britain’s ‘lost generation’: One million young people without work,” documents youth unemployment. The Marxist phraseology is more developed than the other tendencies’, but it serves a self-promotional catastrophism: every crisis is presented as automatically accruing to the RCP’s recruitment, substituting the proclamation of a “Revolutionary Communist Party” for the patient theoretical and political struggle that built the Fourth International. The IMT’s break from the historical continuity of Trotskyism and the ICFI’s struggle against Pabloism goes unmentioned. Overall orientation: a recruitment-driven radicalism that conflates institutional decay with revolutionary preparation.

The Bundibugyo strain has no approved vaccine or treatment because the pharmaceutical industry saw no profit in developing one. The outbreak, compounded by resource-driven conflict and displacement, connects colonial underdevelopment, the failures of for-profit medicine, and the abandonment of Africa by international health bodies.

4. Peru on the knife’s edge — the lessons of the Castillo betrayal

The Sánchez-Fujimori race exactly mirrors the 2021 contest that produced Castillo’s election, congressional coup, and imprisonment, and the collapse of the left’s governing coalition. A Sánchez win demands scrutiny of whether he will face the same sabotage; a Fujimori win would consolidate authoritarian rule in the country where her father governed by decree and oversaw mass sterilizations of indigenous women.

5. Philippines earthquake exposes disaster-preparedness failures under Marcos Jr.

The strongest quake in 35 years hit on the first day of the school year, forcing the closure of 3.2 million students’ schools. The destruction across General Santos and Sarangani raises urgent questions about building-code enforcement and early-warning capacity in a nation of 115 million on the Ring of Fire — and about the Marcos administration’s response.

Pashinyan’s win is celebrated in Washington and Brussels as a blow to Moscow, but for Armenian workers the westward pivot brings IMF conditionality and NATO integration without security guarantees, while the two-thirds shortfall leaves the Azerbaijan-Turkey roadmap blocked and Armenia in a security vacuum.
