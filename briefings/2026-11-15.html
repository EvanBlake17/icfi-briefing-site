1. Trump, Xi and Putin to meet in Shenzhen with no nuclear arms treaty in force

The White House announced that Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin will hold their first three-way meeting on the sidelines of next week’s APEC summit in Shenzhen. The three powers hold the world’s largest nuclear arsenals. No US-Russian arms control treaty has been in force since New START lapsed in February. Trump rejected Moscow’s offer of a one-year extension and now presents the meeting as a chance for a deal on nuclear stockpiles. His own officials have played down expectations.

The agenda shows Washington’s priorities. A senior US official said Trump will discuss the US-Israeli war on Iran, now in its tenth month, but will avoid Ukraine. Zelenskyy has said publicly that he fears a settlement could be reached without Kyiv. Commentators have compared the meeting to Yalta. The Kremlin rejected the comparison, while calling the meeting “extremely significant.” Whatever the outcome, three nuclear powers are meeting to discuss spheres of interest while a war rages in the Gulf and talks on Ukraine continue in parallel.

Key facts: - Venue: Sidelines of the APEC leaders’ summit, Shenzhen, next week. - Arms control: New START expired in February. Trump turned down Russia’s offer to extend it for one year. - US expectations: “We do not expect to get to any kind of agreement,” a senior US official told Reuters. - Agenda: The Iran war is on the table. The US says Ukraine is not. - Kremlin: Spokesman Dmitry Peskov rejected comparisons with Yalta but called the meeting “extremely significant.”

2. Washington floats Donbas-for-NATO trade as Europe forms its own negotiating team

Zelenskyy disclosed that US negotiators had put a hypothetical to Kyiv: “What if Ukraine joins NATO? Would Ukraine withdraw from Donbas?” Russia occupies most of the region. Zelenskyy said he would not cede territory. Any concession would go to a referendum, and only as part of a full package of security guarantees and reconstruction funding. He also ruled out neutrality: “If Putin expects Ukraine’s neutrality to be enshrined, that’s not going to happen.” The formula would trade land for Ukraine’s formal entry into NATO, the alliance whose eastward expansion led to the war. It would leave NATO’s eastern edge on a new front line facing a nuclear-armed Russia.

US envoy Jared Kushner said he and Steve Witkoff aim to move talks “to the next stage,” including direct engagement between Europe and Moscow. He said “both sides” know compromises will be needed. Al Jazeera’s Mike Hanna reported that Kushner said NATO would provide security guarantees for Ukraine. Talks in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday ended without a breakthrough but produced a prisoner exchange. On Friday, EU leaders named a European negotiating team of Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Poland. It will talk to US and Ukrainian officials, not to Russia. The same five states have said they are ready to send troops to a “coalition of the willing” if a ceasefire is reached. Meanwhile the fighting has intensified.

Key facts: - Drone war: Russian officials reported about 1,200 Ukrainian drones fired at Russia on Friday, with oil infrastructure among the targets. Ukraine reported Russian strikes on a hospital and on the port of Odesa. - Casualties: Leaked figures from Russia’s official statistics agency suggest at least 450,000 Russian soldiers have died. - Macron said on X that Europe must clearly state its post-war goals, including to Russia, and that Ukraine has a key role in Europe’s post-war order “alongside NATO.” - Kremlin: Moscow says it is open to talks with Europe but complains that the bloc has offered no proposals. - Zelenskyy: European security cannot be considered “without Europe.”

3. UN reports 25 seafarers killed around Hormuz as shipping runs 65 percent below pre-war levels

UN Assistant Secretary-General Khaled Khiari told the Security Council that at least 25 seafarers have been killed in and around the Strait of Hormuz since the US-Israeli war on Iran began in February. The International Maritime Organization has logged 136 incidents between 1 March and 11 November. October was the worst month, with 35. “This situation cannot be allowed to become the new normal,” Khiari said. Traffic through the strait, which carried about a fifth of the world’s oil before the war, is about 65 percent below pre-war levels, according to UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO).

The two sides remain deadlocked. Tehran says the strait stays closed until the US lifts its naval blockade and sanctions and formally ends the war. Washington says the main shipping lanes have been cleared of mines. Iran has meanwhile reported three tankers striking mines. The UN warned of knock-on effects: higher energy, freight and insurance costs, broader inflation, and food insecurity as fertiliser shipments are held up. Merchant crews are drawn largely from labour-exporting countries in Asia and elsewhere, and they are the war’s least-reported casualties.

Key facts: - Deaths: at least 25 seafarers since February. - Incidents (IMO): 136 between 1 March and 11 November. The peak was 35 in October. - Traffic: about 65 percent below pre-war levels (UKMTO). - Iran’s conditions: an end to the US naval blockade, an end to sanctions, and a formal end to the war. - Economic warning: higher energy, transport and insurance costs, inflation, and fertiliser-driven food insecurity.

4. Iran strikes at US base in Bahrain as Houthi missiles target Riyadh

Iran said on Sunday that it had targeted a US base in Bahrain, home of the US Navy’s Fifth Fleet, and Bahraini authorities issued threat alerts. No damage or casualty figures were available at the time of writing. The war is also spreading along its southern flank. The Saudi-led coalition said its air defences destroyed two ballistic missiles fired at Riyadh. Spokesman Turki al-Maliki called them “launched by the Houthi terrorist militia.” The coalition also shot down three drones over the Eastern Province on Sunday, after intercepting two over Riyadh and the south on Saturday. The US Embassy in Riyadh issued a nationwide security alert. It warned that Houthi attacks on airports and infrastructure had “caused civilian casualties and severe travel disruptions.”

The Houthis said they shot down a coalition reconnaissance aircraft over al-Jawf, which they say is the fifth since the escalation began. Their military spokesman Yahya Saree claimed 72 coalition air strikes in 24 hours across Saada, Taiz, Lahij and al-Jawf. Ground fighting has intensified in Marib and al-Jawf.

Key facts: - Deaths in Saudi Arabia: at least 26 people killed by Houthi drone and missile attacks since 23 September (Saudi authorities). - Displacement: more than 340,000 people displaced in Yemen since August (IOM). - Houthi targets: King Khalid International Airport, the East-West pipeline, and oil infrastructure in eastern and southern Saudi Arabia. - Competing claims: Yemen’s government claims 321 Houthi leaders killed or wounded in 723 operations. Neither side’s figures have been verified.

5. Israel kills Hamas commander in Mawasi “humanitarian zone” as Gaza toll since ceasefire passes 1,600

An Israeli drone strike on Saturday hit a tent in al-Mawasi, the coastal strip of Khan Younis crowded with displaced Palestinians that Israel itself designated a “humanitarian zone.” It killed Samir Abu Younis, whom Hamas confirmed as a senior commander of its Rafah brigade. Two others were wounded and taken to Nasser Medical Complex. Israel’s army struck that hospital on Thursday, killing at least six people, including a surgeon. A separate drone strike in Jabaliya on Saturday killed two Palestinians.

The ceasefire of October 2025 is a ceasefire in name only. Gaza’s Health Ministry counts at least 1,643 Palestinians killed and 5,964 injured since it took effect, 16 of them in the past 48 hours. Winter has set in on a population living mostly in tents. Cold-related deaths have reached 11, including a nine-month-old who died of hypothermia. The office of EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas called on Israel to reopen all crossings and allow “immediate, unimpeded access” for aid, saying “Israel must enable the work of the UN and its agencies.” Brussels has issued such statements for two years without imposing any measure that would enforce them.

Key facts: - Total toll since October 2023: at least 74,695 killed and 176,093 injured (Health Ministry). At least 850 bodies have been recovered from rubble since the ceasefire. - Flooding: heavy rain has directly affected at least 106,000 people (UNICEF). Thousands of tents are submerged and latrines are overflowing into camps. - Infrastructure: more than 700km of sewage network destroyed (Gaza media office). - EU called the strike on Nasser Hospital “deeply alarming” and repeated its call for full Israeli withdrawal and a two-state solution.

6. Nationwide FAA ground stop strands 1.5 million passengers

The Federal Aviation Administration halted all departures nationwide at 3:54pm ET on Friday after a telecommunications failure at the air route traffic control centres. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said airspace reopened at 8:15pm. Al Jazeera called it the longest nationwide ground stop in 25 years. FlightRadar24 counted more than 4,700 cancellations and about 10,000 delays on Friday, with about 700 more flights already cancelled for Saturday. Cirium estimated that 11,000 flights carrying more than 1.5 million passengers were still due to depart after 4pm. At least 827 flights were diverted.

Officials’ own account shows years of neglect. Duffy said overloaded switches and routers failed. Officials later admitted that new technology added to the system had overloaded older components, causing “intermittent” communication and radar gaps. A Government Accountability Office report in September had warned about ageing copper cabling, which it dated to the 1960s, and about badly scheduled system changes. Duffy said full recovery would be “slow,” with normal operations expected by Thanksgiving, and called for $50 billion in air traffic control upgrades. Homeland Security Secretary Mullin said there was no suspected cyberattack.

Key facts: - Cancelled: more than 4,700 flights on Friday. Delayed: about 10,000 (FlightRadar24). - Diverted: 827 by 7:05pm ET. Of those, 670 returned to their origin airports (Cirium). - Affected centres: the system of 21 air route traffic control centres (AirSafe.com’s Todd Curtis). - Warning ignored: the September GAO report on obsolete cabling and poorly scheduled upgrades. - Former Australian aviation safety official Tony Stanton: “These are things that can be foreseen, can be anticipated.”

Eisenkot moves to form minority government as Netanyahu clings to caretaker post

Israel’s 27 October election produced no majority. Netanyahu’s right-wing and religious bloc won 53 of 120 seats and Gadi Eisenkot’s four-party bloc 48, while Arab-led parties took a record 19. President Isaac Herzog gave Eisenkot the mandate after 67 lawmakers, including the Arab-led parties, endorsed him against 46 for Netanyahu. Eisenkot has 28 days, extendable by 14. He has ruled out bringing Arab parties into government and instead proposes a minority “national emergency government.” It could pass with 48 votes against Netanyahu’s 46 if the 26 Arab and ultra-Orthodox members abstain.

Netanyahu, now caretaker prime minister, called in a video statement for a “national unity government” to “eliminate the Iranian threat.” He warned that an Eisenkot government would mean Israel’s “collapse.” He and his allies have claimed without evidence that Arab parties “stole” the election, a campaign Eisenkot called “racist.” Al Jazeera’s report records no difference between the blocs on Gaza or Iran. Eisenkot offers to work with Likud and Religious Zionism if they drop Ben-Gvir’s supporters. The Knesset elects its speaker on Tuesday, a contest between Netanyahu ally Amir Ohana and the opposition’s Matan Kahana.

Iraqi premier fires nine officials over militia oil-smuggling network

Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi dismissed nine senior officials and referred them to the courts after an investigation found illegal refineries and tapped pipelines in Jurf al-Nasr, Babil province. The area is a stronghold of the Iran-aligned Kataib Hezbollah, and Al Jazeera’s Peter Smith said it “had effectively been run as a state within a state.” Those removed include Babil’s police chief and its security and intelligence officers, an Interior Ministry commander, and the director of the Oil Pipelines Company. A leaked intelligence estimate put the network’s take at up to $30 million a month. Kataib Hezbollah has threatened al-Zaidi’s government. Akeel Abbas of the Atlantic Council said the move has more to do with “militia disarmament” than corruption. Al-Zaidi has told Washington that Iraqi territory will not be used to attack neighbouring states. That pledge places Baghdad’s crackdown in the context of the US war on Iran.

Hungary seizes $19.5m in cash buried in forests in probe of Orbán-era minister

Hungary’s new National Asset Recovery and Protection Office said searches on 12 and 13 November in Budapest and three other regions recovered about $19.5 million in forints, euros and dollars. The cash was found in farm buildings, warehouses and a house, and buried in forests, including in “a high-value safe in a secluded location.” Finance Minister András Kármán said 12 treasury staff spent eight hours counting it. The investigation targets Miklós Seszták, development minister from 2014 to 2018. He is suspected of taking more than $37.2 million in bribes and kickbacks on overpriced secondhand buses bought by a state transport company and on government construction projects. He was detained in September and denies wrongdoing. Former central bank governor György Matolcsy has also been arrested, over a central bank foundation’s assets.

Prime Minister Péter Magyar, whose centre-right Tisza party ended Orbán’s 16 years in power in April, said the find marked the end of the “Fidesz-KDNP mafia.” Orbán says the investigations are meant to drive him from politics. A Median poll for HVG found that 70 percent of Hungarians consider prosecuting Orbán-era corruption very important. The Magyar government is itself pro-EU and pro-business, and its anti-corruption drive is also a fight within the ruling class over who controls state assets.

French unions call second interprofessional strike day for 17 November

The CGT, UNSA, FSU, Solidaires, CFE-CGC and FA-FP called a second national strike day for Tuesday, 17 November, after the first on 5 November. They announced it following a meeting with the government on 9 November. Private- and public-sector workers, retirees, school and university students, and gilets jaunes groups have been invited, and marches are planned in cities across France. Several actions are running alongside it. Teachers struck on 10 November over pay. Lycée students are in their third month of protests over overcrowding and teacher shortages. Firefighters have extended a partial strike to 4 December. EDF, Enedis and RTE energy workers are on strike until 31 December over cuts to their employee tariff. Grenoble transport workers have filed strike notice through 1 January. Macron arrived at the Francophonie summit amid this wave. The WSWS has reported that general strike days are also set in Italy (20 November) and Greece (25 November), after Spain’s on 11 November.

South Sudan delays first national elections again after Kiir’s “constitutional coup”

The National Elections Commission moved South Sudan’s first presidential and parliamentary elections since independence from 22 December to 11 February 2027, without giving a reason. It is the third postponement at least of a vote first due in 2022. Registration runs from 14 to 28 November at 3,161 centres. About 90 percent of the roughly 15 million population lacks state-recognised identification, so community leaders will issue “identification forms.” In September President Salva Kiir dissolved the transitional unity government and installed a caretaker administration, so the vote can go ahead without the permanent constitution or census required by the 2018 peace deal. Yasmin Sooka, chair of the UN Commission on Human Rights in South Sudan, called it “a constitutional coup through legislative reforms.” Former vice president Riek Machar has been under house arrest since March and is accused of crimes against humanity. Clashes between his followers and government forces have escalated. The 2013–2018 civil war killed at least 400,000 people.

Francophonie summit in Phnom Penh threatened by Congo-Rwanda split

The 20th summit of the Organisation internationale de la Francophonie opened on Sunday in Phnom Penh. It is hosted by Hun Manet, whose government Al Jazeera describes as a de facto one-party state. About 20 leaders are attending, including Macron, Canada’s Mark Carney, Vietnam’s Tô Lâm, Rwanda’s Paul Kagame and the Democratic Republic of Congo’s Félix Tshisekedi. Monday’s vote for secretary-general has become a proxy for the war in eastern Congo. Kinshasa, the US and UN experts say Rwanda backs the M23 militia there. The DRC has threatened to leave the OIF if Rwanda’s Louise Mushikiwabo wins a third term. On Saturday it withdrew its candidate, Juliana Lumumba, and backed former Romanian prime minister Dacian Cioloș. Kigali says Macron asked Kagame to withdraw Mushikiwabo and was refused. France funds most of the OIF’s €71.7 million budget. Mali, Niger and Burkina Faso left in 2025, and a DRC exit would further erode French influence in its former African sphere. UN rapporteur Tom Andrews urged delegates to press for the release of Cambodian political detainees.

Nvidia leadership crisis drags tech lower as the Fed prepares to hike

Markets were closed for the weekend, and the most recent session was Friday, 13 November. Tech and chip stocks fell after Jensen Huang stepped down as chief executive of Nvidia, which dropped below a $4 trillion market capitalisation ahead of its quarterly earnings. Nvidia’s position as the single largest weight in the indices, and the centre of AI-driven speculation, means its troubles move the whole market. The Nasdaq lost 0.91 percent and the S&P 500 0.40 percent, though both still ended the week more than 2 percent higher. Headlines that an Anthropic IPO is approaching kept attention on AI valuations.

Behind the AI story is a tightening credit squeeze. October CPI rose 0.4 percent month-on-month, with core at 0.3 percent. Fed Governor Christopher Waller said a core reading at that level could put a 50 basis point December hike “into play.” Futures price an 85–88 percent chance of a 25bp hike and about 12 percent for 50bp. A Fed hiking into a war-driven energy shock will be paid for by working-class borrowers through mortgage, auto and credit card rates.

Harvard academic workers’ strike enters third week as College admits no contingency plan

More than 4,000 graduate student workers in HGSU-UAW are in the third week of their strike. They are joined for the first time by non-tenure-track faculty and researchers in HAW-UAW. The Harvard Crimson reports that Dean of Harvard College David Deming acknowledged the College made no extensive contingency plans ahead of the walkout. Harvard Law School has postponed its Ames Moot Court rounds to the spring, citing the strike. Crimson opinion pieces by law professor Nikolas Bowie and others, and by classicist Gregory Nagy, have backed the strikers. Bowie’s piece is titled “Harvard Can’t Defend Democracy in Washington While Denying It in Cambridge.”

Key facts: - Pay: Harvard offers about 3 percent a year. Academic workers’ pay has been frozen since July 2024 while Boston-area prices rose 7.9 percent. One striker said he earns about $2,600 a month and pays about $2,600 in rent. - Union-busting: Harvard removed about 800 workers from the bargaining unit the day after the contract expired in June 2025. It is now seeking to remove 300–400 stipend postdocs and bargains only every two weeks. - Cuts: Harvard paid McKinsey at least $250,000 to plan cuts in the Faculty of Arts and Sciences that could eliminate up to 25 percent of staff positions. - Strike pay: $550 a week, paid only after the first week, from a UAW strike fund of nearly $1 billion.

Jerry White wrote on the WSWS that the strike “collides with the Democratic Party establishment.” The Harvard Corporation includes Obama-era officials Sylvia Burwell and Penny Pritzker alongside executives from Blackstone and KKR. White’s conclusion: “Harvard can outlast a strike confined to Harvard. It cannot outlast a movement of the working class as a whole.”

NYC-DSA votes to urge Ocasio-Cortez to run for president in 2028

At its convention on Saturday, the New York City chapter of the Democratic Socialists of America voted 291 to 123 for a resolution urging Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez to run for president in 2028. The resolution commits the chapter to laying groundwork for a campaign. It is not a national DSA endorsement, though a national debate on backing her is under way, alongside an independent “Ready for AOC” effort. Ocasio-Cortez has said she will decide her plans after the midterms, either a presidential run or a Senate challenge.

Key facts: - Membership: NYC-DSA has about 15,000 members. It endorsed Mayor Zohran Mamdani and has backed Ocasio-Cortez in all five of her general elections since 2018. - Congress: a record eight DSA-endorsed candidates won federal office and take their seats in January. - Co-chair Gustavo Gordillo: “If the US can elect Trump, I think we can elect Democratic Socialists.” - Co-chair Grace Mausser: “We are one of the biggest political actors in the city, in the state, and the country, frankly.” She said a platform would focus on “affordability”: housing, healthcare and childcare. - The resolution’s listed priorities include stopping ICE and the anti-war and pro-Palestine movements. It does not mention the Democratic Party, the vehicle any Ocasio-Cortez campaign would run through.

The vote comes as congressional Democrats pledge “bipartisan common ground” with Trump. It shows DSA’s main function: drawing radicalising workers and youth, angry over the Iran war and the cost of living, back into the Democratic Party.

US measles cases pass 5,400 as kindergarten vaccination falls to 92.4 percent

The CDC’s latest update, with counts as of 12 November, reports 5,412 confirmed measles cases in 2026 across 47 jurisdictions. It also reports six deaths and 43 new outbreaks. About 96 percent of cases (5,216) are linked to outbreaks. The 2025 total was 2,289, itself the highest in more than three decades. State health departments’ counts may run higher than the CDC’s. The 2026 hospitalisation figures were blank on the page retrieved.

The cause shows in the CDC’s vaccination data. Kindergarten MMR coverage fell to 92.4 percent in the 2025–26 school year, from 95.2 percent in 2019–20. That is well below the roughly 95 percent needed for community protection. The CDC estimates that about 280,000 kindergartners are unprotected. The decline follows the political attacks on vaccination that began during the pandemic and are now promoted at the top of the federal health apparatus. The US declared measles eliminated in 2000.

Washington State records second H5N1 death

On 1 November, Yakima County health authorities reported a second H5N1 death and a third confirmed case in the county. As of 5 November, the CDC counted 74 human H5 cases and three deaths in 13 states since April 2024, according to the ECDC’s tracker. The ECDC says that count excludes the two latest Yakima cases, and the death toll does not reconcile across agencies. The figures should be checked against the CDC directly. Outside the US, only Bangladesh, Cambodia and India reported human H5N1 infections between August 2025 and June 2026, 12 in total. No mainstream press coverage of the Yakima death was found, which itself says something about the state of public health reporting.

SpaceX catches Starship upper stage for the first time

On the 15th full-scale test flight, Starship’s upper stage reached orbit, deployed 26 Starlink satellites and completed 14 orbits, compared with one or two on the previous mission. About 22 hours after launch it was caught by the launch tower’s “chopstick” arms at Starbase, Texas, the first time a ship returning from orbit has been caught. The Super Heavy booster’s engines cut off early on descent, and it self-destructed over water, so the planned double catch failed. NASA, which is paying SpaceX billions of dollars to land Artemis astronauts on the Moon, praised the flight. Administrator Jared Isaacman, a billionaire and Musk associate, called it “almost magic.” The engineering is a real achievement. It is also happening inside a private monopoly over US launch capacity and the Starlink constellation, which is now central to Pentagon communications.

Roundup: AI regulation, COVID data gap, journals

M. John Harrison wins the Booker Prize

NPR’s Weekend Edition Saturday led its books segment with M. John Harrison becoming the first science-fiction author to win the Booker Prize. Only a programme summary was available, so the details of the shortlisted work have not been verified. If confirmed, the award is significant for more than prize politics. Harrison, now in his eighties, came out of the British “New Wave” of the 1960s around New Worlds magazine. He has spent decades working against the escapist and consolation-giving conventions of genre fiction. His Viriconium cycle and the Kefahuchi Tract novels (Light, Nova Swing, Empty Space), and his later The Sunken Land Begins to Rise Again (2020), are about dislocation, decay and the instability of the world in late capitalist Britain. The Booker has long kept a line between “literary” and “genre” fiction. Its recognition of Harrison suggests that the line, largely a commercial one, is giving way. That would recognise that some of the most serious imaginative engagement with social crisis has come from writers outside the literary establishment.

Mississippi elects a black senator: A blow to racial politics — Patrick Martin

Democrat Scott Colom, a district attorney, won a Mississippi Senate seat by about 7,000 votes out of more than 950,000 cast. He is the first African American senator from the state since Reconstruction. He won Jackson and the majority-Black counties, but also about six white-majority counties. He narrowly carried Lafayette County, home of Ole Miss, which Trump won by 22 points in 2024. Black Mississippians are just under 38 percent of the population, so Colom could not have won on Black votes alone. In the exit polls, fuel and grocery prices were the top issue (42 percent), followed by threats to democratic rights (26 percent), and nearly 90 percent called high prices decisive. Martin places the result alongside other Democratic wins in the South: Keisha Lance Bottoms as Georgia’s first Black governor, Annie Andrews taking South Carolina’s Thurmond–Graham seat, and Democratic sweeps of Texas statewide races. Taken together, he argues, these results refute the racialist framework promoted by the Democrats and the New York Times’ 1619 Project. He recalls Bilbo, Stennis and Eastland and the murders of Emmett Till and Medgar Evers to show how far attitudes have shifted. Interracial marriage has risen from 3 percent in 1967 to 19 percent of new marriages. Martin’s central line: “The fundamental division in capitalist society is class, not race.” Electing Black Democrats “does nothing to change the nature of capitalism as a system of class exploitation,” as the Obama presidency showed.

One-day general strike hits Spain in run-up to November 29 elections — WSWS reporters

Spain’s first general strike in 14 years, on 11 November, brought out at least 4 million workers. Participation was 90 percent in auto (including Seat Martorell), 85 percent in meat processing and 82 percent in refuse collection, with 100 percent among Algeciras dockers. Production was minimal at Petronor and Mercedes in the Basque Country. Crowd estimates vary widely. Madrid officials counted 120,000 and the Tenants’ Union 400,000. In Barcelona, officials counted 55,000 and organisers 400,000. The Interior Ministry logged 919 police charges, and at least 60 people were arrested. CCOO and UGT called the strike under pressure from the October housing protests, after which Sánchez called snap elections for 29 November. The WSWS argues that the election call is meant to herd workers behind PSOE, Podemos and Sumar against the PP and Vox. The workers interviewed made no such distinction. “Podemos is already out of government, now it’s Sumar and company, they are complicit in everything,” said a retiree, Rafael, who added: “The poor always pay for war, workers are the cannon fodder.” Arturo, an immigrant worker, said rent takes 50–60 percent of many wages. The article calls for rank-and-file committees to prepare a European general strike, linking Spain to the French, Italian and Greek strike days.

“It’s about class”: Powerful strike by Harvard academic workers collides with the Democratic Party establishment — Jerry White

Build the HABAŞ Workers Rank-and-File Committee in Türkiye! — Statement of the HABAŞ Workers Rank-and-File Committee Initiative

On 9 November an overhead crane fell into an arc furnace at the HABAŞ steel plant in Aliağa, İzmir. The explosion and fire killed 19 workers. The statement calls it “social murder.” Two other workers died in separate incidents on 21 October and 5 November, and at least 30 have died at HABAŞ in a decade. The company’s own figures show lost-time accidents rose from 147 to 229 in a year, and among subcontracted workers from 38 to 83. HABAŞ reported 145 billion liras in 2025 sales, of which about 2 percent went to workers, and received 18.2 billion liras in state incentives. Chairman Mehmet Rüştü Başaran is worth close to $1 billion. He has been placed under house arrest. A publication ban has been imposed, and the AKP and MHP voted down a parliamentary inquiry. On 13 November workers refused to enter the plant. Gendarmes with water cannon broke up a road blockade and detained nearly 20, and people linked to the company or Türk Metal attacked a workers’ spokesperson. The committee demands an independent investigation by elected workers, engineers, doctors and lawyers, and a halt to production with full pay. It invokes the right to refuse dangerous work under Article 13 of Law No. 6331. It also calls for an inquiry into reports that HABAŞ steel went to Israel, and for public ownership under workers’ control. It proposes delegations to Aliağa’s refineries, shipyards and ports and to Soma.

Indonesian vice president resigns — Peter Symonds

Gibran Rakabuming Raka, son of former president Joko Widodo, resigned on 12 November. The House was due that day to take up an impeachment inquiry, moved by about 120 members, over his missing high-school qualification, after the Supreme Court invalidated his equivalency certificate on 30 October. Symonds reads the resignation as the collapse of the Widodo–Prabowo alliance. Prabowo is purging Widodo loyalists: Finance Minister Sri Mulyani was sacked, the police chief was moved, and three police generals were arrested for wiretapping cabinet members and journalists, a crime Prabowo suggests could be treason. Prabowo’s weakness is the underlying issue. His Gerindra party holds about 15 percent of seats, and his approval rating has fallen from 81.2 percent to 44.5 percent in a year. His flagship free school meals programme has cost 75 trillion rupiah (about $4.1 billion) amid thousands of food poisoning cases. Real wages fell in August, fuel prices rose more than 30 percent in June, and about 23 million people live below the national poverty line. Prabowo has 14 days to nominate two vice-presidential candidates.

South Korea’s president whips up anti-foreigner chauvinism — Daniel Averton

At a 27 October cabinet meeting, President Lee Jae-myung said foreign workers “don’t do much to help the national economy” because they send wages home. The next day he shared a post from the far-right forum FM Korea attacking “preferential treatment” for multicultural, especially Chinese-origin, families. A presidential secretary apologised to migrant groups. Lee has not withdrawn his remarks. Averton connects the remarks to the crisis of the Democratic Party government. Lee’s approval has fallen to 35.9 percent. Seoul housing prices have risen for 91 consecutive weeks, and real wages fell from April through July. Migrants hold the “3D” jobs (dirty, difficult, dangerous) and die at work at 2.3 to 3.6 times the rate of Korean workers, according to the National Human Rights Commission. At least 463 were killed in industrial accidents over five years. He cites Myanmar worker Aung Min Oo, killed in a conveyor on a KTX railway site in July. Targeting ethnic Chinese, Averton argues, prepares public opinion for Seoul’s role in a US war on China. “All wealth in society is created by the working class, whether native-born or migrant.”

Pope Leo XIV’s South American tour: The Vatican rushes to the aid of discredited regimes — Andrea Lobo

The pope’s 12-day tour takes in Uruguay’s Orsi (24 percent approval), Argentina’s Milei (37 percent) and Peru’s Keiko Fujimori, whose approval fell from 57 to 37 percent within weeks of taking office. Oklahoma and Texas execute 2 men, bringing US total to 5 in 8 days — Kate Randall

Oklahoma executed James Pavatt, 73, a retired Air Force master sergeant who maintained his innocence, on 12 November. The state never established who fired the fatal shots, and the parole board refused clemency 3–2. Texas executed John Allen Rubio, 46, whose paranoid schizophrenia was documented at trial, the same day. That makes five executions in eight days and 37 so far in 2026, with five more scheduled. They include Nidal Hasan’s firing-squad execution on 3 December. Eight veterans have been executed this year (Death Penalty Information Center). Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, the state’s first Democratic governor since 1990, says she “personally” opposes the death penalty but will not rule out executions. Tennessee’s “Victims of Communism Day” part of campaign against socialism in US schools — Jane Wise

Under House Bill 2089, Tennessee high schools on 9 November held their first mandatory 45-minute lesson on “victims of communism,” timed to the anniversary of the October Revolution. The lesson groups Lenin with Stalin, Mao and Pol Pot, and adds Maduro’s Venezuela, ten months after the US-backed regime change there. The state provided no curriculum. Wise documents the campaign’s bipartisan character. Clinton signed the 1993 law creating the Victims of Communism Memorial Foundation. In December 2024, 156 Democrats, including Jeffries, Pelosi, Khanna and Raskin, voted for the “Crucial Communism Teaching Act.” In September Education Secretary McMahon held up Tennessee as a national model, citing young people’s growing sympathy for socialism. Wise shows how the “100 million dead” figure from the Black Book of Communism was assembled, and notes that two of the book’s principal contributors repudiated it. She sets against it the death toll of imperialist wars. The lesson omits Stalinism’s murder of the Bolshevik leadership and of Trotsky. Her line: “The ruling class is not afraid of Stalinism. It is afraid of 1917.”

About 300 staff and students confronted Goethe University’s executive board on 3 November over a leaked 59-page restructuring plan. Australia: Todd Russell, Beaconsfield mine disaster survivor, dies aged 54 — Martin Scott

Todd Russell was trapped for 14 days with Brant Webb after the 25 April 2006 rockfall at Beaconsfield, which killed Larry Knight. He died after a long illness.
