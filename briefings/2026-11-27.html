1. Gulf war widens as Italians die in Kuwait and a projectile hits a Saudi refinery

The nine-month US-Israeli war on Iran is spreading across the Gulf. Two technicians from Rheinmetall Italia, the Italian arm of the German arms maker, were killed at a Kuwaiti air-defence surveillance station. Kuwait says they were hit by falling debris as defences intercepted Iranian drones approaching a “vital site.” Within 24 hours, an aerial projectile hit Saudi Aramco’s 400,000-barrel-per-day Jazan refinery on the Red Sea coast, near the Yemeni border. It killed a Nepali worker, injured several others and halted some operations. The Saudi-led coalition blamed the Houthis.

Speaking by phone to troops at al-Dhafra (UAE) and Prince Sultan (Saudi Arabia) airbases on Thanksgiving, Trump said: “You’re going to continue that deployment, and we have victory at hand.” He added that victory would come “whenever I want it to be.” Asked about renewed strikes, he said “if we do something, we will definitely hit Pickaxe Mountain,” the deeply buried site near Natanz. He played down reports that Russia and China helped Iran target US bases, saying they are “very little involved.” Iran’s Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi called the Kuwait deaths “suspicious” and “a precooked recipe for war,” and offered a joint investigation.

Key facts: - Kuwait: A Kuwaiti and a Pakistani worker were also wounded; Al Jazeera’s later report says four injured. IRGC spokesman Sardar Shekarchi called the accusation cover for Israeli-US “false flag” operations. Italian PM Giorgia Meloni expressed her “deepest sorrow,” and the GCC secretary-general called the attack “brutal.” - Bahrain: On Saturday, Bahrain reported an Iranian strike on a civilian industrial facility. The IRGC said it had targeted US air-defence radars. - Yemen spillover: The day before Jazan, the Houthis claimed attacks on Ras Tanura, Saudi Arabia’s largest refinery and export hub, and on the ports of Jeddah and Jazan. Saudi-backed government forces claimed 767 strikes on Houthi positions on Thursday and said they “neutralised” 117 fighters. Air strikes hit Sanaa. - Human toll in Yemen: WHO counts 2,361 killed and 10,982 injured since fighting escalated in August, and the UN says more than 360,000 have been displaced. - Economic fallout: Earlier Houthi attacks led to the cancellation of most international flights to Riyadh and the postponement of the AFC Asian Cup and the FII investment conference. OPEC ministers meet Sunday on 2027 production targets. - Trump on oil: “We don’t need Middle East oil. But what we do need is we need peace in the Middle East.” He also claimed Iranian missiles can reach Europe.

The deaths of arms-industry workers in Kuwait show how far the war’s infrastructure reaches. European weapons firms are staffing Gulf air defences while Washington keeps a naval blockade of Iranian ports.

2. Ukraine strikes Russian ships off Libya as EU haggles over a $49bn war loan

Kyiv says it sank a Russian warship in Benghazi port and struck a cargo vessel carrying military equipment off Libya on Sunday night. Libya’s eastern forces said two officers of Khalifa Haftar’s Libyan National Army (LNA) were killed. The attack has opened a rift with Rome. Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani said Ukraine was wrong to “violate Libya’s sovereignty” and that “Italy was absolutely unaware of Kyiv’s intentions to strike in Libyan territorial waters, even inside a port.” Musa al-Koni, vice president of the Tripoli-based Presidential Council, called it “a declaration of war against the Libyan state.” The Russia-aligned LNA vowed retaliation “at a time and place of its choosing.”

In Brussels, an extraordinary EU summit chaired by António Costa failed on Thursday to agree a roughly $1.76 trillion budget for 2028–2034 and a new $49bn loan to cover Ukraine’s economic and military needs next year. The package includes $110bn for Kyiv over seven years. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz threatened to block the budget unless all members make a “collective commitment” to Ukraine “without exception.” He also insisted “we can’t and we mustn’t take on more debt.” Meloni said the EU was “light-years away from a solution.” Hungarian PM Péter Magyar put the chance of a deal that day at “virtually zero.” The Perspective by Johannes Stern on the WSWS describes European governments as “running amok, making decisions that heighten the risk of a nuclear catastrophe.”

Key facts: - Libya stakes for Italy: Libya supplied roughly a quarter of Italy’s crude imports last year, its largest source. It is also the main departure point for migrants crossing to Italy. Tajani cited Eni and Italy’s small military presence in Libya: “The war between Russia and Ukraine must be prevented from widening.” - Italian politics: Italy has given Ukraine more than €3bn in military aid (Kiel Institute). League leader Matteo Salvini, who opposes further weapons shipments, is strengthened by the incident, says ISPI’s Matteo Villa. - Ukrainian line: Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha called the vessels legitimate targets and said the strikes were “not intended to harm Libya or its people.” - EU obstacles: Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Hungary and Slovakia oppose using EU funds for military aid to Ukraine. Elections next year in France, Italy, Poland, Greece, Estonia, Finland and Slovakia are driving the push for a year-end deal, which requires unanimity. - Broader campaign: The Perspective lists Ukrainian naval-drone attacks in the Gulf of Finland, a Russian warship hit in the Baltic on November 11, and Moscow’s warnings that a blockade of Kaliningrad could trigger use of its “full arsenal.” It also cites Merz’s call to put Ukrainian NATO membership back on the agenda and Polish demands that NATO shoot down Russian missiles over Ukraine.

3. Washington targets 14 economies with new excess capacity tariffs

The Office of the US Trade Representative announced new tariffs on Wednesday against 14 trading partners. They follow an eight-month Section 301 investigation into what Washington calls “structural excess capacity.” This is the administration’s third legal route since the Supreme Court ruled in February that the emergency-powers law did not authorise the April 2025 “Liberation Day” tariffs. A temporary 10% tariff expired in July and was replaced by 10–12.5% duties on 60 economies under Section 301, justified on forced-labour grounds. The new duties are timed just before mid-December G20 meetings and the January 10 expiry of the US-China trade truce.

Key facts: - Targets: China, Vietnam, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the EU, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan and Thailand. Mexico and Norway were investigated but spared for now. - Rates: China faces a proposed 20% on top of roughly 25% from Trump’s first term, and Vietnam 20%. Other rates await Federal Register publication. A separate 10% tariff targets finished Embraer aircraft from Brazil. - Stated grievances: Chinese steel, aluminium, EVs, appliances and solar panels, plus Chinese investment in Vietnam, Indonesia and Cambodia to bypass earlier tariffs. Other complaints cover German and Irish surpluses, Japanese and Chinese car industries, and Singapore’s chips. - Reactions: China’s foreign ministry called “overcapacity” “a false issue.” The European Commission “strongly reject[s] the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity.” Switzerland rejected the allegations. - Experts: Georgetown’s Gregory Shaffer called the use of Section 301 “cynical,” saying it lets the president bypass Congress’s taxing power. Trade expert Dmitry Grozoubinski said it was meant as “a scalpel” but is being used “like a flamethrower.” - Politics: Republicans lost heavily in the November 3 midterms, where cost of living dominated. Democrats take both chambers in January.

4. Gaza winter floods and a meningitis surge under Israeli aid restrictions

Gaza’s fourth winter of war is pushing displaced Palestinians to flee yet again, from flooded tent camps into damaged buildings that are at risk of collapse. A storm from November 6 flooded about 70,000 tents within 12 hours, affecting some 226,000 people, according to preliminary UN figures. On November 8, a sand embankment collapsed onto tents south of Deir el-Balah, killing five people, including children. An infant drowned during the same storm, and a nine-month-old died of hypothermia on November 13. Another storm was forecast within days.

Israel keeps the population confined to less than 40% of the territory, according to IOM and WFP. It blocks shelter materials as “dual-use” and has said reconstruction equipment will enter only after Hamas fully disarms. Defence Minister Israel Katz, in the caretaker government, threatened a new offensive that would push one million Palestinians south.

Key facts: - Disease: WHO recorded 692 suspected meningitis cases from October 1 to November 23, mostly in babies under one. Gaza’s Health Ministry issued an epidemic alert on November 24 after 272 cases in October, against a usual monthly average of 30–50. - Shelter: OCHA says about 1.36 million people, 65% of the population, live in tents or makeshift shelters. More than 150,000 tents wait outside Gaza, mostly in El Arish. The Norwegian Refugee Council has fewer than 6,500 tents in stock inside. - Profiteering from the blockade: Winter supplies for a family of six cost over $6,000 inside Gaza, against about $700 abroad (NRC). Donors have funded 35% of the $283m minimum winter shelter appeal. - Infrastructure: Eight pumping stations and three main rainwater basins in Gaza City were out of service when the first storm hit. Civil defence received 2,166 distress calls on November 7 but could send crews to only 218. - Diplomacy: Qatar’s emir and Egypt’s el-Sisi met in Cairo on Thursday and discussed “joint mediation efforts to solidify the ceasefire.” El-Sisi called for reconstruction “without delay.”

5. Federal judge strikes down Trump’s second birthright citizenship order

US District Judge Christopher Cooper ruled on Wednesday that the executive orders Trump signed on August 6 are unconstitutional. “The president cannot carve out new exceptions to birthright citizenship,” Cooper wrote. The orders were a narrower follow-up after the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s original order in June, relying on Wong Kim Ark (1898). They targeted three categories: children born through “birth tourism,” children of foreign-government or international-organisation workers without diplomatic immunity, and children with a parent classified as an “alien enemy.”

Key facts: - A Maryland court had already blocked the same policy with a preliminary injunction in September. A separate judge denied an injunction on August 28. - ACLU lawyer Cody Wofsy: the Supreme Court has “twice rejected efforts to gut birthright citizenship.” - White House spokeswoman Lauren Bis: “just another ruling from an activist judge.” - The Migration Policy Institute estimated that about 255,000 babies a year would have been denied citizenship under the original order. - The “alien enemy” category links citizenship to wartime emergency powers, the same Alien Enemies Act framework the administration has used for deportations.

6. New York Times documents suggest the CIA waterboarded more prisoners than admitted

Documents the New York Times obtained by suing for material from special prosecutor John Durham’s investigation point to an unacknowledged waterboarding. Durham was appointed in 2008, and his probe ended without criminal charges. A partially redacted summary of a 2012 FBI interview with a CIA contract interpreter describes a prisoner called “Bashir” strapped to a board with his head tilted down into water, with “approximately three to four people ‘doing this.’” The interpreter mentioned a detainee death that matches “Detention Site Cobalt,” known as the “Salt Pit,” and the Times concluded the site was likely near Bagram, outside Kabul.

Key facts: - The CIA has admitted waterboarding only three men, all at black sites in Thailand and Poland: Abu Zubaydah, Khalid Shaikh Mohammed and Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri. All three remain at Guantánamo. - The 2014 Senate report found Abu Zubaydah was waterboarded 83 times. US intelligence reports say he was not a member of al-Qaeda. - The CIA has never acknowledged waterboarding in Afghanistan. It did not respond to a request for comment. - The interpreter “saw the incident in passing but did not participate himself.”

No official under the Bush or Obama administrations was ever prosecuted for the torture program. The new evidence surfaced only through a newspaper lawsuit, more than a decade after the investigation closed.

Middle East

Settlers bulldoze Palestinian homes in Jaloud as West Bank attacks hit record

Security cameras showed settlers bringing a bulldozer to two two-storey homes of the Tubasi family in Jaloud, near Nablus, at about 1am Thursday and smashing their concrete foundations (Reuters). Settlers had set up a tent near the homes in April, then cut water and power, set fires, and finally gave the family an hour to leave or be killed “like the Dawabsheh family.” In September, soldiers escorted the family back under a court order. About 200 settlers attacked ten minutes later and burned the houses. The army called Thursday’s attack “a serious criminal act,” but settlers stayed in the area all day, and the court-ordered removal has not happened.

Africa

Erdogan hosts Haftar in Ankara as Turkey courts both Libyan camps

Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan received Khalifa Haftar in Ankara on Wednesday. It was the eastern commander’s first visit there, nearly seven years after Turkish intervention drove his forces out of western Libya. The meeting came days after Tripoli’s prime minister met Turkey’s foreign minister, and Erdogan reportedly plans to visit Libya in December, his first trip in 15 years. The visit came the same week as the Ukrainian strike on Benghazi port, which shows how far Libya has become a theatre of competing powers.

  • In July, Ankara reached agreements with Haftar’s LNA on military infrastructure and training. In October, eastern authorities adopted the 2019 Turkey–Tripoli maritime boundary deal, which Greece, Israel and Egypt oppose.
  • US mediators, including Trump adviser Massad Boulos, back Saddam Haftar to chair the three-member Presidency Council. Factions in Misrata oppose this.
  • A UN-backed “4+4” roadmap in August called for elections within two years. Libya faces a weakening dinar, fuel and cash shortages, and a year of protests and strikes.
  • Analyst Sansom Milton: “division remains profitable for elites and armed groups on each side.”

Cairo fuel tanker inferno kills 31

A fuel tanker overturned and burst into flames at about 6:30pm Wednesday on an elevated stretch of Cairo’s ring road in al-Waraq, Giza. The fire destroyed 27 vehicles. The Health Ministry’s toll on Thursday was 31 dead and 26 injured, and forensic teams are using relatives’ DNA to identify bodies. Prosecutors detained a 21-year-old car driver who said he clipped the tanker while overtaking at 110 km/h. They have not explained why a tanker was on the road at rush hour despite a 2018 ban on trucks over four tonnes from 6am to midnight (AFP).

  • The Transport Ministry says it spent about $37bn on transport from 2014 to 2024. Yet road deaths rose 10.8% to 5,829 in 2025, with about 84,000 injured (CAPMAS). A Giza train crash earlier this month killed at least seven.
  • Transport Minister Kamel al-Wazir, appointed after the 2019 Ramses station fire, again rejected calls to resign.
  • The government’s answer is charity: seven charities have given 24 million pounds (about $445,000), and the cabinet promised bank certificates for bereaved families.
  • A microbus driver who survived: “It was God’s hellfire on the ring road.”

Guinea-Bissau junta postpones elections to 2028

Transitional President Major-General Horta Inta-a issued a decree on Thursday moving presidential and legislative elections from December 6, 2026, to November 2028. It cites “an insufficiency of the technical, material and financial conditions.” The army seized power in November 2025, disrupting a presidential vote and ousting Umaro Sissoco Embalo. The transitional charter set a one-year transition. In August, voters approved a constitutional amendment expanding presidential powers. ECOWAS has called for a “short, structured, and transparent” return to civilian rule. The country of 2.2 million is a transit hub for Latin American cocaine bound for Europe and part of a wave of West African coups since 2020.

Asia-Pacific

Indonesian gold mine flood kills at least 13 days after candy factory fire

Floodwater hit an unlicensed gold mine at Pongkor in Bogor district, West Java, on Monday, about 60km south of Jakarta, in an area where state-owned PT Antam also mines. At least 13 workers are dead and 13 missing, the search and rescue chief said Thursday. Eleven miners escaped. Police heard of trapped miners only on Tuesday, about 16 hours later, because the mine operated illegally and locals first tried to pump out the water themselves. A full-scale operation began only after PT Antam filed a formal report after midnight on Wednesday. Some 325 rescuers are working in shafts about 100 metres deep, narrow and poorly lit.

The disaster follows the November 22 fire at PT Fonusa Agung Mulia’s Milkita candy factory in Cikande, Banten, which has killed at least 26 workers. Al Jazeera’s last confirmed toll on November 23 was 24. The WSWS’s detailed report on the factory fire is summarised in the WSWS coverage section.

US measles cases pass 6,000 with nine deaths

As of November 24, the CDC reports 6,039 confirmed measles cases in 2026 across 47 jurisdictions, with 43 outbreaks and 9 deaths. About 97% of cases (5,829) are linked to outbreaks. The count was 5,412 on November 12 and 2,289 for all of 2025. Cases passed 5,000 in early November, the highest in 35 years. The CDC is working with the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists on a standard definition of a measles death, after earlier reports gave four or six. That may partly explain the jump to nine. A PAHO commission met November 10–13 to review US measles elimination status, and the research found no outcome. The next CDC update is December 4. The figures follow the administration’s dismantling of federal vaccine policy and point toward the formal loss of US elimination status.
