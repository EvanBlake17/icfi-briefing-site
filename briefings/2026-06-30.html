1. US–Iran war ceasefire strains as Iran strikes US bases in Kuwait and Bahrain

The four-month US war on Iran lurched toward renewed open conflict over the weekend before both sides paused on Monday. On June 28, Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps fired ballistic missiles and drones at the US Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and the US Fifth Fleet headquarters in Bahrain, claiming to have destroyed eight US military sites. The strikes followed two days of US bombing around the Strait of Hormuz, including Sirik and Qeshm.

After four days of trading strikes, both sides stood down Monday. Secretary of State Marco Rubio signaled that technical talks in Switzerland are possible June 29–30. The war began February 28; a 14-point interim memorandum of understanding signed in early June gave both sides 60 days to reach a final agreement. The current flashpoint is Iran’s push to collect tolls from ships transiting Hormuz — a demand Oman explicitly opposes.

Key facts: - Iran claimed eight US military sites destroyed in the June 28 strikes on Kuwait and Bahrain - Iran recorded roughly 4,800 cyber incidents targeting Israel this month, up from ~1,600 in June 2025 - Global GDP growth is now forecast at just 2.5% for 2026, attributed in large part to the conflict; inflation in developing economies is projected to rise from 4.2% to 5.2% - Oil prices pulled back toward $70 as investors focused on the resumption of US–Iran talks

The war directly endangers the populations of Gulf states drawn into the crossfire and inflicts an invisible toll on Iran’s working class, whose civilian infrastructure and casualties receive almost no serious attention in Western media.

2. Venezuela earthquake toll passes 1,450 as US Marines deploy to reopen La Guaira port

The death toll from the twin earthquakes that struck Venezuela on June 24 has surpassed 1,450, with more than 3,150 injured and over 46,600 reported missing. A magnitude 7.2 quake struck at 18:04 local time, followed just 39 seconds later by a magnitude 7.5 mainshock. La Guaira, north of Caracas, saw more than 1,400 buildings destroyed, and Simón Bolívar International Airport was heavily damaged.

Approximately 130 US Marines arrived at the port of La Guaira to reopen it for aid delivery, with the USS Fort Lauderdale delivering supplies and more than 300 US search-and-rescue responders already operating in the affected areas. As Andrea Lobo noted on the WSWS, the framing of the deployment as humanitarian “aid” obscures that Venezuela’s government remains under a years-long US sanctions regime that weakened the country’s infrastructure.

The disaster compounded a separate atrocity: a US deportation flight (Flight 164) carrying 146 Venezuelans arrived six hours before the quakes and was held at the Hotel Santuario La Llanada for processing. The building collapsed entirely. Only 12 of the 147 deportees survived; one survivor reached hospital “intubated and with both legs amputated.”

Key facts: - Over 300 aftershocks have followed the mainshock - The June 24 Supreme Court ruling in Mullin v. Doe — issued the same day as the earthquakes — stripped federal courts of jurisdiction to review Temporary Protected Status termination decisions - Over 5,000 Venezuelans remain in ICE detention

3. Supreme Court overturns 91-year-old Humphrey’s Executor precedent

In a 6-3 ruling on June 29, the US Supreme Court found that Trump’s March 2025 firing of FTC Commissioner Rebecca Kelly Slaughter without cause was lawful, overturning the 1935 Humphrey’s Executor v. United States precedent. The decision eliminates removal protections for officials of multi-member independent agencies, fundamentally restructuring the executive branch and expanding presidential control over bodies Congress had deliberately insulated from political pressure.

NPR described the ruling as taking “a sledgehammer to much of the federal government’s regulatory structure.” It affects the FTC, NLRB, MSPB, CPSC, NTSB and dozens of other bodies that regulate labor disputes, workplace discrimination, product recalls, plane-accident investigations and credit unions.

Key facts: - In a separate 5-4 ruling, the Court allowed Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook to remain pending further litigation, with the majority stating the decision does not implicate Fed removal restrictions - The ruling means Trump can now remove any official of the NLRB, MSPB or CPSC blocking his agenda on union-busting, federal-worker firings or product-safety deregulation - No major outlet has reported specifically on the consequences for labor-rights enforcement

Sources NPR Supreme Court overturns Humphrey’s Executor in FTC case · CBS News Supreme Court rules for Trump in FTC firing case · The Hill Supreme Court strips firing protections from independent agencies

4. European heatwave kills more than 1,300 since June 21

WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus announced more than 1,300 excess deaths across Europe since June 21, stating that “European homes, workplaces and schools were not built for these temperatures.” France alone recorded approximately 1,000 excess deaths, with temperatures reaching 44°C (111.2°F) in one town and at least 40 people dying by drowning. Spain recorded 327 heat-related deaths; Germany at least seven.

Climate-attribution research from World Weather Attribution found that heatwaves like this are now “about 30 times more likely” than in the pre-climate-change era, with events that once occurred once per 300 years now happening more than once per decade. Europe warms at twice the global average rate.

The WHO’s death count skews heavily toward people over 65, shut-ins and low-income workers without air conditioning or paid sick leave. That France — with one of the world’s most comprehensive welfare states — still recorded 1,000 excess deaths underscores the systematic abandonment of the elderly and the poor.

Key facts: - Temperatures topped 40°C in Germany, the Czech Republic and Poland - Germany saw two drowning deaths in Berlin as people sought relief in water - Today’s WSWS Perspective (see WSWS coverage) frames the deaths as “capitalism-driven climate change and criminal government neglect,” not a natural disaster

5. Israel strikes Lebanon 48 hours after signing framework ceasefire

Israeli strikes resumed on June 28 — just 48 hours after a US-brokered framework ceasefire was signed in Washington on June 26 — targeting southern Lebanon and the Hezbollah buffer zone. One Lebanese civilian was killed, the first death since the deal, and one Israeli soldier, Captain David Hazutt, 21, of the Golani Brigade, was also killed.

The war began March 2, 2026, when Hezbollah fired rockets at Israel following the killing of Iran’s supreme leader; more than 4,200 people have been killed in Lebanon since then. PM Netanyahu called the framework “historic” and “a massive blow to Iran and Hezbollah,” while Rubio called it “the beginning of the beginning.” Hezbollah leader Qassem called the deal “humiliating” and “a surrender of sovereignty,” rejected it outright, and said fighters would remain engaged.

Key facts: - The framework links Israeli withdrawal to Hezbollah disarmament — not unconditional withdrawal — and establishes a 10km Israeli security zone inside Lebanon - The agreement followed five rounds of talks

6. UN inquiry finds Israel deliberately killing Palestinian children

A UN Commission of Inquiry concluded that Israel has deliberately targeted and killed Palestinian children, constituting genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity. Approximately one Palestinian child has been killed every day on average since the October 2025 “ceasefire” took effect.

Over the weekend, the IDF struck a Hamas tunnel system in central Gaza, claiming Hamas was “restoring” it “in violation of the ceasefire.” Senior IDF intelligence and Southern Command officers warned Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir that Hamas’s military wing is producing hundreds of explosive devices and anti-tank missiles every month, preparing for renewed war.

Key facts: - Palestinians continue to face severely limited medical care, food security and shelter under the purported ceasefire - The findings drew on patterns of strikes documented since October 2025

7. Ukraine launches record 660-drone assault on Russia

Ukraine launched one of its heaviest nighttime drone assaults of the war, targeting a dozen Russian regions, Russian-held Crimea and surrounding seas. Russian air defenses intercepted 660 Ukrainian drones, exceeding the previous record of 556 set on May 17. Ukrainian long-range drones have systematically targeted Russian oil production and energy facilities, choking fuel supplies and stalling battlefield efforts, according to Western officials.

The territorial situation remains contested. ISW data shows Russia suffered a net loss of 20 square miles over four weeks; Ukraine’s DeepState OSINT group puts Russian gains at 12 square miles over the same period. Putin claimed June 23 that Russian forces are close to capturing Kostyantynivka.

Key facts: - Energy blackouts caused by Russian strikes have cut Ukrainian economic growth by 2.5 percentage points so far in 2026 - Ukraine’s strikes have brought “chaos to Crimea,” decimating Russian logistics, per Al Jazeera

South Africa: mass anti-immigrant protests as June 30 deadline passes

The South African government set June 30 as a deadline for undocumented foreigners to leave the country, triggering nationwide anti-immigrant protests. More than 25,000 foreigners had already been bussed out before the deadline, with thousands more fleeing to repatriation centers, consulates and makeshift camps. President Cyril Ramaphosa urged protesters to demonstrate peacefully, warning that “intimidation, threats and violence will not be tolerated.” Police deployed to hotspots nationwide; by early morning no major incidents had been recorded.

The deadline was set by the ANC government, not simply by mob pressure. South Africa’s 32% unemployment rate — the product of decades of neoliberal policy — has created the material conditions for the scapegoating of immigrant communities for the social crisis produced by the ruling class.

Belfast riots expose neo-Nazi “Active Clubs” network

Riots that broke out June 9 across Belfast — following a stabbing allegedly committed by a Sudanese man — have been linked to a neo-Nazi network, the “Ulster Youth Club,” part of the global “Active Clubs” movement. Homes, businesses and vehicles were set alight, and rioters went door-to-door targeting immigrants’ homes. Per Wired, the network played a role “in not only stoking tensions, but advising and orchestrating the masked youths who spearheaded much of the violence.”

The co-founder of the Global Project Against Hate and Extremism said: “Protests in Belfast had hardly started before Active Club senior voices were functioning as a support network for the racist riots and encouraging replication in other countries.” Active Clubs are a global network of fascist, white-nationalist youth groups organized around mixed martial arts, with cells confirmed in the US, Canada, Australia and across Europe. As of June 29–30, no individual rioters had been confirmed as formally affiliated.

DRC bans mass gatherings in Kinshasa to contain Ebola

The Democratic Republic of Congo has banned mass gatherings in the capital, Kinshasa, and the provinces of Tshopo, Haut-Uele and Bas-Uele to halt the spread of Ebola. The outbreak’s expansion to include the capital represents a significant escalation in the public-health emergency. Full casualty figures were not available as of June 30. The DRC’s recurring health crises unfold against the backdrop of imperialist resource extraction that has left the country with one of the world’s most under-resourced public-health systems.

Millions face cutoff of food aid and healthcare

One year after Trump signed the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act” (OBBBA) on July 4, 2025, 4.7 million people have lost SNAP food-stamp benefits (11% of all recipients) and 3.9 million have lost ACA health-insurance subsidies. ACA plan enrollment fell from 23.1 million in 2025 to 19.2 million in 2026, a 16% drop.

The cuts cross party lines. Arizona — governed by Democrat Katie Hobbs — has cut SNAP enrollment by over 50%, stripping benefits from 457,000 people, including 196,000 children, to avoid a $201.5 million federal penalty. SNAP enrollment is down in all 50 states and by 10% or more in 21, including Democratic-run Illinois, Connecticut and Massachusetts. As Patrick Martin noted on the WSWS, a University of Pennsylvania study projected that even an earlier estimate of 3.2 million losing SNAP would produce 93,000 premature deaths over 14 years; at actual numbers the toll approaches 140,000.

Texas moves to impose Christian nationalist curriculum

The Texas State Board of Education approved a new statewide reading list incorporating Bible stories — Noah’s Ark, David and Goliath, creation accounts — from kindergarten through high school, effective 2030, presented as “classical” education rather than religious instruction. The move accompanies a social-studies overhaul reducing world history and geography in favor of Texas and US history.

It follows Texas Senate Bill 10 (effective September 2025) requiring display of the Ten Commandments in all classrooms, upheld by a federal appeals court in April 2026 after dissenting judges warned the ruling “trampled on the First Amendment.” Since 2021, over 22,000 book challenges have targeted works on race, gender, sexuality and LGBTQ themes, including campaigns to remove Islamic mathematical and astronomical contributions from curricula. The WSWS argues the program is designed to divide workers and youth and to cultivate support for military recruitment.

Science, technology, and public health

  • HPV vaccine eliminates cervical-cancer deaths in young English women — no deaths recorded among women aged 20–24 from 2020 to 2024, a first
  • Measles tops 2,134 US cases — 30 new outbreaks; 93% of cases outbreak-associated
  • H5N1 reaches Australia — first detections of the globally circulating strain in migratory seabirds

A study published in The Lancet, led by Queen Mary University of London and funded by Cancer Research UK, estimates that the HPV vaccine has prevented around 200 deaths from cervical cancer in England, and found that no women aged 20–24 died from the disease between 2020 and 2024 — the first such period on record. The finding is a striking demonstration of what coordinated, publicly funded immunization can achieve, even as vaccine programs face political assault and funding cuts internationally.

Disease and outbreak roundup: - Measles: As of June 25, the US has recorded 2,134 confirmed cases in 2026, with 30 new outbreaks and 93% of cases (1,982 of 2,134) outbreak-associated — driven by collapsing vaccination rates - H5N1 bird flu: Australia confirmed its first detections of the globally circulating H5N1 strain in migratory seabirds (a brown skua and northern giant petrel) near Esperance, Western Australia, plus a case in South Australia; authorities are conducting drone surveillance around sea-lion breeding sites. The strain remains widespread in wild birds worldwide and continues to infect US dairy and poultry workers sporadically - Ebola: The DRC outbreak’s spread to Kinshasa (see International developments) marks a dangerous urban escalation

In research, a 20-year Japanese study published in Nature Communications reported that repeated cloning of laboratory mice from a single donor could not continue beyond 58 generations, after large structural and lethal DNA mutations accumulated and birth rates declined from the 27th generation — with implications for the long-term limits of cloning technology.

On technology, the BIS warning of an AI “investment bust” (see World economy) is itself a major tech-policy development: the concentration of more than $1 trillion in capital expenditure among five firms, on the speculative premise that “only a small number of players with superior technology will ultimately dominate,” reflects the irrationality of AI development organized around private profit rather than human need. Climate science also featured prominently this week, with World Weather Attribution confirming that fossil-fuel emissions have made the deadly European heatwave roughly 30 times more likely (see Top stories).

Arts and culture

This week’s cultural calendar is dominated by the European summer festival season, even as the heatwave and ongoing wars cast a long shadow over the continent. The Karlovy Vary International Film Festival opens July 1 with Pedro Almodóvar’s Bitter Christmas, a Cannes-premiered tragicomedy that the Spanish director will present at a special screening in Mariánské Lázně. Almodóvar, now in his mid-seventies, continues to work through themes of memory, mortality and the intimate wreckage of family life — a body of work that, at its best, treats human subjectivity with a seriousness largely absent from contemporary commercial cinema. The festival’s lineup spans Cannes winners and Czech premieres, offering a window into a European art cinema that remains, however unevenly, engaged with social reality.

The festival circuit’s awards season also drew to a close. The 25th Tribeca Festival in New York handed top honors to Cotton Fever, Labrador – Autopsy of Silence and Jail Time Records — the last a documentary engaging directly with incarceration and its cultural aftermath. The Provincetown International Film Festival on Cape Cod celebrated filmmakers including Jane Schoenbrun and paid tribute to John Waters, the veteran transgressor whose work skewered American respectability and middle-class conformity for half a century.

In the visual arts, the Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art in Arkansas — founded with Walmart heir wealth — debuted a 114,000-square-foot expansion anchored by an exhibition of Keith Haring’s rarely shown three-dimensional pieces. Haring’s politically charged work against apartheid, nuclear weapons and the neglect of the AIDS crisis sits in uneasy tension with the source of the museum’s funding, a contradiction worth noting: the fortunes of the retail oligarchy increasingly underwrite the institutional framing of 20th-century radical art.

A note of cultural-political significance from the news pages: the Texas curriculum overhaul (see United States) represents a frontal assault on cultural and intellectual life. The drive to inject Bible stories into public schooling from kindergarten, alongside more than 22,000 book challenges since 2021 — including efforts to erase Islamic contributions to mathematics and astronomy — is an attempt to subordinate education and historical memory to a reactionary, nationalist project.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “Europe’s record heatwave: Climate catastrophe or world socialism” by Thomas Scripps — read in full on wsws.org. Scripps grounds the crisis in hard data — June temperatures that “would have been virtually impossible 50 years ago” — and catalogs the material wreckage: power-plant shutdowns, halted transport, mass school closures, 200,000 poultry deaths in France, the Po River drying to critical lows. He argues food-price inflation linked to extreme weather is “rising more than twice as fast as average groceries inflation” and may account for 30 to 50 percent of recent continental inflationary pressure, with Allianz projecting losses exceeding $600 billion by 2030. His central charge: European governments are “arsonists” who suppress the science they claim to accept, because capitalism cannot subordinate profit to long-term planning. The conclusion: “Safeguarding human life on earth means seizing power away from these arsonists by expropriating the oligarchy and toppling capitalist governments in a world socialist revolution.”

UAW calls snap vote at Bridgewater Interiors in Warren, Michigan — Shannon Jones

The UAW is rushing a snap ratification vote at Bridgewater Interiors after workers rejected a previous contract by a 95 percent margin in May. Production starting pay under the new deal would be $20/hour rising to $29 after four years, with no cost-of-living raises, no pension and no job-security provisions — against 4.2 percent annual inflation. Shawn Fain personally intervened to pressure workers; a Local 400 president’s salary was quietly raised from $95,000 to $118,000. A parallel case at Nexteer Automotive (Saginaw) saw three previous rejections overridden and a worker fired for criticizing a UAW rep. The WSWS calls for rejection and for the building of independent rank-and-file committees.

Police mobilized to break picket lines in 6-week Ontario strike — Carl Bronski

Australian citizen to return from Syria under police-state conditions — Mike Head

Haitians charged in Chile with people smuggling: a right-wing frameup — Mauricio Saavedra

A June 15 comptroller’s report claimed 64 Haitian children couldn’t be located, igniting media hysteria about trafficking and organ harvesting; by June 23 police had found all 64 safe and in school. Saavedra ties the campaign to President Kast’s family history (a Nazi Party father; implication in the Pinochet-era Paine massacre of 70 peasants) and to Kast cutting children’s services by $221 million while manufacturing the panic.

A letter from historian Steven Mintz on “The American Revolution and Its Place in History”

Mintz, Professor of History at UT Austin, commends a WSWS webinar for characterizing the Revolution as “a bourgeois revolution: a revolt against mercantilist trade restrictions, caste and ascribed status, aristocratic privilege, inherited dependence.” He defends Marxist staged historical analysis and argues the Revolution “profoundly destabilized the ideological foundations of slavery.” He criticizes younger historians for abandoning ideological analysis, jumping “from material structures to discourse, identity, or power without adequately explaining how ideas acquire authority,” and contrasts the disengagement of major historical associations with the WSWS’s public approach.

Left Voice

Left Voice’s “Elections in Colombia Signal Another Victory for the Right in Latin America” correctly identifies the victory of far-right millionaire Abelardo de la Espriella as part of a wave of Trump-aligned candidates across the region — a genuine warning. But the analysis remains within a framework of denunciation rather than a concrete program for the Colombian working class to break from the Petro government’s bourgeois nationalism. Separately, Juan Dal Maso’s piece engaging Peter Frase on Mamdani’s “first 100 days” as mayor reflects the PTS tendency’s persistent orientation to the DSA milieu — debating the trajectory of a Democratic Party mayor rather than counterposing an independent revolutionary perspective. The day’s output combines sharp anti-fascist rhetoric with continued adaptation to the very reformist currents it formally criticizes.

PSL / Liberation News

The PSL’s “Why Trump failed in his criminal war against Iran — and why we need to keep up the pressure” advances the line that the Iranian regime’s resistance represents a defeat for US imperialism. While correctly opposing the US war, the PSL invests the bourgeois-clerical regime in Tehran with a progressive, anti-imperialist character, obscuring its repression of the Iranian working class and its own reactionary maneuvers. This is the politics of “anti-imperialist” campism: solidarity with a capitalist state rather than with the Iranian and Gulf-state workers who are the war’s true victims.

Socialist Alternative

Socialist Alternative (ISA) is orienting heavily to the new Corbyn–Sultana formation in Britain. “The Tragedy Of Your Party & Lessons For Socialists” and the broader “Which Way Forward for the Left?” issue of International Marxism treat the 800,000 sign-ups to the Corbyn–Sultana party as a historic opening, urging socialists to build it as a broad left vehicle. This is a renewed version of the broad-party perspective that produced one debacle after another — from SYRIZA to Die Linke — subordinating the independent mobilization of the working class to left-reformist parliamentary projects. The domestic piece “Justice For Cyrus Carmack-Belton,” on the acquittal of a gas-station owner in the killing of a Black teenager, channels legitimate anger into the framework of racial-justice protest without a class program.

Socialist Worker (SWP)

Alex Callinicos’s “Europe’s backing risks widening war” warns that British and EU backing for Ukraine could fuel a conflict approaching “the nuclear brink” — a useful observation that nonetheless stops short of a revolutionary defeatist program against all the imperialist powers. “‘Peace talks change nothing,’ says Lebanese socialist” correctly exposes the Israel–Lebanon framework as a fraud. But the SWP’s signature move remains the cross-class “unity” front: its promotion of the new “Together” alliance and “unity marches against the far right” subordinates the anti-fascist struggle to alliances with Labour figures and NGOs rather than the independent mobilization of the working class. The day’s orientation mixes incisive anti-war commentary with a popular-frontist anti-racism.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Active Clubs: the transnational fascist network behind the Belfast riots

The Belfast riots are being treated as a UK-specific story, but the “Active Clubs” network is explicitly international, with cells in the US, Canada, Australia and Europe coordinating to encourage replication of anti-immigrant violence. This is an underreported story about transnational fascist organizing enabled by social-media platforms that have gutted content moderation — and a warning of the organized infrastructure being built atop the wave of state-sponsored xenophobia.

Recommended reading NPR Could neo-Nazi youth, or ‘Active Clubs,’ have played a role in Belfast riots?

2. US corporate bankruptcies hit a 15-year high

Thirty-two “mega” bankruptcies and 117 large-company filings over the past 12 months — 44% above the historical average — point to deepening instability beneath the tech-driven stock rally. The collapse of firms like Spirit Airlines, Rite Aid and Joann means mass layoffs and the destruction of working-class jobs and pensions, even as the markets celebrate Alphabet’s entry into the Dow.

Recommended reading CFO Dive US corporate bankruptcies jump to 15-year high

3. USTR proposes “forced labor” tariffs on 60 economies

The Trump administration’s USTR has proposed 10%–12.5% additional tariffs on imports from 60 economies, cynically invoking “forced labor” enforcement to escalate its trade war — including against Canada, the EU and Mexico. A socialist analysis would expose how the language of labor rights is weaponized for protectionist and great-power ends, while the same administration guts protections for US workers at home.

Recommended reading CNBC US proposes tariffs on 60 economies over forced labor

4. HPV vaccine eliminates cervical-cancer deaths among young English women

A Lancet study found no cervical-cancer deaths among English women aged 20–24 from 2020 to 2024 — a historic public-health achievement attributable to a coordinated, publicly funded vaccination program. This is a powerful demonstration of what social planning can accomplish, and a sharp counterpoint to the anti-vaccine drive and public-health funding cuts being pursued in the US.

Recommended reading The Lancet HPV vaccination and cervical cancer mortality in England

5. H5N1 bird flu reaches Australia as pandemic risk rises

Australia has confirmed its first detections of the globally circulating H5N1 strain in migratory seabirds, closing one of the last gaps in the virus’s worldwide spread. With scientists warning the virus “could spark a human pandemic in 2026,” the continued absence of coordinated international public-health planning — and the suppression of disease surveillance under budget austerity — demands sustained socialist attention.

Recommended reading CDC A(H5) bird flu: current situation summary

6. Almodóvar opens Karlovy Vary as European art cinema confronts a continent in crisis

Pedro Almodóvar’s Bitter Christmas opens the Karlovy Vary festival on July 1, the latest instance of major auteurs anchoring smaller European festivals as Hollywood retreats into financialized franchise output. A WSWS review situating the film and the festival within the social crisis gripping Europe — war, heat deaths, the rise of the far right — would serve the site’s longstanding engagement with serious contemporary cinema.

Recommended reading Prague Reporter 10 films to see at the 2026 Karlovy Vary International Film Festival
