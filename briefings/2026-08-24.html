1. Washington launches economic D-Day sanctions against Iran

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent held a Monday press conference to unveil what the administration is calling the largest coordinated economic isolation campaign in history against Iran, six months into the war that began with the US-Israeli “Operation Epic Fury” strikes of February 28. Bessent had trailed the announcement in a Financial Times op-ed and then in a Sunday evening post on X declaring that “at dawn begins an economic D-Day.” The measures target Iranian oil, shipping and financial channels, and — critically — third countries. “Any nation that serves as a financial artery of a withering regime should expect to share in its isolation,” Bessent said, language aimed at India, China, Turkey and the Gulf states.

The escalation follows the expiry of the 60-day window set by the June memorandum of understanding, which both sides have now abandoned. The unresolved question is control of the Strait of Hormuz. Iranian security officials warned that ships violating Tehran’s transit rules face detention or confiscation, and threatened to halt oil flows entirely if neighbouring states join the sanctions push.

Key facts:

  • UK Navy data cited by CBS show commercial shipping through the Strait fell to 4 percent of pre-war levels by early August — only 21 inbound and 18 outbound transits in the week ending August 7.
  • The US military reportedly assisted in moving over 15 million barrels of oil through the Strait in a single day.
  • Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian defended the June accord: “There is not a single provision in this agreement that amounts to capitulation,” and claimed Tehran negotiates from a “position of power.”
  • Independent casualty estimates for Iran range between roughly 3,500 and 11,300 dead, with verification crippled by internet throttling and the succession crisis following the killing of Ali Khamenei in the February strikes.
  • Al Jazeera reports at least 145 countries have registered fuel price increases traceable to the war.
  • Oil fell on the day — WTI to about $85.65 (-1.62%), Brent to $93.09 (-1.38%) — as traders awaited the details.

Bessent’s “choose sides” framing is a demand that the entire world subordinate its trade to American war aims — the same logic being applied simultaneously to Canada.

2. Canada declares itself at war with the US over trade

Prime Minister Mark Carney told reporters in Ottawa that Canada is “at war” with the United States after Trump used Section 338 of the 1930 Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act to impose 50 percent tariffs on roughly $20 billion in annual Canadian imports. It is the first use of Section 338 since the statute was written 96 years ago. “You’re at war when you get attacked,” Carney said, pledging to match Washington “dollar for dollar in order to protect Canadian workers, farmers, families and businesses.”

Ottawa will apply counter-tariffs on $20 billion of US products on September 8, concentrated in steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper and electronics. Negotiations have not resumed; US officials say talks have not begun and there is no schedule for them.

Key facts:

  • Section 338 permits the president to impose duties of up to 50 percent on countries deemed to discriminate against US commerce, bypassing the investigative procedures required under Sections 232 and 301.
  • The measure lands on an economy in which roughly three-quarters of exports go to the United States, with auto, steel and forestry towns most exposed.
  • Writing on the WSWS, Nick Beams argued the conflict demonstrates that “the objective contradiction between world economy and the nation-state system” is now tearing apart even the most integrated capitalist partnership on earth.

Both governments are preparing to make workers pay: Washington through consumer price increases and Ottawa through the “support packages” that invariably arrive attached to concession demands.

3. Nordic and Baltic leaders converge on Kyiv as Russian strikes hit wartime peak

The leaders of the eight Nordic-Baltic states — Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, Latvia, Lithuania, Norway and Sweden — assembled in Kyiv for an NB8 summit with Volodymyr Zelensky, as Russian long-range strikes kill civilians at the highest rate since the opening months of the 2022 invasion and Ukrainian interceptor stocks run low.

Zelensky held six bilateral meetings on the margins. The summit communiqué prioritized a European anti-ballistic capability, accelerated production of interceptors and anti-drone systems, energy-grid repair before winter, and financing mechanisms including the frozen Russian assets question. Zelensky told the leaders Ukraine needs more than 300 additional US Patriot interceptors to survive the winter air campaign, and warned that Moscow intends to mobilize a further 300,000 troops after its elections.

Key facts:

  • Ukraine’s energy infrastructure has again been targeted systematically ahead of the heating season, with the summit devoting a full session to grid resilience.
  • The NB8 states have become, per capita, the largest military donors to Kyiv; the summit also addressed Ukraine’s EU accession track.
  • The four-year war has produced no negotiated framework; the Trump administration’s efforts, run in part through Jared Kushner, have stalled.

The spectacle of eight heads of government flying into a war zone to promise more missiles underscores that European rearmament — not any peace initiative — is the operative policy. The pension and social cuts being prepared in Paris, Berlin and London are the domestic side of the same ledger.

4. Congo Ebola epidemic becomes deadliest in the country’s history

The Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has become both the largest and the deadliest in the country’s history, and is expanding faster than any Ebola epidemic ever recorded. WHO data as of August 20 put the toll at 5,290 confirmed cases and 2,516 deaths, a case fatality rate approaching 48 percent. That surpasses the 2,299 deaths of the 2018-2020 North Kivu epidemic.

The outbreak began in the Mongbwalu health zone of Ituri province and has spread to 54 health zones across six provinces — Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu, Haut-Uélé, Tshopo and Bas-Uélé. Ituri accounts for 4,447 cases and 1,984 deaths across 28 of its 36 health zones.

Key facts:

  • The epidemic is caused by Bundibugyo ebolavirus, not Zaire ebolavirus. The Ervebo vaccine and the licensed monoclonal antibody treatments (Inmazeb, Ebanga) are certified only against the Zaire species, leaving responders without a proven vaccine or therapeutic.
  • Case growth accelerated sharply through August: from 4,665 cases and 2,184 deaths on August 12 to 5,290 and 2,516 by August 20 — over 600 new cases and 330 deaths in eight days.
  • The response is unfolding in eastern provinces wracked by armed conflict and mass displacement, with health-worker infections and community resistance to burial teams repeatedly reported by MSF.
  • Uganda has recorded linked cases, prompting ECDC monitoring.

The scale of this catastrophe — an epidemic several times larger than the 2014-16 West African outbreak in case-fatality terms — has drawn a fraction of the international coverage devoted to the Iran war, and no emergency global funding mobilization comparable to the sums being spent on interceptors.

5. ICE arrests roughly 300 Haitians in Ohio after TPS expiration

Immigration and Customs Enforcement has begun mass “find-and-arrest” operations against Haitian nationals across Ohio following the July expiration of Temporary Protected Status. A federal source told reporters roughly 300 Haitians have been arrested in the state in recent days; 17 have been detained in Springfield itself, a city of some 12,000 Haitian residents. Springfield Mayor Rob Rue confirmed ICE told his office it is conducting field enforcement in the city, across Ohio and nationwide.

The Haitian community has largely stopped leaving home. Churches report collapsed attendance; employers in Springfield’s logistics and manufacturing plants — which recruited Haitian workers directly — report absenteeism.

Key facts:

  • Approximately 350,000 Haitians nationally face loss of TPS protection.
  • Jacob Crosse documented on the WSWS the arrest of Pastor Gilbert Joubert Adrien and the detention of a five-year-old boy in Texas.
  • An estimated 205,000 children have experienced a parent’s immigration detention since January 2025.
  • Deportations are proceeding to a country the US State Department itself rates Level 4 “Do Not Travel,” with Port-au-Prince under armed group control.
  • Springfield was the target of Trump and Vance’s 2024 “eating the pets” campaign; the current operation is the follow-through.

Crosse’s article stresses “the bipartisan foundations of the mass deportation apparatus” — the detention infrastructure now being used was built and expanded under Obama and Biden.

6. SNAP rolls fall by 5.6 million as work requirements bite

Enrollment in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program has fallen by more than 13 percent in twelve months — from 42.2 million recipients in May 2025 to 36.6 million in May 2026 — a decline far steeper than the Congressional Budget Office projected when the “big beautiful bill” was passed. That is 5.6 million people removed from federal food assistance.

The bill imposed an 80-hour-per-month work requirement and stripped exemptions from veterans, homeless people, young adults who aged out of foster care, and adults aged 55 to 64.

Key facts:

  • Arizona recorded the sharpest drop: enrollment fell 55 percent from April 2025 to April 2026, more than 400,000 people, which the state attributed largely to its own administrative failure implementing the federal changes amid unprecedented call volumes.
  • Advocates report large numbers of people who remain eligible are being cut for missed paperwork deadlines or inability to produce documentation.
  • Food bank networks report demand rising in direct proportion to the enrollment declines.
  • The savings are being channelled into the tax cuts and the military budget.

The administration and its press allies are presenting the collapse as a fraud-reduction success. It is a deliberate transfer: the destruction of the food assistance rolls at the same moment the Treasury is unveiling a war-financing sanctions regime.

The New York Times revealed a previously undisclosed private meeting between House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries and Jared Kushner, held “in a private space in New York City, offered up by a mutual friend of the two men.” The Times reported the discussion touched on housing and immigration, and framed the meeting as Trump’s circle recognizing “the likelihood of a Democratic-led House” and “trying to foster as much of a working relationship as possible before any change.”

Key facts:

  • Martin documents that Democratic congressional candidates drawn from military and intelligence backgrounds have roughly doubled — from about 30 in 2018 to at least 60 in 2026 — including former admirals, generals and intelligence operatives.
  • The likely bargain: Democratic votes for Republican budget/austerity priorities in exchange for continued or expanded Ukraine military assistance.
  • Martin’s conclusion: whichever party prevails in November, the working class faces “a government committed to war abroad and austerity at home.”

Hyundai workers stage first full strike in a decade

Roughly 40,000 members of Hyundai Motor’s South Korean union struck for a full day on August 21 — the first complete walkout in ten years — idling the Ulsan, Asan and Jeonju plants across both eight-hour shifts. Workers from Hyundai, Kia and supplier firms rallied outside Hyundai headquarters in Seoul in red headbands. Central demands are a higher retirement age (currently 60), job protections against AI and automation, and permission for parts suppliers to raise prices.

The union then agreed to suspend the strike scheduled for August 24 and return to a 17th round of negotiations at Ulsan.

  • The strike vote passed with 92.03 percent support; partial walkouts since July total 120 hours and over 2.3 trillion won ($1.66 billion) in lost production.
  • Real wages have fallen 1 to 1.4 percent against 2.6 percent inflation.
  • Kia and POSCO workers have also passed strike votes.
  • Ben McGrath argues on the WSWS that the Korean Metal Workers’ Union is deliberately rationing the action and preventing coordination between plants, and calls for rank-and-file committees independent of the KMWU apparatus.

French government moves to break the link between pensions and inflation

The French Finance Ministry, under Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, is preparing to sever the indexation of pensions to inflation — the mechanism that has protected retirees’ purchasing power for decades. Official estimates put the saving at €6 to €12 billion in 2027 alone. A “hybrid” scenario is under study: a total freeze for the highest pensions, an intermediate rate for median pensions, and full revaluation only for the smallest.

Lecornu’s government attempted a straight pension freeze in the 2026 budget to raise €3.6 billion; parliament rejected it. The government has since rammed the 2026 budget through without a parliamentary vote.

  • Alexandre Lantier calculates on the WSWS that sustained de-indexation could cut pensioners’ purchasing power by as much as 43 percent over a decade.
  • French military spending is being taken from €36 billion to €70 billion; billionaire wealth has risen 255 percent to €1.23 trillion under Macron.
  • Lantier’s conclusion is that neither the union confederations nor the New Popular Front will mount serious resistance, and calls for struggle toward a Socialist United States of Europe.

Serbia turns to Russia for firefighting aircraft as EU resources run dry

Serbia has been fighting wildfires for nearly a month — 20 active fires, with the Deliblato Sands nature reserve burning since late July — without activating the EU Civil Protection Mechanism. Instead Belgrade requested and received a Russian Emercom Il-76, which can drop 42 tonnes of water per pass. Interior Minister Ivica Dačić confirmed its arrival. There is currently no aircraft of comparable capacity available within the EU mechanism, whose resources have been exhausted by fires across Spain, Portugal, Greece and the Balkans.

The European Commission confirmed Serbia never activated the mechanism, while noting it had supplied Copernicus satellite imagery on request. EU officials have publicly questioned Belgrade’s turn to Moscow — an accession-candidate state accepting Russian emergency assistance during the Ukraine war.

Serbia itself sent aircraft and crews to Spain earlier this summer. The episode is a concrete measure of how far climate-driven fire seasons have outrun European civil-protection capacity, at a moment when the same governments are redirecting budgets into rearmament.

Landfill collapse kills 30 in Conakry

A mountain of refuse at Dar Es Salam, the largest open-air dump in Guinea’s capital, collapsed in the early hours of Sunday after heavy rains beginning around 2 a.m., burying the shacks built at its base. Thirty people were killed and at least six seriously injured; several homes were destroyed and search operations continued through Monday.

Lt. Col. Baldé Mamadou Bailo, sanitation officer for the military engineering battalion, said the site had been designated for closure on that very day.

  • Dar Es Salam has grown for decades without engineering controls, drainage or slope stabilization, while informal settlements of waste-pickers expanded directly against it.
  • The disaster follows the same pattern as the 2017 Koshe collapse in Addis Ababa (115 dead) and the 2000 Payatas collapse in Manila (over 200 dead) — the recurring product of urban poverty forced to live on the waste of capitalist production.

Supreme Court’s Voting Rights Act ruling reshapes the midterms

The Supreme Court’s decision in Louisiana v. Callais, written by Justice Samuel Alito, effectively nullified the Voting Rights Act provision requiring states to draw maps giving racial minority voters an opportunity to elect candidates of their choice, treating majority-minority districts as unconstitutional racial gerrymanders. Democrats estimate the ruling could yield Republicans as many as 19 additional House seats.

  • Nine states — Alabama, California, Florida, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, Tennessee, Texas and Utah — have redrawn congressional maps since last year.
  • Within an hour of the decision, the Republican-controlled Florida House approved an aggressively gerrymandered map projected to net four additional Republican seats.
  • The Court has been criticized, including by its own dissenters, for intervening in election law while the 2026 cycle is already underway — a departure from its own repeated admonitions to lower courts.

The dismantling of the central enforcement provision of the 1965 Act, sixty-one years on, is being carried through by judicial fiat with no significant mobilization from the Democratic Party beyond fundraising appeals.

Mamdani sues the City Council to block $10,000 payments to school aides

Mayor Zohran Mamdani has filed suit against the New York City Council to block the RESPECT Check Act, passed unanimously on July 16, which would provide payments of up to $10,000 during the 2026-27 school year to approximately 26,000 paraprofessionals at an estimated cost of $244 million. The administration’s argument rests on the 1967 Taylor Law: by legislating a “workforce stabilization” bonus directly, the Council allegedly bypassed mandatory collective bargaining.

  • Paraprofessionals — overwhelmingly women, disproportionately Black and Latina — earn between $33,200 and $56,726 a year against a median NYC rent of $3,707 a month.
  • Council defenders cite a 2023 state appeals court ruling that additional payments independent of regular salary need not be collectively bargained, and Court of Appeals findings that the Taylor Law does not bar a municipality from unilaterally granting an economic benefit.
  • The Taylor Law is the statute that bans strikes by New York public employees and imposes two-days’-pay penalties for each day on strike.

Jesse Thomas and Barry Grey note on the WSWS the significance of a self-described democratic socialist mayor invoking the state’s principal anti-strike law against the lowest-paid workers in the school system.

Nancy Kassebaum Baker dies at 94

Nancy Landon Kassebaum Baker, Republican senator from Kansas from 1978 to 1997, died Friday, August 21 at 94. She was the first woman elected to the US Senate without having succeeded a husband in office, and the daughter of Alf Landon, the 1936 Republican presidential nominee crushed by Roosevelt. She co-authored the 1996 Kassebaum-Kennedy health insurance portability act and broke with her party over sanctions on apartheid South Africa. Her career belongs to a vanished period of the Republican Party — one whose disappearance is itself a measure of the collapse of bourgeois liberalism.

COVID surveillance in dispute

A significant methodological conflict has opened up in US COVID surveillance. As of August 15, the CDC’s wastewater dashboard rated national activity “very low.” Stanford’s WastewaterSCAN program, measuring the same period, rates SARS-CoV-2 activity “high” nationwide with a significant upward trend over 21 days, and reports August concentrations up 106 percent over July 2026. The divergence stems from the CDC’s rebasing of its wastewater scale, which has the effect of making a rising wave appear routine.

Detections are present in all 50 states, with Texas, Mississippi and California showing elevated activity even on CDC’s own scale. Two Omicron descendants drive the wave: NB.1.8.1 (“Nimbus”), which reached an estimated 43 percent of sequenced US cases by late June, and BA.3.2 (“Cicada”), circulating in wastewater in more than 30 states and accounting for roughly 15 percent of current cases.

US measles at a 35-year high

CDC data as of August 20 record 2,777 confirmed measles cases in 2026 — the highest annual total since 1991, already well past the 2,289 recorded for all of 2025. There have been 36 new outbreaks this year, and 94 percent of cases (2,614 of 2,777) are outbreak-associated, reported across 47 jurisdictions. The Pan American Health Organization is expected to review US transmission data later this year, with the loss of the elimination status held since 2000 now the likely outcome.

Major studies

  • The Lancet published the Phase 3 HOPE-3 results for Capricor Therapeutics’ cell therapy deramiocel in Duchenne muscular dystrophy: the trial met its primary endpoint, slowing decline in upper-limb function by 54 percent versus placebo — the first positive Phase 3 in this indication in years.
  • The Lancet also reported that upadacitinib 15 mg daily produces clinically meaningful repigmentation of face and total body in adults and adolescents with non-segmental vitiligo, with no new safety signals.
  • The Lancet Public Health published the 2026 Europe report of the Lancet Countdown on health and climate change, subtitled “narrowing window for decisive health action” — documenting rising heat mortality, expanding vector ranges and the health costs of continued fossil fuel dependence across the continent.
  • A related modelling study finds that once age-stratified vulnerability is accounted for, intolerable heat will be experienced at far larger scales and for longer durations than previously estimated — with direct implications for heat action planning in the very countries now cutting civil protection budgets.

Technology and AI policy

  • The EU AI Act crossed into active enforcement on August 2: fines are now enforceable, chatbot disclosure requirements are live, and the EU AI Office can formally open investigations into non-compliant organizations.
  • Representatives Jay Obernolte (R-CA) and Lori Trahan (D-MA) released a 269-page bipartisan discussion draft of the Great American Artificial Intelligence Act of 2026, organized into four titles: Frontier AI Governance, Workforce, Cybersecurity, and Research/Development/International Cooperation. Arts and culture

The 83rd Venice International Film Festival opens September 2 with a lineup announced in the past days that is unusually loaded with films about power and money. Danny Boyle’s Ink, a dramatization of Rupert Murdoch’s acquisition of the Sun and the birth of the modern tabloid, opens the festival in competition — a subject with obvious contemporary charge as Murdoch’s outlets function as direct instruments of the Trump administration. Twenty films compete for the Golden Lion, with Maggie Gyllenhaal presiding over the jury.

The most anticipated entry is Lee Chang-dong’s Possible Love, his first feature since Burning (2018). Lee is among the very few contemporary filmmakers whose work treats South Korean class society with genuine seriousness — Poetry, Secret Sunshine and Peppermint Candy are studies in how ordinary people are broken by social forces they cannot name. His return arrives at a moment when 40,000 Hyundai workers are striking over automation. Hirokazu Koreeda brings Look Back, an adaptation of Tatsuki Fujimoto’s manga about two young artists and the violent interruption of creative life; Koreeda’s persistent theme — improvised families formed against the failures of the state — continues to make him one of the most humane filmmakers working. Also in competition: Martin McDonagh’s black comedy Wild Horse Nine and Casey Affleck’s directorial effort Company. Giovanni Veronesi’s historical drama Dio Ride closes out of competition.

Political conditioning of arts funding in the US continues to intensify. The National Endowment for the Arts has terminated grants on explicitly political grounds, informing recipients that their projects fall “outside” the focus on funding “projects that reflect the nation’s rich artistic heritage and creativity as prioritized by the President.” Among the cancellations documented by Artnet: a $50,000 grant to Mass MoCA supporting an exhibition by Jeffrey Gibson, and a $30,000 grant to the Contemporary Arts Museum Houston for Mary Ellen Carroll: How To Talk Dirty and Influence People. The formula makes the criterion explicit — presidential preference as the standard for public arts funding, a mechanism with obvious precedents.

The financial squeeze runs parallel. The Philadelphia Museum of Art faces an operating deficit of roughly $10 million, with new leadership pursuing a “turnaround” that in practice means staffing reductions at an institution whose workers unionized in 2020 and struck in 2022.

In Britain, workers at the Victoria and Albert Museum have voted for strike action, exposing — as Paul Mitchell wrote on the WSWS — the poverty wages and casualization underpinning what the government markets as a “world class” cultural sector. The V&A vote and the Philadelphia deficit are the same phenomenon: cultural institutions maintained as prestige assets while the labour that runs them is driven below subsistence.

Deaths. Reggae producer and historian Steve Barrow died at 80. Co-founder of the Blood and Fire label, Barrow did more than almost anyone to rescue Jamaican dub and roots recordings from commercial oblivion, reissuing King Tubby, Augustus Pablo and Yabby You with scholarly annotation; his Rough Guide to Reggae remains the standard reference. Rick Adelman, the Hall of Fame basketball coach, died at 79. Dorita Burgos, the Argentine actress whose career spanned the Perón years, the dictatorship and the democratic period, died at 90.

This week in history: August 24-30

2001 — Assassination of Abu Ali Mustafa. Israeli Apache helicopters fired two US-supplied missiles into the Ramallah office of the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine’s general secretary, killing him at his desk. Mustafa had returned to the West Bank only in 1999 after 32 years in exile. It was the first Israeli assassination of a top-tier Palestinian political leader in the Occupied Territories and marked the open adoption of targeted killing as declared state policy — with Washington’s acquiescence establishing the precedent for the drone assassination programmes of the following two decades.

1976 — The Soweto general strike. Following the June massacre of schoolchildren protesting Afrikaans-language instruction, a general strike called by student organizations mobilized an estimated 70 to 80 percent of Black workers in the Johannesburg area. The strike demonstrated that the apartheid regime’s vulnerability lay in the industrial working class rather than in student protest alone — the lesson the ANC leadership would subsequently work to contain within a nationalist framework that preserved capitalist property relations after 1994.

1951 — The US-Philippines Mutual Defense Treaty. Signed in Washington, the treaty bound Manila to American strategic direction and preserved the vast Clark and Subic Bay installations. It was one of a series of bilateral pacts — with Japan, South Korea, Australia and New Zealand — through which Washington constructed the Pacific architecture of the Cold War. The same treaty is today the legal basis for the Enhanced Defense Cooperation Agreement sites facing the South China Sea.

1926 — Piłsudski’s 55 decrees. Three months after his May coup, Józef Piłsudski issued 55 decrees in a single act, gutting the powers of the Sejm and establishing the Sanacja regime’s personal rule. The Polish parliamentary system, barely eight years old, was dismantled without formal abolition — an early model of the constitutional erosion that would be repeated across interwar Europe, and a warning the WSWS draws on directly in analyzing the present.
