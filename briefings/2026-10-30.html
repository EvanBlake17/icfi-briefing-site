1. Spain’s second high-speed rail disaster this year kills 69 near Urda

The death toll from Wednesday’s crash near Urda, in Toledo province, has reached 69, and about 110 people were injured. At 8:54 a.m. on October 28, a Renfe AVE high-speed train hit a stationary Iryo train on the Madrid–Andalucía line. A collision on the same line at Adamuz nine months ago killed 46. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez declared three days of national mourning, and The Independent reports that a former minister is among the dead.

The official explanation is “human error.” Transport Minister Óscar Puente says a computer failure at 8:30 a.m. disabled the LZB automatic train-protection system on a 63 km stretch. At 8:36 a.m., according to the government’s account, an Adif control operator in Madrid told the AVE driver to resume normal running despite a red signal. NBC has reported on audio recordings of those exchanges, and ABC News quoted an official describing the order as “inexplicable.” The crash comes before snap national elections on November 29, and the BBC describes “sorrow and outrage in politically divided Spain.” Rail unions announced strikes immediately after the crash. In today’s Perspective, Alex Lantier calls the “human error” explanation a fraud that shifts blame for the systemic degradation of a liberalised rail network onto individual workers.

Key facts: - One Spanish outlet, El Constitucional, reported 74 dead. Reuters, AP and most other outlets give 69. - Families’ associations issued a joint statement: “It has happened again, and it will happen again.” - King Felipe and Queen Letizia visited survivors in hospital in Toledo. Pope Leo sent condolences. - AP says the disaster forces Spain to “reckon” with a second deadly crash of the year, weeks before the election.

2. Israeli election ends in deadlock as Likud claims the vote was “stolen”

With more than 99% of ballots counted, neither Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc nor the opposition has the 61 seats needed for a majority in the 120-seat Knesset. Haaretz figures cited by Al Jazeera give Netanyahu’s bloc 53 seats, the opposition led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot (Yashar) 48, and Arab-led lists a record 19. Ra’am is projected to win eight of those. Turnout was reportedly the highest in 50 years. The Central Elections Committee must certify the results by Wednesday, and the new Knesset is due to be sworn in on November 10.

Likud is claiming without evidence that Arab-led parties “stole” the election, possibly with foreign help. Reuters reports that Netanyahu, facing the loss of power, has “stepped up attacks on Israel’s Arab parties.” Election committee chair Noam Sohlberg said a letter had raised “serious suspicions” of foreign interference. He could not verify them and passed them to the Shin Bet. Eisenkot said that “the days of Netanyahu using state institutions against Israeli interests for his personal needs are over.” He has also pledged not to govern with Palestinian Israeli parties. That leaves him without a path to 61 unless he breaks that pledge or wins defections.

Key facts: - Yair Golan (Democrats) is the only opposition leader open to a coalition with Arab-led parties. He met Ra’am leader Mansour Abbas and said it is “a time for action.” - Eisenkot met Naftali Bennett (Together) to discuss forming a government quickly. Analysts suggest the opposition might try to replace the Knesset speaker, who is close to Netanyahu. - CNBC expects “weeks of coalition haggling.” - Netanyahu’s claim of fraud mirrors Trump’s in 2020 and Flávio Bolsonaro’s this week (see Brazil below). For the opposition, the dispute is over who leads the genocidal state, not whether it continues.

3. Dockworkers strike against arms shipments at more than 100 ports today

Port workers on five continents are striking, demonstrating or holding solidarity actions today, Friday, October 30, against the movement of weapons and troops through their docks. Reuters reports action at “more than 100 ports,” and the organisers count 113 ports in 22 countries and regions. Unions in Greece, the Basque Country, Turkey, Morocco, Italy and France initiated the action, and the ILWU Local 10 in San Francisco and Oakland and ver.di in Hamburg have announced support. Ports named include Marseille, Genoa, Piraeus, Hamburg, Tangier, Mumbai and Oakland. The action builds on a mobilisation on February 6 and is much larger.

The demands combine opposition to the genocide in Gaza and the US-Israeli war on Iran with opposition to rearmament. They also include higher wages, job security, shorter hours and protection from job losses to automation. Mother Jones estimates that a global port stoppage could freeze about $2 billion in freight. The Socialist Equality Parties in Europe issued a statement backing the strike (see WSWS coverage below). It notes that the joint union declaration “does not even mention Ukraine.”

Key facts: - Slogan: “Dockers do not work for war.” - Several shipping-industry outlets put the total at 111 to 113 ports in 22 countries. - In Italy, the action coincides with wider strikes. Livorno dockworkers have been trying to block arms shipments linked to the Gaza flotilla. - The strike comes as governments in France, Italy and Germany step up repression of anti-war and social protests.

4. Iran war costs top $47 billion as mines strike tankers in Hormuz

The Pentagon told lawmakers that the eight-month war on Iran had cost at least $47.1 billion as of October 16. That figure excludes base repairs and fuel. About $29 billion of it is projected for replacing munitions, and the Congressional Budget Office estimates continuing costs of $2–3 billion a month. The Pentagon acknowledges 18 US deaths, while the Washington Post, citing six unnamed officials, reports at least 22. It lists 865 service members as wounded.

Iran has kept the Strait of Hormuz closed since early March. About a fifth of the world’s oil and LNG normally passes through it. Today the IRGC navy said three tankers caught fire after hitting mines in the strait and a fourth vessel struck mines in its eastern section. Names, flags and casualties have not been reported. On the diplomatic side, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi says Tehran is in “close and ongoing contact” with mediators over a seven-day plan to reopen the strait. Trump says he approved Vice President Vance’s outreach on Iran’s need for medical supplies but denies directing him to restart talks. Al Jazeera reports that the White House has said the US will not attack Iran before the November 3 midterms. Military policy is being timed to the election calendar.

Key facts: - AAA puts the average price of US diesel at $6.35 a gallon, against $3.75 a year ago. - Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer said: “For eight months, American families have been buried by the heavy cost of Trump’s disastrous war in Iran.” Democrats are attacking the war’s cost, not its existence. - Iran, Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan met in Tehran on Monday and agreed to keep talking. Days later the Houthis claimed six strikes inside Saudi Arabia (see International).

5. Markets deliver an austerity ultimatum to Lula as Bolsonaro’s son flees to Miami

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva of the Workers Party (PT) won Sunday’s runoff by more than four million votes over Senator Flávio Bolsonaro of the Liberal Party. Financial markets responded with a selloff. The real fell about 12% against the dollar in spot trading, and Bloomberg describes investors “seeking bottom after Brazil rout.” Valor reports a “tug-of-war over fiscal policy” inside the incoming government. The Economist warned that if Lula does not change course, “his golden victory will turn to ashes.” The Financial Times wrote that investors will force his hand if he does not rein in spending.

Flávio Bolsonaro, under investigation for embezzlement and money laundering, had been ordered by the Supreme Federal Court to surrender his passports. He left Brazil overland with his wife and two daughters for Argentina, then flew via Ecuador to Miami, landing on Wednesday. He says he will lead the opposition from the US. The New York Times reports that the Trump administration helped the family leave and that officials expect to grant them permanent residency. Trump said he had not taken sides but called the election “a disgrace” and “a great embarrassment” to Brazilians, pointing to the market fall. The Liberal Party has accepted the result.

Key facts: - The US and Brazil have an extradition treaty, but it is unclear whether Brasília will seek extradition or whether Washington would comply. - Names floated for finance minister include Vice President Geraldo Alckmin and Piauí governor Rafael Fonteles. The outgoing finance team has signalled “fiscal adjustment.” - In his victory speech, Lula said Congress “was chosen by the people” and promised to work with it.

6. Russian drone strike on Dnipro station kills 14 as Ukraine sends 613 drones toward Moscow

Russian attacks killed at least 19 people across Ukraine on Thursday. Fourteen of them died on a platform at Dnipro’s main railway station. Ukrzaliznytsia said a jet-powered drone hit as a train was preparing to leave for Zaporizhzhia. Governor Oleksandr Hanzha said at least 14 others were injured, six critically, including an 11-year-old. Two teenagers were killed in Chernihiv and three people in Kremenchuk. Al Jazeera’s correspondent describes a rising trend of Russian strikes on railway stations and other transport infrastructure.

Ukraine launched one of its largest drone attacks on the Russian capital. TASS said most of 613 drones sent toward Moscow were downed. Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said 231 were destroyed on approach, and the Defence Ministry claimed 1,214 drones intercepted across Russia overnight. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called it “very strange” for Kyiv to target Moscow while more than 22 African leaders were in the city for a summit.

Key facts: - Zelenskyy called the attack “horrific” and asked how many “terrorist strikes” Russia had carried out since the US eased sanctions on some Russian oligarchs and the energy sector. - A six-day local ceasefire around Oleshky is reportedly holding. - Trump’s October 11 “energy ceasefire” has collapsed. Ukraine resumed strikes on refineries, and Zelenskyy says Russia hit Ukrainian energy targets 76 times in the following week. The next round of US–Russia–Ukraine talks excludes the European powers.

7. France mobilises 70,000 police for Halloween cost-of-living protests

France is preparing for another national day of protest tomorrow, Saturday, October 31. The yellow vest movement and student unions called it in what they describe as the “third act” of a revolt against the rising cost of living. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez warned of a “very high risk of public disorder.” He is deploying about 70,000 police and gendarmes, the same “exceptional” level as last week, with armoured vehicles and water cannon. Last Saturday’s protests drew about 410,000 people by the ministry’s count and roughly twice that by the unions’. The ministry says more than 2,700 people were arrested that day. The SEP statement on the dockworkers’ strike puts total arrests during the protest wave at more than 10,000.

Demonstrations will be permitted but kept out of city centres. Nuñez accused the “ultra-left” and some yellow vest activists of promoting Halloween evening gatherings calling for a “revolution” and said those gatherings will be banned. He also warned protesters against using Halloween masks to hide their faces. Le Monde reports that lawyers are fighting the government in court over curfews imposed after earlier protests, and the prime minister has promised more of them. The Senate passed a bill this week making protesters pay for damage caused during demonstrations.

Key facts: - Far-right groups clashed with left-wing protesters at rallies in about 15 cities. Nuñez promised an “extremely firm” response and said, “We will tolerate no excesses.” - UN experts have raised concerns about excessive force against students. - Protesters plan to gather outside the National Assembly and town halls across the country. - Last week the Louvre, Musée d’Orsay, Picasso Museum and Grand Palais closed for the Saturday protests.

Houthis claim six strikes on Saudi oil and airports as 800,000 remain trapped in Taiz

Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree claimed missile and drone attacks on the Ras Tanura refinery, the Aramco facility at Abqaiq, King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, King Fahd International Airport in Dammam, the Rabigh refinery and a military headquarters in Najran. The claims have not been independently confirmed. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted two ballistic missiles fired at Riyadh. Shrapnel wounded one person and damaged a house and a car. Today the coalition said it downed a drone heading for the Mecca region. The Houthis denied launching it and said they target only “oil facilities and military bases.” Saudi Arabia’s foreign ministry called for negotiations to prevent further escalation.

The civil war has resumed in full. Fighting broke out again in July and escalated after the Houthis seized Yemen’s Red Sea coast last month. Houthi media report more than 100 Saudi-led air strikes on Sanaa and eight other governorates in 24 hours, and Houthi forces are advancing on Jabal Sabr, the mountain above Taiz.

  • OCHA says more than 281,000 people have been displaced since early August and that “time is running out” to reach 800,000 people trapped in Taiz. Aid now reaches the city only by narrow mountain routes.
  • Human Rights Watch said the Houthi siege of Taiz may amount to the war crime of using starvation as a method of warfare.
  • Yemen’s internationally recognised government asked the UN Security Council for “deterrent and punitive measures” over Houthi attacks on Aden airport. Flights there have resumed.

Merz tours East Asia and joins a Taiwan war-game exercise

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has visited Singapore, Seoul and Tokyo with a delegation of about 30 business representatives, including the drone maker Quantum Systems and Airbus Defence & Space.   • In Singapore, Merz called for an interregional agreement with the members of the Trans-Pacific Partnership and a long-term EU–ASEAN trade deal.

Cleveland-Cliffs keeps Stelco as 500 layoffs proceed

The WSJ reports that Cleveland-Cliffs will keep Stelco despite a jobs dispute with Ottawa. Yahoo Finance Canada quotes the company’s CEO as saying he will “likely” sell eventually, while up to 500 layoffs at Hamilton and Nanticoke, Ontario, go ahead. Stelco employed about 3,000 people before the cuts. In a new letter, Industry Minister Mélanie Joly demanded that Stelco recall workers “immediately.” She said she would meet the owner only if the company presents a “concrete” plan to bring them back. Prime Minister Mark Carney said Cleveland-Cliffs must honour its 2024 commitment to keep unionised jobs for five years, and Ottawa has threatened legal action.

  • Stelco workers rallied in Hamilton on Thursday, telling the government that “actions speak louder than words” (CBC).
  • The NDP, USW Local 1005 and the Revolutionary Communist Party are calling for nationalisation. The WSWS takes up the political issues in a polemic today.

Mexican truckers’ 19-state strike ends with “dialogue tables”

On October 26–27, truck drivers and small transport operators blocked highways across Mexico over highway violence, extortion and tax changes. The interior ministry (Segob) counted 38 protest points in 19 states, from Nuevo Laredo to the port access at Cardel, Veracruz. Blockades near Cancún stranded travellers at the airport. The immediate trigger was the October 21 killing of AMOTAC delegate Estanislao Hernández Lozano in Puebla. After about 34 hours, the National Chamber of Freight Transport (CANACAR) and allied associations lifted the blockades in exchange for four working tables with federal agencies. These cover tax incentives, truck financing, drivers’ social security and security.

  • President Claudia Sheinbaum called it a tax dispute involving “a very small sector” and said: “What there cannot be is a defense of privileges.”
  • Under the agreement, the groups commit to “avoid new disruptions to the public and to free transit.”

AI spending fears hit tech stocks as Binance suffers record hack

Arts and culture

Patrick Stewart, 1940–2026

Sir Patrick Stewart has died in Los Angeles at 86. His spokesperson said he had Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia. He grew up in Mirfield, West Yorkshire, in a working-class home with no bathroom. His father, a former British Army regimental sergeant major, was violent toward his mother. Stewart joined an amateur drama group at 12 and won a scholarship to the Bristol Old Vic Theatre School. He then spent 14 years with the Royal Shakespeare Company, playing Mark Antony, Henry IV, Shylock and Oberon.

Most audiences knew him as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation (seven seasons, 178 episodes) and as Professor Charles Xavier in the X-Men films, ending with Logan (2017). The Picard role mattered beyond the series. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, when American culture celebrated the market and military triumphalism, the show gave tens of millions of viewers a commander who valued diplomacy, reason and science over force. It imagined a future without money or want, however idealised and contradictory that vision was. Stewart brought a Shakespearean actor’s seriousness to the part and gave it real dignity.

His stage work continued to the end. His 2007 Macbeth brought some of his best reviews and his only Tony nomination (“Nearly 50 years I’ve waited”), and he played Pinter’s No Man’s Land and Beckett’s Waiting for Godot opposite Ian McKellen. He wrote openly about the domestic violence of his childhood (“Violence is always a choice a man makes”) and was a patron of Refuge and an ambassador for the International Rescue Committee. He was knighted in 2010.
