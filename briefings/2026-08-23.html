1. Trump’s “economic D-Day” against Iran opens new phase of war

The US-Israeli war against Iran, now in its 176th day, has entered what Vice President Vance called a “new phase of economic pressure.” Trump has threatened an “economic D-Day,” vowing “tremendous economic consequences” for any country continuing to trade with Tehran. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent will hold a press conference Monday afternoon to unveil the measures, having promised sanctions “like have never been seen in the history of economic isolation on a country.” The announcement is designed as one half of a “one-two punch” alongside the continuing naval blockade of Iranian ports through the Strait of Hormuz — a blockade that has interdicted or redirected dozens of commercial vessels since it was reinstated on July 14.

Iranian officials responded with open defiance and an explicit doctrinal shift. Military spokesman Brig. Gen. Mohammad Akraminia stated that “our country’s military doctrine was defensive in the past, but gradually during these two wars, we witnessed the military doctrine moving from a defensive posture toward an offensive one.” Foreign Minister Araghchi dismissed the “economic D-Day” threat, and a foreign ministry spokesman called Trump’s demands “a recipe for an abysmal return to full-scale classic colonialism.” Security chief Mohsen Rezaei issued the sharpest warning: “If countries surrounding Iran join economic war, not a drop of oil will leave the Persian Gulf.” Iran formally notified its neighbors that joining the US campaign would render them “an enemy.”

Key facts:

  • Rice prices in Iran have risen 60 percent and beef 150 percent since the war began; the IMF projects inflation approaching 70 percent by year’s end and a contraction of more than 5 percent in GDP.
  • Kpler estimates Tehran holds roughly four months of export revenue in oil already outside the blockade; beyond that, oil income “could drop to zero.”
  • Parliament speaker and chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf: “The US put the security of all its allies at risk for the sake of Israel.”
  • Bessent, describing the strategy’s logic: “If maximum economic pressure, then likely no large-scale kinetic restart.” Trump nonetheless told a Friday rally that Iran could be bombed “a little bit more.”
  • Brent crude is near $94, on track for a second consecutive weekly gain of roughly 6 percent, driven principally by the measures against Iran.

2. US-Canada trade war becomes reality as 50 percent tariffs take effect

Trump’s 50 percent tariffs on approximately $28 billion in Canadian goods took effect at midnight Friday after Prime Minister Mark Carney suspended trade negotiations, blaming “last-minute changes in the U.S. proposed terms” that were “unfair, uneconomic, and called into question the reliability of any deal.” At a Saturday news conference Carney said the tariffs were “designed to hurt us and divide us” and called the American move “a miscalculation.”

Canada will retaliate dollar for dollar. Carney announced counter-tariffs effective September 8 targeting American steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics. The rupture comes alongside Trump’s separate threat of 100 percent tariffs on Canadian goods should Ottawa conclude a trade agreement with China — an explicit demand that Canada subordinate its trade policy to Washington’s confrontation with Beijing.

Key facts:

  • The tariffs are the highest bilateral rate imposed between the two economies since the 1930s.
  • A coalition of 25 US states is separately challenging the administration’s new 10–12.5 percent tariffs on goods from 60 trading partners.
  • Canadian auto, steel and aluminum communities in Ontario and Quebec — already hit by earlier rounds — face the immediate brunt, with the auto parts supply chain integrated across the border since 1965.
  • Carney’s “dollar-for-dollar” framing places the burden of retaliation on Canadian consumers through higher import prices, while offering no protection against layoffs.

3. Global bond market convulsions as US debt passes $40 trillion

The US gross national debt registered $40.047 trillion on August 18, and the bond market has not settled since. The 30-year Treasury yield rose to 5.34 percent that day — its highest level since 2007 — while the 10-year touched 4.74 percent, near the peak of Trump’s second term. Treasury Secretary Bessent responded with his second intervention of the month, doubling long-end buyback operations to a minimum of $4 billion per operation. Yields fell briefly, then rebounded, wiping out the decline.

The selloff is global, not American alone, driven by inflation expectations, deficits, and unprecedented competition from corporate debt issuance tied to the AI datacenter buildout. Every 25 basis points of increase in the weighted average rate on $40 trillion of outstanding debt adds roughly $100 billion to the annual interest burden. Interest is currently accruing at approximately $3.84 billion per day, with about $8 billion per day in net new borrowing. As Tom Hall wrote on the WSWS, the ruling class’s answer to the debt crisis will be “a frontal assault on the social position of the working class” — the destruction of Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid to guarantee the bondholders.

Key facts:

  • Fed Chair Powell used his final Jackson Hole address on August 22 to open the door wider to a September rate cut, unveiling the Fed’s revised policy consensus statement and noting the labor market is near maximum employment.
  • CNN, NPR, CBS and PBS all ran explainers during the week on what rising yields mean for mortgages, car loans and credit cards — an indication of how directly the crisis is transmitted to working-class households.
  • Corporate borrowing for AI infrastructure has pushed term premiums — the extra yield demanded to hold government paper — to multi-year highs.

4. Ebola in eastern Congo becomes deadliest outbreak in DRC history

The Bundibugyo ebolavirus epidemic in the Democratic Republic of the Congo is now the deadliest outbreak the country has ever recorded, the World Health Organization confirmed. As of data through August 19, the DRC has reported 5,290 confirmed cases and 2,516 deaths, surpassing the 2,299 deaths of the 2018–2020 North Kivu outbreak.

The epidemic began in the Mongbwalu health zone of Ituri province and has expanded over two months into five provinces — Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu, Haut-Uélé and Tshopo — now affecting 49 health zones. Ituri remains the epicenter with 4,447 cases and 1,984 deaths across 28 of its 36 health zones. WHO declared the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on May 17.

Key facts:

  • 80 percent of new cases fall outside contact-tracing lists, meaning the great majority of transmission chains are unknown — the clearest measure of surveillance collapse.
  • There is no licensed vaccine against Bundibugyo ebolavirus; the Ervebo vaccine that ended prior epidemics protects only against Zaire ebolavirus.
  • The outbreak is unfolding in precisely the provinces devastated by the M23 war, where health infrastructure has been shattered and millions are displaced.
  • Deaths passed 700 by mid-July — meaning the toll has more than tripled in five weeks.

7. Mass deportations to Haiti begin after termination of TPS

The first ICE deportation flight to Haiti since the Trump administration terminated Temporary Protected Status for more than 300,000 Haitians landed in Cap-Haïtien on August 20. The ICE Air flight originated in Alexandria, Louisiana. Haiti’s National Office of Migration had prepared for 175 people; 161 arrived. It was the eighth deportation flight to Haiti this year and the first since Washington warned Haitian authorities to expect a sharp increase.

US officials have told Port-au-Prince to prepare for twice-weekly flights returning roughly 250 Haitians per week. The removals are being carried out into a country in a state of near-total collapse: gang control of most of the capital, mass internal displacement, and a health and food system in ruins. Jacob Crosse wrote on the WSWS that the deportations amount to a policy of deliberately returning people to conditions of “social murder.”

Kazakhstan votes in snap parliamentary election

Kazakhstan held early parliamentary elections Sunday, with seven parties contesting all 145 seats by party list for five-year terms. Polls opened at 7 a.m. and closed at 8 p.m.; the Central Election Commission reported turnout at 17.96 percent as of 10 a.m. The pro-presidential Adilet party is widely expected to win an overwhelming majority, with several smaller parties loyal to President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev also entering parliament.

The vote follows Tokayev’s dissolution of the Mazhilis and is being presented as a further stage in his “reform” agenda. It comes four years after the January 2022 uprising of oil workers and youth in Zhanaozen and Almaty, crushed with the help of CSTO troops — a mass movement that remains the central unspoken reference point of Kazakh politics. The OSCE Office for Democratic Institutions and Human Rights deployed an observation mission.

DRC and M23 agree negotiation roadmap in Doha process

Representatives of the Democratic Republic of the Congo government and the Alliance Fleuve Congo/M23, together with Qatar, the United States, Togo (as African Union mediator), the AU Commission and Switzerland, met in Switzerland from August 17 to 21 and produced a roadmap of sequenced steps and timelines for negotiations under the Doha Framework Agreement. The parties agreed that the order in which protocols are negotiated does not dictate the order of implementation, and committed to a standardized method for reporting ceasefire violations.

The Expanded Joint Verification Mechanism Plus (EJVM+) has been operationalized with a secretariat established; a reconnaissance mission was conducted August 20 with MONUSCO logistical support, ahead of the first EJVM+ ceasefire verification mission scheduled for August 24 in Minembwe. The process, brokered by Washington and Doha, is inseparable from the struggle for control of Congolese cobalt, coltan and copper — the same eastern provinces now consumed by the Ebola epidemic.

Six EU states demand a windfall tax on oil profits

H5N1 spreads through Australian bird populations

Highly pathogenic H5N1 avian influenza is now spreading among Australian bird populations, as WSWS science writer Gary Alvernia reported. Australia had been the last inhabited continent free of the H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b panzootic that has killed hundreds of millions of birds and spread into mammals across every other continent, including Antarctica. Its arrival threatens endemic species with no evolved resistance and creates new reservoirs for reassortment.

In the United States, CDC influenza surveillance as of August 7 showed no indicators of unusual activity in people, including avian influenza A(H5). From 2024 through May 2026, 71 human cases of highly pathogenic avian influenza were identified in the US, concentrated among dairy and poultry workers, and the CDC’s public risk assessment remained “low.” That assessment rests on a surveillance apparatus that has been substantially defunded — a point of continuing WSWS criticism.

JWST finds early galaxies three to four times more massive than believed

Astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope have found that massive early galaxies contain far more small, faint stars than models predicted. The hidden low-mass population may make some of these galaxies three to four times more massive than previously estimated — a finding that compounds the existing tension between JWST observations of the early universe and standard models of galaxy assembly, which struggle to explain how so much mass accumulated so quickly after the Big Bang.

Separately, astronomers reported observations of stars repeatedly approaching supermassive black holes and surviving the encounters, producing repeating light bursts that unexpectedly dim with each return — a class of repeating partial tidal disruption events whose fading mechanism remains unexplained.

  • Multilingualism and brain aging: people speaking four languages showed brains appearing approximately 13 years younger than monolingual counterparts on imaging-based biological age measures — evidence for cognitive reserve, though the finding is confounded by the class distribution of multilingualism.
  • Alcohol withdrawal and relapse: mice showed more than double the activity in a brain region associated with stress and addiction following alcohol withdrawal, with the neural signature appearing before relapse behavior — a possible predictive biomarker.
  • Peppermint oil and hypertension: adults with mildly elevated blood pressure taking a small dose twice daily for 20 days saw systolic pressure fall by an average of 8.5 mmHg.
  • Exercise and weight: exercise proved substantially more effective at preventing weight regain than at producing initial weight loss, through muscle preservation and metabolic support — a direct rebuttal of the moralizing framework used to justify withholding obesity treatment.
  • Immunotherapy: a yeast-based food supplement restored immune capacity to fight cancer in mice, a low-cost candidate adjunct.
  • Greenland sharks: the 400-year-old species maintains healthy retinal tissue and specialized proteins for Arctic vision with no age-related decline — a model system for studying vertebrate longevity.
  • Photonics: freezing the liquid core of an optical fiber produced an environment in which light and sound interact more than 1,000 times more strongly than in ordinary fibers, with implications for sensing and signal processing.
  • Materials: Tokyo Metropolitan University researchers derived theoretical design rules for new thermoelectric materials, relevant to waste-heat recovery.

The politics of cultural memory. Two archaeological reports this week bear on how societies remember catastrophe. Excavators at Pompeii uncovered 15th- and 16th-century artifacts indicating that the buried city retained a continuous cultural presence long after Vesuvius, contradicting the assumption of total abandonment. In Budapest, a Wehrmacht motorcycle was recovered from the Danube containing the remains of two German soldiers — physical residue of the 1944–45 siege, surfacing in a country whose government has spent fifteen years rehabilitating the Horthy regime that made that siege possible.

Jacobin / DSA

“Working-Class Voters Are Winnable for the Left. Here’s How.” argues from polling that working-class voters are not hostile to progressive causes but prioritize material questions — housing, healthcare, jobs — over “culture war” and “defend democracy” framing. The empirical observation is accurate; the conclusion drawn is a communications strategy. Nothing in the piece questions the programmatic content of what is to be communicated, and the entire exercise is oriented toward improving Democratic electoral performance among workers rather than breaking them from the Democratic Party.

“For Socialists, Legislative Hardball Has Limits” warns DSA-aligned members of Congress against emulating House Freedom Caucus obstructionism, on grounds it would isolate socialists rather than build power. The article’s premise — that “socialists” operate as a faction within the Democratic congressional caucus, negotiating for leverage — is precisely the perspective that has produced the political collapse of the DSA “left” over Gaza, over the war budgets, and over every confrontation with the party leadership. The debate is over tactics inside a capitalist party.

Assessment: Jacobin’s output continues to treat the Democratic Party as the necessary terrain of socialist politics, with strategic disputes confined to how best to operate within it.

Left Voice

“U.S. Imperialism Is Still at an Impasse in Iran” opposes the US blockade as a failing project and refuses to lend support to either Washington or the Iranian bourgeois state — a formally correct position, and one that distinguishes Left Voice from the Stalinist-influenced tendencies. “Trump’s Fight Against Birthright Citizenship Seeks to Enshrine Unequal Rights and Deepen Working-Class Divisions” frames the attack as a ruling-class strategy to divide workers by citizenship status rather than as an abstract constitutional question. “Puerto Ricans Fight Back Against Water Rationing and Privatization amid Severe Drought” covers the water crisis without appeals to Democratic reform.

The limitation lies elsewhere: the Morenoite tradition of the PTS orients toward whatever movement is in motion — campus encampments, union caucuses, protest coalitions — without a struggle to build an independent revolutionary party against the existing apparatuses. Correct anti-imperialist formulations coexist with an organizational practice of adaptation.

Assessment: The strongest anti-war line of the tendencies surveyed, undermined by an orientation to spontaneous movements rather than the construction of a party.

Liberation News / PSL

“73 Years Without Peace: Why the U.S. is still rehearsing war in Korea” condemns US-South Korean exercises as imperialist provocation while favorably contrasting the South Korean president’s overtures to Pyongyang — the characteristic PSL substitution of preferred bourgeois diplomacy for independent working-class opposition to militarism on both sides of the DMZ. “Nuyoricans march for Puerto Rican independence amidst the water crisis” frames the struggle against the fiscal control board in terms of national self-determination rather than the common interests of Puerto Rican and mainland US workers against the same banks. “Pentagon’s scorn for sailors exposed amid USS Lincoln suicide attempts” is the strongest of the three, using a mental health crisis aboard a carrier on extended deployment to expose the treatment of enlisted personnel as disposable.

The IST’s method remains that of building the broadest possible protest coalitions — including with Labour lefts, Muslim community organizations and union officials — around single issues, rather than fighting for political independence of the working class.
