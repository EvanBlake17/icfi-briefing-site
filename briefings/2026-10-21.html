1. France extends curfews and deploys troops ahead of Saturday protests

After the first quiet night since the October 17 “yellow and youth” mobilization, Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu announced Tuesday that targeted nightly curfews would continue in central Paris, Lyon, Nice and several northern towns “for as long as necessary” (NPR). The government said it will deploy troops alongside police and mount a large security operation for new demonstrations on Saturday, October 24. It also admitted “failures” in its response to the weekend’s unrest (Detroit News/AP). Lecornu and Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez pushed intelligence briefings blaming Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s France Unbowed (LFI) for the violence. A suspect was arrested over the arson of Limoges town hall (BBC). On Monday a student blockade closed the Louvre (see Arts and culture).

In the bond market, French government yields rose Monday on concern that the protests will derail the deficit cut, while US Treasury yields fell (MarketWatch). Al Jazeera and CNN both ran explainers asking whether France’s debt crisis foreshadows America’s. In today’s Perspective, Nick Beams calls this a “feedback loop” in which borrowing for rearmament drives up interest rates, and those rates then force cuts to social spending. Alex Lantier wrote that the curfews, the invitation to the far-right National Rally to crisis meetings and Lecornu’s refusal to rule out a state of emergency show that “the French government prepares dictatorship.”

Key facts: - Curfews: Paris and Lyon city centers, Nice, and four northern cities with a two-night curfew. Lecornu said “targeted curfews” kept the peace overnight (China Daily). - Weekend toll: about 2,400 arrests, nearly 1,000 police injured and more than 1,500 shops looted (Outlook India, Fox Business). Later reports put total arrests above 2,500. - Repression: Lecornu has promised a “massive penal response” and said that “for now, curfews seem more suited to the situation” than a state of emergency. - Budget: Lecornu’s plan cuts €54 billion. Marine Le Pen has proposed €140 billion in cuts by 2032, which reportedly eased pressure from the bond market. - Unions and LFI: The CGT has put off national action until November 5. Mélenchon has floated price freezes and €10 billion for youth, citing the 1968 Grenelle Accords.

2. Witkoff flies to Putin after Oslo talks as Trump tells Zelensky to step aside

Speaking at the White House on Monday, Trump said “it’s time for somebody else to negotiate on behalf of Ukraine,” and added that “Zelensky is a man that should have had this war settled a long time ago.” Al Jazeera quoted him as saying “I don’t care how it’s done.” Zelensky met US envoy Steve Witkoff in Oslo, with Jared Kushner joining remotely. Zelensky said Ukraine has nearly run out of US-made Patriot interceptors, accused Washington of delaying deliveries, and offered a 30-day pause on strikes against Russian oil refineries in exchange for more. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the talks “positive” and told Zelensky nothing had been “deliberately denied” to Ukraine. Zelensky later reported “positive signals” and welcomed the restoration of US advance warning of Russian strikes.

Witkoff arrived in St. Petersburg on Wednesday to meet Putin. According to Axios, he will present a peace proposal. The New York Times (via Meduza) reports that the envoys will also seek the release of an American held in Russia. Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre said the talks will cover both European and US proposals.

Key facts: - Starlink: The Wall Street Journal reports that Elon Musk ordered Ukraine’s military-grade Starlink service throttled. It is disputed whether Trump or other officials were involved before the decision, but the two men discussed it afterwards. - Civilian toll: A Russian drone strike on a park in Kharkiv region killed six people, three of them children. Local official Yurii Yurchenko said, “This strike was aimed at civilians.” Overnight attacks killed four more civilians and wounded 27. - In orbit: A Russian satellite approached a Finnish-American radar satellite used by Ukraine’s military and then pulled away. It is the first known uninvited rendezvous between two active spacecraft in low Earth orbit (Democracy Now!).

3. Hegseth holds secret talks with Netanyahu as Iran mediation stalls

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth made an unannounced trip to Israel on Monday and met Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu at Ovda air base in the Negev (New York Times). Defense Minister Israel Katz reportedly attended. The Jerusalem Post and Anadolu report that the IDF chief of staff and other senior commanders were excluded for “confidentiality.” Military.com reports that the two discussed the Iran war, and AOL/Axios framed the visit as coming while Trump “weighs a massive new Iran offensive.” The meeting came a day after Trump endorsed Netanyahu and a week before Israel’s October 27 election. As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, the visit is part of preparations to resume full-scale attacks on Iran once Israel’s election and the November 3 US midterms are past.

Diplomacy is going nowhere. Pakistani army chief Field Marshal Asim Munir left Tehran on Tuesday without a breakthrough after meeting President Masoud Pezeshkian and parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf (Reuters). Tehran says it will not re-engage seriously until the US lifts its naval blockade and sanctions. The New Arab reports that Munir carried a new Pakistani–Qatari proposal on Iran’s nuclear program and frozen assets, which reportedly received “positive responses.”

Key facts: - US forces in the region: about 20,000 troops. The Theodore Roosevelt carrier group is due by the end of November, alongside the 13th Marine Expeditionary Unit (WSWS, citing Axios and Fox). - Netanyahu: “The regime must fall, and I will bring it down.” - Iranian counter-threats: Iranian media have called for strikes on Israeli nuclear sites if the US hits the “Pickaxe Mountain” facility near Natanz (Live Mint). - Iraq: Iran’s ambassador in Baghdad claims Iraq has agreed to expel Iranian Kurdish armed groups from the north (Al-Monitor). - Congress: The U.S.-Israel FUTURES Act (Sections 219 and 1217) would set up a defense-technology integration arrangement with little precedent among close US allies (Responsible Statecraft).

4. Sudan drone strikes kill more than 300 in Kordofan and Abyei

Two drone strikes in RSF-held West Kordofan on Sunday evening killed at least 150 people (BBC). Democracy Now! reported a single strike near a market that killed 80, which it called one of the deadliest single strikes since the war began in April 2023. On Monday, a drone hit a fuel depot next to a crowded market in Abyei, the disputed territory on the Sudan–South Sudan border. The territory’s information minister says at least 153 were killed (France 24). The RSF blamed the Sudanese Armed Forces, and Foreign Exchanges’ Derek Davison assesses the army as the likely perpetrator. All of West Kordofan is under RSF control.

Key facts: - Humanitarian scale: more than 13 million forcibly displaced. 33.7 million people, over two-thirds of the population, need aid. - Abyei: The strike reaches into a territory claimed by both Sudan and South Sudan, so the war is now hitting the border zone.

5. Justice Department charges 28 Californians as Trump threatens election officials with jail

On Monday the Justice Department charged 28 California residents, all legal immigrants with residency status or green cards, with voting-related crimes. It alleges that 21 of them voted illegally in past elections, and 11 have been arrested (New York Times, NPR). The charges accompany a DOJ emergency court filing to stop California processing certain returned mail ballots. It follows a letter from Acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter claiming that 20,000 people on California’s rolls are “likely noncitizens,” without evidence that any of them tried to vote. Trump posted that California election officials “will all go to jail for a long period of time!” Los Angeles County Clerk Dean Logan and Secretary of State Shirley Weber refused to comply. The Sacramento Bee quoted experts who said that if the DOJ could prove more than 28 cases, “the public would have heard about them.”

Patrick Martin wrote on the WSWS that the numbers involved could not change any result. In his view, the California targeting is meant to manufacture a confrontation and lay the basis for rejecting an expected Democratic win, while the Democrats rely on courts and state officials because they fear a mass movement more than they fear Trump.

Key facts: - Scale of the campaign: The DOJ has charged 113 people in 20 states over two months (WSWS). - Votes already cast: more than 10 million by early October. Over 100 million are expected by November 3. - Insurrection Act: Trump has not ruled out invoking it to deploy troops or federal agents at polling places. - Money: Aided by a recent Supreme Court campaign-finance ruling, the RNC is pumping $50 million into the midterms (New York Times). - From the bench: Justice Elena Kagan said Tuesday that the conservative majority’s rulings on the Voting Rights Act and campaign finance have “damaged democracy” and are fueling polarization (AP, Washington Post).

6. Meloni orders crackdown as “No Meloni Day” protests spread across Italy

Italy is now facing its own wave of youth and social protest. Premier Giorgia Meloni announced tougher security measures after riots in Milan (AP). On Friday, “No Meloni Day” demonstrations, organized largely by school student groups against crumbling schools and rising military spending, brought about 100,000 people into the streets of more than 40 cities. Marchers in Rome, Milan, Turin and Bologna carried placards reading “Fund schools, not wars.” Truckers, farmers and climate activists protested at the same time (teleSUR). Ten thousand rallied in Rome on Saturday against the cost of living.

The WSWS’s Marianne Arens reports a parallel revolt in the south. Taranto steelworkers are fighting the closure of the former Ilva plant, which threatens up to 20,000 jobs. Hauliers and farmers blockaded the port and the Eni refinery, and half of Eni’s petrol stations in Apulia reportedly ran dry.

Key facts: - Repression: 271 detentions, 220 of them minors. There was a crisis cabinet meeting, 500 more soldiers were deployed, and security laws are being tightened. - Living standards: Inflation is 4.2% against wage rises of 2.5%. Real wages have fallen 9.4% since 2021, and 5.4 million people live in absolute poverty (WSWS). - Energy: prices are up 22–26% since February, and diesel is up 31%. - Military spending: €42.7 billion in 2025, with targets of 3.2% of GDP by 2028 and 5% by 2035. - Union timetable: a grassroots unions’ strike on October 30 and a CGIL general strike on November 20. CGIL leader Maurizio Landini condemned the “looting.”

7. Houthi strike kills 73 in Yemen as Britain sends RAF to defend Saudi skies

The death toll from Monday’s Ansarullah (Houthi) missile strike on a military training camp in Shabwah province has risen to 73, with 14 still missing (Reuters). In London, Defence Secretary Wes Streeting announced that the Royal Air Force will conduct “defensive counter-air operations over Saudi Arabia” to protect Saudi cities and infrastructure from Houthi attacks (AP). Officials stress that the mission is defensive. Foreign Exchanges notes it is unclear whether that line will hold. The Burnham Labour government is now directly involved in the Gulf monarchy’s war.

Key facts: - Taiz: UN rights chief Volker Türk demanded that Ansarullah allow aid into the besieged city, where food prices have spiked and access to clean water is limited (France 24). - Displacement: more than 250,000 since July, according to UNHCR. Taiz has seen the largest movements both into and out of the city (Al Jazeera).

Israeli forces killed at least four Palestinians in Gaza on Tuesday after an overnight and daytime “surge” of strikes, mainly in Gaza City (Al Jazeera). The Gaza Health Ministry counts 1,511 killed since the October 2025 “ceasefire.” Under the blockade on commercial goods imposed October 14, the World Food Programme will cut the number of people it feeds each month from 1.6 million to 1.1 million because of funding shortfalls, which it partly attributes to US cuts. In the West Bank, Yesh Din counts at least 170 settler attacks since late September, and OCHA has recorded more than 2,060 attacks this year in about 300 communities. Police announced 10 arrests over the weekend assault on European diplomats only on Monday.

The Knesset election is on October 27. Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar leads the polls. Ben-Gvir tried to have the Arab-majority parties disqualified, and the Supreme Court overturned the move. Stellantis workers in Canada vote 97.3% to strike

More than 9,000 Unifor members at Stellantis voted 97.3% to authorize a strike (CBC, Toronto Star). About 6,400 of them work at the Windsor Assembly Plant. The others are at the idled Brampton Assembly Plant, which Stellantis plans to sell to armoured-vehicle maker Roshel, and the Etobicoke casting plant. The contract expired September 21. Stellantis began a three-week layoff at Windsor on October 19. Unifor President Lana Payne calls a strike a “last resort.” Unifor has not filed for a “no board” report, so the earliest legal walkout is the second week of November. Payne’s stated goal is to bring the Jeep Compass back to Brampton, which would end plans to bring back about 3,200 workers in Belvidere, Illinois (WSWS).

Pentagon and Seoul spar over wartime command of South Korean forces

On Tuesday the Pentagon reaffirmed that the transfer of wartime operational control (OPCON) to Seoul remains “conditions-based.” A senior official said the agreed conditions are unchanged but the “threat environment [is] evolving faster than ever” (Korea Herald, Herald Business). The dispute was set off by USFK commander Gen. Xavier Brunson, who said on October 14 that “none of us are also able to say conditions haven’t changed.” President Lee Jae-myung said Monday that wartime control must be regained “at an early date,” and his office insists that a handover would keep the alliance intact (UPI). Lee wants the transfer finished by 2030. The US has held wartime command since the Korean War.

Europe’s rearmament: Finland buys 1 million landmines, EU unveils Arctic strategy

Finland will buy 1 million anti-personnel landmines for more than $100 million. It left the Mine Ban Treaty in January along with Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland, and the International Campaign to Ban Landmines has urged all five to reverse course. In Brussels, the EU published a 20-page Arctic strategy that, according to AFP, aims to reduce dependence on the United States. It mentions the US only a handful of times and Canada almost two dozen. The plan includes a “sovereign and secure” submarine cable linking Greenland to the mainland, built with Canada, Japan and South Korea. It also backs the IRIS² satellite constellation, meant to rival Starlink, along with anti-drone and “hybrid attack” defenses against Russia and more icebreakers.

Afghanistan: 19.2 million face acute hunger this winter

The UN humanitarian office (OCHA), echoing the World Food Programme, said 15.9 million Afghans, nearly one in three, faced crisis-level or worse food insecurity in September and October. That includes 3.4 million in emergency conditions. The total is projected to reach 19.2 million during the November–April lean season, with 5 million in emergency hunger, which spokeswoman Olga Cherevko called “one step away from famine.” More than 4 million children under six will need treatment for acute malnutrition next year.

NAB axes 2,500 jobs as Australian unemployment hits four-year high

National Australia Bank will cut up to 2,500 jobs, about 6% of its workforce, with up to 1,500 gone before the end of 2026 and the rest by June 2027. The restructuring will cost about A$300 million before tax (Reuters, Bloomberg). More than 2,000 staff have already been emailed (AAP). Australian unemployment is 4.7%, its highest since October 2021, and total labor underutilization is 11% (WSWS).

Republicans make France’s riots their midterm closing message

In the Oval Office, Trump called the French protests “a full-on revolution” that was “about immigration,” not education, and called for mass deportations. At a Kansas rally he told voters that migrants are burning France to the ground, and he presented the riots as a picture of what is at stake in the midterms: “A once great nation burned to the ground.” An AP “Fact Focus” found that Trump and leading Republicans are blaming France’s immigrants for the riots without evidence. Lawful permanent resident dies in ICE custody; US citizen’s arm broken in Long Beach

Emiliano Andrade-Covarrubias, 56, a Mexican-born lawful permanent resident, was found unresponsive on Saturday in his cell at the GEO Group-run Rio Grande Processing Center in Laredo. He is the seventh person to die in ICE custody in Texas this year and the second in Laredo alone. Naza opens in Britain and wins at Hot Springs

Naza, the investigative documentary by Israeli journalist Yuval Abraham (co-director of No Other Land) and Rachel Szor, opened in UK cinemas on October 15. Paul Mitchell’s WSWS review called it “a marriage between investigative journalism and cinema” that exposes the machinery of genocide from inside. He also argued that Glazer’s framing of the violence as a universal human tendency toward “dehumanisation” obscures its roots in Zionism and imperialist backing.

The bond markets, rearmament and the global assault on social spending — Nick Beams

Beams treats the French government’s response to the October 17 revolt as orders delivered by “the wolves of finance capital.” He sets out the mechanics in detail: - French public debt is about €3.5 trillion, roughly 120% of GDP. - Debt servicing is projected to rise 25% this year, to €65 billion. - The 10-year yield rose from 4.80% to 4.90% in a single day. - Bank of France governor Emmanuel Moulin warned France risks being “strangled by interest rates.” - Lecornu says rates tied to the “geopolitical context” will cost the state an extra €10 billion next year.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Sudan’s drone war kills 300 in two days as the world looks away

Strikes on markets and a fuel depot in West Kordofan and Abyei killed more than 300 people in 48 hours, and the WSWS published nothing on it. The article could cover the regional and imperialist sponsors of both the army and the RSF (the UAE, Egypt, Saudi Arabia and Turkey), the spread of the war into the disputed Abyei border zone, and the humanitarian collapse affecting 33.7 million people.

Recommended reading France 24 More than 150 killed in Sudan drone strike in disputed Abyei border region

2. Britain enters the Saudi–Houthi war

The Burnham government’s deployment of the RAF over Saudi Arabia, presented as “defensive counter-air operations,” marks the direct entry of British imperialism into the expanding Gulf war. The WSWS’s UK coverage should take this up together with the 73 dead in Shabwah, the siege of Taiz and the silence of the SWP and RCP.

Recommended reading AP UK to fly air patrols over Saudi Arabia against Houthi attacks

3. Witkoff meets Putin: Trump moves to dictate terms over Zelensky’s head

Trump says “somebody else” should negotiate for Ukraine, Musk has reportedly throttled Ukraine’s military Starlink, and Patriot deliveries have been delayed. Witkoff is now meeting Putin in St. Petersburg. A perspective could examine how Washington is now treating the Ukraine war as something to be traded away in a deal with Moscow, while the European powers rearm to continue it on their own account.

Recommended reading Reuters/US News US envoy Witkoff arrives in St Petersburg for talks with Putin

4. Ebola spreads to Nigeria and Kenya after the destruction of global health aid

Nigeria’s first case and Kenya’s ninth come after the dismantling of USAID and cuts to WHO funding. This fits the WSWS’s ongoing coverage of the pandemic and public health, and could draw on the measles figures (4,410 US cases) to show that the anti-science policy is global.

Recommended reading France 24 Nigeria records first Ebola case as outbreak spreads across Africa

5. Senator who sued 700 patients: Marshall, medical debt and the arrest of the poor

The New York Times finding that Sen. Roger Marshall sued more than 700 former patients, leading to 81 arrests, together with Trump’s antisemitic defense of him, is a concentrated picture of the criminalization of poverty through medical debt. It should be connected to the Drop Site revelations about Mike Rogers and Aegis.

Recommended reading Democracy Now! Trump stumps for Kansas Sen. Roger Marshall

6. Mississippi clears cop who shot a toddler over stolen diapers

The ruling that the shooting of 1-year-old Kohen Wiley was “justified,” with no footage released, is an outrage that exposes police violence and the state’s cover-up. It should be covered with an emphasis on class and social conditions, not only race.

Recommended reading Democracy Now! Mississippi AG rules officer justified in fatal shooting of Black toddler

7. Afghanistan: famine conditions for 19 million after sanctions and aid cuts

The UN projects that 19.2 million Afghans will face acute hunger this winter, and 4 million children will need malnutrition treatment. This is the result of two decades of US occupation followed by frozen assets and sanctions.

Recommended reading France 24 UN says 19 mn Afghans facing acute food insecurity by April

8. Meta and Brazil’s runoff: tech oligarchs and the far right

Brazil’s Electoral Court has given Meta 24 hours to answer charges that it is suppressing Lula’s reach and boosting Flávio Bolsonaro before Sunday’s runoff. Coverage could connect Zuckerberg’s alignment with Trump to the global role of tech oligarchs in politics. It should also expose the pseudo-left “critical vote” for Lula.

Recommended reading Democracy Now! Brazilian protesters rally outside Meta offices over alleged election interference
