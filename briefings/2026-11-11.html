1. Spain’s first general strike in 14 years paralyses transport as police charge picket lines

Spain is holding a 24-hour general strike on Wednesday, November 11, over housing costs and wages. It is the first national general strike since 2012. CCOO, UGT and CGT called it together with the Tenants’ Union (Sindicato de Inquilinas) and other groups. RTVE called it unprecedented because it is the first general strike held with national elections already called. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez dissolved parliament and set elections for November 29. The day opened with police charges and road blockades: pickets cut more than 30 points on Catalonia’s road network after 7 a.m., including the AP-7, the A-2 and Barcelona’s Ronda del Litoral. In Madrid they blocked the M-30 at Legazpi. CCOO reported 13 injured and one arrested at the Mercamadrid wholesale market. UGT condemned police action against workers picketing the Granada Metro depots.

The strike has become an election-campaign battle. The PSOE government supports the mobilisation. The PP, Vox and the employers’ federation denounce it as “ideological” rather than about labour issues. Minimum service orders keep 75% of Renfe Cercanías commuter trains at peak hours, 80% of the Madrid Metro and only 25% in Barcelona. The day before, Spanish train drivers struck over maintenance and safety after the country’s second fatal rail crash this year. That was also the day of the state funeral, attended by the royal family, for the 69 people killed in the October 28 high-speed collision.

The WSWS European Editorial Board’s statement argues that the bureaucracies of CCOO and UGT took up the strike “to channel anger behind” Sánchez’s re-election. It calls for rank-and-file action committees to join the Spanish strike with the national days of action in France (November 17), Italy (November 20) and Greece (November 25) “into a single European general strike.”

Key facts: - The trigger was the September 23 eviction of Maricarmen Abascal, an elderly woman who died afterward. The eviction was carried out under a PSOE housing law and by PSOE-government police (RTVE, Jacobin interview). - This is the first all-out national strike since the 2012 strikes against Rajoy’s labour reform. - The strike demands are the right to housing, decent wages, and measures to stop the rise in the cost of living (ABC, RTVE). - The employers and the right-wing opposition say the strike is political, not a labour dispute. The government parties treat it as a show of support.

2. Trump holds off on new Iran strikes as Tehran denies talks and demands its seven conditions

Trump met Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani at the White House on Monday, with Vice President Vance present. Afterward he said he would hold off on new strikes against Iran “for now,” after appeals from regional states. He claimed Iran’s military capabilities were “largely neutralised” and said “Iran militarily is effectively over.” He also said US forces remain “locked and loaded, ready to go for any reason that changes.” Trump said Gulf partners (Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain and Kuwait) prefer the status quo, meaning the US naval blockade continues. He called it “the greatest naval blockade in the history of our country.” Qatar presented Iran’s two-day proposal, and on Tuesday a Qatari foreign ministry spokesman said both sides were making “positive statements.”

Tehran denied any negotiations were under way. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, wrote: “FOLD YOUR HAND. STOP manufacturing FAKE NEWS about imaginary talks.” He told Washington to “SURRENDER to the seven conditions Iran has repeatedly set out.” The conditions include lifting US sanctions, unfreezing Iranian assets, recognition of a new Hormuz shipping channel set up with Oman, and an end to hostilities across the region, including Israeli strikes in Lebanon. Speaking in Paris on Tuesday, Secretary of State Rubio said there was “no need” to attack Iran again. He also said Iran should pay reparations rather than receive them, and accused Tehran of “demanding pallets of cash.”

Key facts: - The war began February 28. Iran has kept the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed and has offered to reopen it if Washington accepts its terms. - US “Task Force Iron Gate” is trying to disrupt Iranian trade over land and across the Caspian. Washington has offered a $20 million reward for information on Iran’s nuclear infrastructure. - President Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has asked for an end to the state of “neither war nor peace.” Iran “will not surrender to force and bullying,” he said. - Macron is negotiating with Gulf states to lower insurance costs for ships crossing Hormuz (Bloomberg). - The administration admits the war fed inflation and helped cost Republicans the midterms. US gasoline fell below $4 a gallon for the first time since July. - The Jerusalem Post reports the IDF is preparing for a possible surprise Iranian attack while Washington pursues a deal.

3. US, Russia and Ukraine open first trilateral talks since the Iran war began

US-mediated talks between Russia and Ukraine opened in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner are leading them, and a NATO official, Gen. Alexus Grynkewich, is present. According to RIA Novosti, the first session lasted nearly four hours. Talks were set to resume Wednesday. It is the first trilateral round since negotiations were suspended when the Iran war began. Zelensky called the first day “constructive and quite substantive.” He said the sides discussed prisoner exchanges and “the substance of security guarantees for Ukraine.” The Washington Post reports the agenda includes a broad discussion of European security. Observers describe that as a risky concession to Moscow’s demand for a say over the continent’s security order. US officials want to use the talks to persuade Russia to accept wider negotiations that include European governments.

The fighting continued on both sides. Overnight Russia launched 162 drones and a ballistic missile at ten regions and hit five major substations that carry electricity between regions, according to Prime Minister Sergii Koretskyi. Russia said it shot down 155 Ukrainian drones over eight regions and Crimea. It also claimed 146 drones intercepted over Leningrad region, where a strike set the Ust-Luga Baltic oil export port on fire and injured three people (Bloomberg). Weekend strikes had already cut power to more than 600,000 people in Dnipropetrovsk region and 250,000 in Kharkiv.

Key facts: - Reuters, citing two industry sources: Ukrainian drone strikes since late October have cut Russia’s oil-refining capacity by about 30%, disrupting nine of its 35 major refineries. - Ukraine’s chief negotiator is Rustem Umerov. Trump said he sees a “strong inclination” on both sides to end the war. - The Guardian reports Ukraine has taken its first Indonesian prisoner of war, as Russian recruitment of foreign fighters grows. - The war, which began February 24, 2022, will soon enter its fifth year with the front lines largely deadlocked.

4. House Republicans bury Hegseth impeachment by 211–204 as 11 Democrats skip the vote

On Tuesday evening the House voted 211–204 to refer Rep. Thomas Massie’s impeachment resolution against War Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee. That kills it for the lame-duck session. Massie, who lost his primary to a Trump-backed challenger and is leaving Congress, was the only Republican to vote no. Independent Kevin Kiley voted with the Republicans. All 203 Democrats who voted opposed the referral, but 11 did not vote, among them Nancy Pelosi. Eight more Democratic votes would have defeated the motion and forced the House to take up the articles. Democratic leaders said they supported impeachment but did not whip the vote. Democrats may bring back narrower charges in January, when they will hold a large majority.

The resolution had eight articles. Three concerned the Iran war, which Massie said violated the War Powers Resolution’s 60-day limit. Others covered the killing of fishermen in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific whom the Pentagon labelled “narco-terrorists,” the seizure of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, and the Pentagon’s attempt to bring criminal charges against six Democratic lawmakers over a video urging troops to refuse illegal orders. That morning Hegseth held his first Pentagon press conference in six months and took questions only from pro-Trump outlets. He called the effort “a political spectacle, a personal manifesto driven by ego and resentment,” and described it as “sabotage.” Trump called Hegseth’s performance “A PLUS, PLUS, PLUS” and Massie a “TOTAL LOSER.”

As Patrick Martin wrote on the WSWS, the Democrats’ focus on Hegseth “shields Trump,” who ordered every operation listed in the articles. Senate Democrats have reportedly floated Hegseth’s removal as a bargaining chip in defence-spending talks focused on renewing Ukraine aid.

Key facts: - The same day, House Republicans chose Jim Jordan over Steve Scalise as minority leader for the next Congress, and Senate Republicans kept John Thune as leader. He will be minority leader in January (see United States). - Rep. Don Bacon (R-Neb.) said he would have voted to impeach over Hegseth’s firings of officers and Ukraine aid cuts, but not over Iran. - CNN called the vote “a surprising show of unity from a battered and divided Republican Party.”

5. Saudi–Houthi war escalates with missiles over Riyadh and strikes on Sanaa

Fighting between Saudi Arabia and Ansarallah (the Houthis) has become one of the most dangerous fronts of the wider Gulf war. On Tuesday the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile and a cruise missile fired at Riyadh and another ballistic missile aimed at the south, where Civil Defence issued alerts for Abha and Khamis Mushait. It also said it destroyed a drone over the oil-rich Eastern Province. The Houthi-run Saba agency reported Saudi airstrikes on the al-Mufazir gas station in Sanaa’s Jadar district, where gas trailers caught fire, and on a state oil company building in the south of the capital. Houthi outlets claim attacks on Riyadh airport and Aramco facilities.

Yemen’s Health Ministry says the toll from Saudi-attributed airstrikes on al-Shariya, east of Sanaa, has risen to 44 dead, including 21 children, with about 40 wounded. Ansarallah says it recorded 91 coalition strikes across six governorates in 24 hours and 4,460 coalition attacks since September.

Key facts: - The fighting escalated after the Houthis advanced toward the Bab el-Mandeb Strait in September. Since then, Houthi strikes on Saudi airports, ports and oil sites and Saudi airstrikes in Yemen have grown steadily. - Several floors of Aramco’s Riyadh headquarters were damaged on November 8. Aramco’s Eastern Province headquarters had already been badly damaged in October. - Eurasia Review reports that Sudan’s army chief al-Burhan has refused Riyadh’s request for troops against the Houthis.

6. Trump’s Board of Peace rebukes Israel over Gaza floods as 940,000 displaced live in flood zones

The US-backed Board of Peace, set up under Trump’s Gaza plan, called Israel’s response to the weekend floods “insufficient” and urged it to let essential aid in. Netanyahu’s office rejected the criticism that night. It said Hamas “has not surrendered even one weapon,” accused it of turning aid into “terror tunnels,” and said Gaza must not be rebuilt until it disarms. The New York Times reported that the floods “reflect shortfalls of the U.S.-backed truce.” According to the Guardian, charities warn that more than 940,000 displaced people live in flood-prone areas while they wait for 150,000 tents.

About 70,000 tents flooded within 12 hours, with water up to 1.5 metres deep in places. Civil Defense received more than 2,100 distress calls. A sand cliff collapsed in Deir al-Balah and killed at least five Palestinians, four of them children. Israeli attacks went on: Al Jazeera reports at least six Palestinians killed on Tuesday, including a father and his 13-year-old son.

Key facts: - Gaza Health Ministry: 74,643 killed since October 2023, and more than 1,600 since the October 2025 “ceasefire.” - Save the Children blamed Israeli restrictions on shelter and fuel: “This is a choice, and children are paying the price.” - The International Rescue Committee’s Bob Kitchen told CNN that Gaza is “barely suitable to sustain human life.”

Israel’s new Knesset sworn in as Eisenkot sets Hamas test for coalition partners

All 120 members of Israel’s 26th Knesset took the oath in Jerusalem on Tuesday, but there is still no new government. Paraguay’s Ueno Bank collapse: ousted president’s home raided as teachers strike for their pensions

The central bank seized the digital lender Ueno Bank on November 3 over solvency concerns. The collapse shut almost half of Paraguay’s population out of their bank accounts, and it has already brought down a head of state. President Santiago Peña, a close Trump ally, was impeached on Friday and is in pretrial detention on corruption and breach-of-trust charges. On Monday prosecutors searched his Asunción residence for nearly five hours and seized phones, computers and CCTV footage. The International Business Times reports a hidden armoured vault found behind a wall of the mansion. Prosecutors have asked Luxembourg to help freeze crypto assets and asked the US and Argentina to help trace missing funds.

  • The guarantee fund returned about $160 million to nearly 1.2 million depositors. Coverage stops at about $36,000 per account, which leaves public funds held at Ueno at risk.
  • According to El País, Ueno collapsed under losses from loans to companies linked to its controlling shareholder. It had attracted customers with promises of 40% cashback.
  • Teachers suspended classes and hundreds of workers protested in Asunción to demand their pension funds back (UPI).
  • New President Pedro Alliana, who is also allegedly implicated, asked his ministers to resign. He said Rubio called him to reaffirm US support.
  • The Banker reports that more than a dozen US and European banks, fund managers, rating agencies and law firms financed, rated or audited Ueno in the two years before it failed.

Lula pledges “fiscal responsibility” and a security ministry in first post-election address

Hot October CPI puts a half-point Fed hike in play

The Labor Department reported Tuesday that consumer prices rose 0.4% in October. That is down from 0.6% in September but above the 0.3% forecast. Core CPI rose 0.3% for the third month running. Shelter (+0.3%) made up more than 30% of the increase, food rose 0.5%, gasoline 1% after September’s 9.1% jump, and airfares 0.4%. Economists attribute the persistence to housing, war-driven energy costs, tariff pass-through on goods such as furniture, toys and appliances, and AI-driven demand pushing up electricity and electronics prices. The Bureau of Labor Statistics published no annual rate, because the October 2025 shutdown stopped data collection. Economists estimate the annual rate is near 4%.

The Fed under Chair Kevin Warsh raised rates in September and held in October. Before the release, Governor Christopher Waller said he was “leaning toward” a December hike and would back 50 basis points if the data ran hot. Stacy Keach dies at 85: a stage actor formed in the Vietnam War era

Stacy Keach died on Tuesday at his family home in Poland, aged 85, after “a long and difficult battle with a serious heart condition and heart failure,” his wife Małgosia Tomassi said. Most obituaries lead with Mike Hammer, the hard-boiled TV detective he played in the 1980s. Keach’s real importance was on the stage and in the American film revival of the late 1960s and early 1970s. He was trained in the classics and became one of the leading American Shakespearean actors of his generation. Off-Broadway in 1967 he starred in Barbara Garson’s MacBird!, which recast Lyndon Johnson as Macbeth at the height of the Vietnam War. He then played Buffalo Bill in Arthur Kopit’s Indians (1969), a play about the destruction of the Native peoples and the commercial myth-making that covered it up. His finest screen performance came in John Huston’s Fat City (1972), a picture of washed-up boxers and day labourers in Stockton, California, which remains one of the most unsentimental American films about working-class defeat. His career nearly ended with a 1984 prison sentence in Britain on drug charges. Late roles included Alexander Payne’s Nebraska (2013).

Among other things it suspends the political rights of people in pretrial detention. Castanheira criticises the pro-government left (Brasil de Fato, Revista Fórum) and also the “opposition-style” left of Belluzzo–Jabbour and PSOL’s Radical Left Caucus, which urges Lula to resist austerity while keeping him at the head of the movement. He cites Dilma Rousseff’s 2014–15 austerity as the precedent and warns of a real danger of a military coup.

Florida Republicans narrowly retain power as nearly 4 million vote for “democratic socialist” Nixon — Matthew Taylor

DSA member Angie Nixon lost the Senate race to Ashley Moody, 48.0% (3,974,149) to 50.9%. That is a 2.9-point gap, against Rick Scott’s 12.8-point margin in 2024. Byron Donalds beat David Jolly for governor by only 106,240 votes, even though Donalds’ operation raised about $173.6 million to Jolly’s $26.9 million, including $15 million from Ken Griffin. Republican redistricting failed to gain any of the four seats it targeted. Nixon defeated the establishment’s Alexander Vindman in the primary, 56.1% to 43.9%. In a CBS interview she first declined to call Cuba a dictatorship, then at a rally with Ocasio-Cortez called it a “failed system.” Taylor cites this as the DSA adapting to anti-communism. He argues that the vote shows wide support for socialist ideas, which the DSA directs back into the Democratic Party.

Rail and airline bosses demand Carney government arrogate power to pre-emptively ban strikes under its Bill C-39 — Carl Bronski, Pierre Lajeunesse

Bill C-39, the “Building Canada Strong Act,” passed second reading on October 8 with Liberal and Conservative votes. It would write into law the government’s power to end legal strikes and impose binding arbitration, and extend conciliation from 60 to 90 days. Before the Senate, CPKC CEO Keith Creel demanded arbitration “before a work stoppage is allowed to inflict nationwide damage.” Air Canada, Air Transat and WestJet asked for longer notice periods and intervention before strikes start. Core defence spending is projected to rise from $75.1 billion in 2026–27 to $99.2 billion by 2030–31. The authors note that the Canadian Labour Congress at first praised parts of the bill and that the NDP’s alliance with the Liberals enabled earlier Section 107 strikebreaking. They urge workers to defy strikebreaking orders.

Paraguay: The Ueno Bank collapse and the deepening crisis of bourgeois rule — Juan Martinez

This is new detail beyond International developments. Ueno lent about $628 million to companies linked to its controlling shareholder, against a legal limit of about $80 million. More than $500 million from the Social Security Institute was deposited there, and teachers’ pension funds at stake total about $60 million. Twenty-seven people have been charged, including all eight directors and CEO Miguel Vázquez. Peña faces up to 20 years. The central bank ignored at least ten warnings, and a supervisor’s report calling the bank “a financial time bomb” was suppressed. Martinez argues that the outcome was decided when Horacio Cartes abandoned Peña. He calls the Paraguayan Communist Party’s “broad, independent front” popular-front politics, and demands full restitution of savings and nationalisation of the banks under workers’ control.

Jacobin / DSA

  • “Whatever Centrists Say, the Left Won Big in the Midterms” (Corey Robin). Robin argues that the Democrats’ biggest win in 50 years, along with the DSA and progressive victories, proves the centrists wrong. He grants that left candidates often won by smaller margins than moderates and says it does not matter. His strategy is the “left wing of the possible”: “the slow boring of hard boards” inside the Democratic Party, accepting narrow wins and using office to pass progressive legislation. He sets the left’s success against Slotkin’s and Ossoff’s margins, which treats the DSA as a faction competing for position inside a capitalist party. The article does not mention the Iran war, the Democrats’ failure on Hegseth, or the pledges to “work with Trump.”
  • “Trump’s Midterm Rout Didn’t End the Threat From J. D. Vance” (Gilbert Achcar). Achcar warns that Vance, “a consummate far-right ideologist” backed by Peter Thiel, could be “an even more dangerous president than Trump,” and calls for making the fight against Miller and Vance the priority. He criticises Vance’s “America First” and his silence on Big Tech, but says almost nothing about the Democrats. He frames the danger around a single person and the 2028 election. That points readers toward a lesser-evil Democratic campaign rather than an analysis of the class forces producing dictatorship.
  • “On Strike for a Different Spain”. This interview with Mario Arguiriano of the Coordinadora Juvenil Socialista is notably to the left of Jacobin’s own line. He calls the CCOO/UGT version of the strike “an embarrassment, an insult to the concept of a general strike” and blames the PSOE for the Abascal eviction. But his programme is a “broad front for political rights” plus a long-term party, with no international strategy. Jacobin is happy to publish radical voices abroad while its US line stays inside the Democratic Party.

Assessment: Jacobin is consolidating the DSA’s place in the incoming Democratic majority. Payday Report adds that Mamdani has begun meeting union leaders to find a “leftist” Democratic presidential candidate for 2028.

Left Voice

  • “Hegseth Takes the Fall, but the War Machine Doesn’t Stop” (Samuel Karlin). Karlin argues that the impeachment push is a bipartisan effort “to restore civil-military relations” so US imperialism can keep fighting. He calls both parties “imperialist parties alike” and says “Socialists should play no part” in that project. This is correct as far as it goes. His alternative is an anti-war movement “from below” in the streets, workplaces and schools, drawing on Italian port strikes and German youth refusing conscription. It has no programme for the working class to take power and no call for an international party. It amounts to the protest politics of the PTS’s “Trotskyist Fraction.”
  • “Trump’s Promotion of Stephen Miller Shows Only the Streets Can Stop ICE” (La Izquierda Diario). The piece rightly says workers “can place no trust in either the White House or the Democratic Party leadership,” pointing to Democratic votes for ICE budgets. It calls for community self-defence committees, coordination “alongside grassroots unions,” and “a general strike until ICE is driven off the streets.” It treats the unions as a ready-made base and calls for a general strike without asking who would lead it. That avoids the question of breaking with the union bureaucracy that has kept such struggles isolated.

Assessment: Left Voice criticises the Democrats more sharply than Jacobin does. But it substitutes militant protest for building revolutionary leadership, and it still looks to the unions.

Socialist Worker (SWP)

  • “Workers’ campaign to end Palantir’s NHS contract”. Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)
