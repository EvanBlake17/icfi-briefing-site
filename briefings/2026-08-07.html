1. US-Israel war on Iran grinds on as Oman brokers Hormuz reopening

Trump told reporters the war with Iran would end “pretty soon” and that a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, mediated by Oman, could be concluded shortly. A US official said the arrangement would involve “no tolls or charges” on shipping. The claim comes six months after the February 2026 US-Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and destroyed Iranian nuclear and military infrastructure, and nearly two months after a June ceasefire memorandum of understanding that both sides have repeatedly violated.

Iran’s chief negotiator publicly derided Trump’s approach as “theater diplomacy on loop,” referring to Washington’s abrupt cancellation of a planned strike earlier this month. The most significant development is on the materiel side: despite Trump’s insistence that the US has “massive amounts” of munitions, reporting indicates the Pentagon has exhausted nearly 80 percent of its interceptor stocks for a key missile-defense system — a constraint that bears directly on Washington’s ability to sustain a prolonged regional war while simultaneously supplying Ukraine and preparing for conflict with China.

Key facts:

  • Saudi Arabia is reportedly preparing for “multiple coordinated attacks” from Iraqi Shia militias operating in coordination with Houthi forces, raising the prospect of the war spreading to the Arabian Peninsula’s oil infrastructure.
  • More than 20 million Shia pilgrims converged on Karbala for the Arbaeen commemoration this week under heavy Iraqi security precisely because of the spillover risk (see International developments).
  • Trump’s stated timetable for ending the war has now shifted repeatedly since February without any formal settlement.

2. Houthis kill 38 Saudi-backed troops, strike eighth tanker in Red Sea blockade

Houthi forces killed at least 38 Saudi-backed Yemeni government troops and wounded 29 in missile and drone attacks on Thursday, August 6, the deadliest single day of ground fighting in Yemen in months. The attacks came as the Houthi naval blockade of Saudi oil shipping entered its third week.

Houthi military spokesperson Yahya Saree announced on August 5 that fighters had “successfully targeted the Saudi oil tanker ‘Wafa’” off Yanbu in the northern Red Sea with ballistic missiles — the eighth Saudi tanker struck since the blockade began on July 22. The group says the northern Red Sea escalation is retaliation for Riyadh rerouting tankers away from the Bab el-Mandeb chokepoint, and has vowed to “close all access points” to Saudi oil shipping.

Key facts:

  • Yanbu is Saudi Arabia’s principal Red Sea oil export terminal and the western terminus of the East-West pipeline built specifically to bypass the Strait of Hormuz — meaning both of Saudi Arabia’s export routes are now under threat simultaneously.
  • The blockade has been the single largest driver of the summer’s crude price firmness, with September WTI closing at $77.85 on Thursday.
  • The Yemeni war, restarted as an adjunct of the US-Israeli campaign against Iran, has produced no meaningful coverage of Yemeni civilian casualties in the US press.

3. Trump signs two executive orders attacking birthright citizenship

Trump signed two executive orders on Thursday, August 6, aimed at narrowing birthright citizenship — his second frontal assault on the 14th Amendment and the first since the Supreme Court struck down his January 2025 order by a 6-3 vote on June 30, 2026.

The first order expands the categories of people the administration claims are ineligible for birthright citizenship to include “alien enemies of the United States, members of foreign terrorist organizations and large categories of people who lobby and act on behalf of foreign government.” The second targets so-called “birth tourism.” Both are expected to face immediate legal challenge.

Writing on the WSWS, Jacob Crosse characterized the orders as an attempt to establish, by executive decree, the principle that citizenship is a revocable privilege conferred by the state rather than a constitutional right — the legal architecture for mass denationalization.

Key facts:

  • The June 30 ruling explicitly upheld birthright citizenship as constitutional; the new orders attempt to achieve the same end by redefining who falls within the amendment’s “subject to the jurisdiction thereof” clause.
  • The “alien enemies” category is deliberately elastic and overlaps with the administration’s designation of political opponents and pro-Palestinian activists as agents of foreign powers.
  • The orders arrive alongside an AP investigation showing ICE has detained more than 50 spouses and parents of active-duty US service members (see United States).

4. Senate committee votes to hold Fauci in contempt of Congress

The Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee voted 8-5 along party lines on Thursday to hold Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress. The referral now goes to the attorney general.

Fauci invoked the Fifth Amendment more than 100 times during his July 29 testimony, stating that he feared politically motivated prosecution. Committee chairman Rand Paul framed the vote around “whether a witness who has received the benefit of a federal pardon can be ordered by this committee to answer questions and then defy that order without consequence” — a reference to Biden’s pre-departure pardon. Paul released more than 1,100 pages of Fauci’s private diaries in advance of the hearing.

Today’s WSWS Perspective places the vote in the context of a broader criminalization of infectious-disease research: virologist David Morens was indicted in April 2026 on five counts; Peter Daszak was fired and debarred in January 2025; NIH researchers were arrested for transporting deactivated mpox samples. As the Perspective notes, “the man referred for prosecution is the one who, at least initially, opposed” the policy of mass infection.

Key facts:

  • The contempt vote comes as the CDC reports 10,468 confirmed cyclosporiasis cases across 47 states with more than 12,255 additional cases awaiting confirmation and two deaths; 345 salmonella cases across 27 states, prompting a recall of more than 1.5 million dozen eggs; and the worst measles year since 1991.
  • Roughly 1.4 million people have died of COVID-19 in the United States and approximately 30 million worldwide.
  • No Democratic senator on the committee voted for the referral, but the party has offered no defense of the underlying scientific work.

5. July jobs report: 83,000 payrolls added, employment level down 833,000 for the year

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the July employment situation at 8:30 a.m. Eastern this morning. Nonfarm payrolls rose by 83,000 and the unemployment rate held at 4.2 percent.

The headline stability conceals a contracting labor market. The employment level has fallen by 833,000 over the course of 2026, and the jobless rate has been held down primarily by declining labor force participation rather than job creation. June’s gain was revised to a mere 57,000, the weakest in four months, following a downward revision of May to 129,000. The broader U-6 measure, which includes involuntary part-time and discouraged workers, has risen to 8.2 percent, and the employment-population ratio sits at a four-year low. Citigroup economists project unemployment breaking above 4.5 percent in coming months.

Key facts:

  • Consensus forecasts had been for 80,000-83,000 payrolls with unchanged unemployment — the report landed almost exactly on expectations, giving the Fed no impetus to move before its September 15-16 meeting.
  • Job growth has been concentrated in health care and leisure/hospitality; manufacturing and federal employment continue to shed positions.
  • The report follows an ADP private payrolls miss on Wednesday that had already signaled softness.

6. Russian and Ukrainian strikes kill dozens in a week as Black Sea shipping comes under attack

Russian strikes killed at least six people in Ukraine overnight August 5-6 — three in Balakliya in the Kharkiv region and three in Sumy — with 19 wounded. Moscow said it intercepted 605 Ukrainian drones over the same period, an indication of the scale the drone war has now reached.

A Russian attack on a foreign-flagged, wheat-laden vessel in the Black Sea killed a crew member and set the ship on fire. Key facts:

  • Between August 1 and 4, at least 27 people were killed: 16 in Russia, including seven in Arkhipo-Osipovka (three of them children) from drone debris, and 11 in Ukraine, including eight in Zaporizhzhia.
  • Zelensky has warned that Ukraine is running critically low on interceptor missiles for its US-supplied Patriot systems — the same shortage constraining US operations against Iran.
  • Neither Washington nor Moscow has advanced any negotiating framework since the collapse of talks earlier this year.

7. Gaza: 1.6 million face extreme hunger as Israel holds 70 percent of the Strip

Nine months into the October 2025 “ceasefire,” more than 75 percent of Gaza’s population — 1.6 million people — face extreme acute food insecurity and critical malnutrition risk. Nearly 101,000 children are projected to face acute malnutrition through mid-October, more than 31,000 of them severe cases.

Israel’s military now controls close to 70 percent of Gaza’s territory, an expansion well beyond the lines established under the ceasefire agreement. Israel has violated the agreement at least 3,795 times through daily air, artillery and small-arms attacks, according to a tally through mid-July 2026. Hamas has separately agreed to a US-brokered roadmap involving staged disarmament, per mediators from Egypt, Qatar, Turkey and the United States.

Key facts:

  • The “ceasefire” has functioned as a mechanism for consolidating military occupation and administering starvation rather than ending hostilities.
  • The famine is administratively produced: aid entry remains throttled at levels far below the minimum required, with distribution militarized.
  • UNESCO this month placed Palestinian and Lebanese heritage sites on its List of World Heritage in Danger, formally acknowledging the destruction of cultural patrimony (see Arts and culture).

8. Amnesty finds “hallmarks of genocide” in Sudan’s El Fasher as famine spreads

Amnesty International has described Rapid Support Forces atrocities in El Fasher as “a stain on the conscience of humanity” following the paramilitary group’s completion of an 18-month siege and capture of the North Darfur capital. A separate UN investigation concluded the takeover bore “the hallmarks of genocide,” with thousands killed.

Famine conditions are now confirmed in El Fasher and Kadugli. Roughly 375,000 people are in the most extreme hunger category, concentrated in North Darfur, South Kordofan and West Kordofan. Civilians are reported to be surviving on ambaz, the peanut-oil pressing residue normally used as animal feed. Amnesty sent its findings to RSF commander General Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo on June 10 and received no response.

Key facts:

  • The RSF is armed and financed by the United Arab Emirates, a principal US regional partner; the Sudanese Armed Forces receive backing from Egypt and, indirectly, Saudi Arabia.
  • Sudan’s war has displaced more people than any other conflict on earth yet receives a fraction of the coverage devoted to Ukraine or Gaza.
  • No Western government has imposed meaningful sanctions on the UAE over its role.

Student kills seven at Thai high school in country’s worst massacre since 2022

A Grade 9 student opened fire at Debsirin School in the Bang Kruai district of Nonthaburi province, outside Bangkok, on August 7, killing seven people and wounding roughly 15 before taking his own life. Police said the 15-year-old had shot and killed his grandparents at their home in Bang Bua Thong district before travelling to the school.

Thailand has one of the highest rates of civilian firearm ownership in Asia. The attack follows a school shooting in Hat Yai earlier this year, a pattern of youth violence that has developed alongside deepening social distress, a stagnant economy and chronic political instability.

Italy issues record 25 red heat alerts; Austria breaks national record twice in two days

Italy’s health ministry issued red-level heat alerts for 25 cities — the largest number on record — as the country endured its fourth major heatwave of summer 2026. Austria separately broke its all-time national temperature record twice within two days this week.

The alerts are the highest of Italy’s three-tier system, indicating danger to the entire population rather than only vulnerable groups. Outdoor and agricultural workers bear the overwhelming burden: Italy has no national heat-stress standard mandating work stoppages, and enforcement of regional ordinances is minimal. Southern and Central Europe have now experienced four consecutive summers of record or near-record heat, with no corresponding change in labor protection or infrastructure investment.

More than 20 million pilgrims mass at Karbala for Arbaeen under war shadow

More than 20 million pilgrims converged on Karbala, Iraq, for the annual Arbaeen commemoration marking the seventh-century killing of Imam Hussein — among the largest annual gatherings of people anywhere on earth. Most travel on foot, many walking the 80 kilometres from Najaf, sustained by volunteer-run food and rest stations along the route.

This year’s pilgrimage took place under heavy Iraqi security amid the ongoing US-Israel-Iran war and the risk of attacks on Iranian pilgrims crossing the border. The scale of the mobilisation — and the fact that it is organized almost entirely through informal networks of poor and working-class Iraqis and Iranians — is a reminder that the populations of the region being written off as adjuncts of one state or another are capable of extraordinary collective organization.

WestJet flight attendants win recognition of unpaid ground work after four-day strike

Roughly 4,400 WestJet flight attendants represented by CUPE Local 8125 walked out at 12:01 a.m. MT on August 2 after overnight talks collapsed, forcing the cancellation of 309 flights over the Canadian long weekend and disrupting travel for an estimated 250,000 passengers. WestJet had answered the union’s 72-hour strike notice with a 72-hour lockout notice.

The central issue was compensation for duties performed on the ground — boarding, deplaning, delays — which the industry’s “credit hour” system has historically treated as unpaid. A tentative agreement was reached on August 3; if ratified, pre-departure work will be recognized as paid time. The dispute follows the 2025 Air Canada flight attendants’ strike, which broke through the same unpaid-labour regime and which Canadian carriers have since sought to contain.

Yen intervention exposes US Treasury market fragility

The dominant financial story of the week remains the extraordinary joint US-Japan currency intervention. The Japanese government and Bank of Japan bought yen for a second consecutive day while the New York Fed sold euros to buy yen on behalf of the US Treasury — the first coordinated US-Japan yen-buying operation since 1998. Tokyo spent roughly ¥5.33 trillion on Friday after a record ¥8.45 trillion the previous day, potentially selling as much as $59 billion. The yen gained as much as 1.4 percent to a near three-month high of 155.20 per dollar, on top of a 3.8 percent surge over the prior two sessions, before settling near 157.

Nick Beams argues on the WSWS that the operation was undertaken to protect the US Treasury market, not Japan. Critically, the Treasury chose to sell euros rather than dollar securities. Barry Eichengreen: “The message is that US Treasury secretary Scott Bessent & Co worried that selling dollar securities… would put additional strain on the long end of the US Treasury market.” Matt King: “The decision and the manner of the intervention ultimately say as much about US vulnerabilities as they do about Japan.” Japanese 10-year yields have reached 2.8 percent and 30-year yields 4.0 percent — nosebleed levels for a market anchored near zero for two decades — threatening an unwind of a yen carry trade running into the low trillions of dollars.

FDA approves first mRNA flu vaccine after refusal-to-file reversal

The FDA on August 5 approved Moderna’s mFlusiva, the first licensed mRNA-based influenza vaccine. The agency granted standard approval for ages 50-64 on the basis of a 40,000-person trial showing 27 percent fewer confirmed influenza cases than a standard-dose comparator, and accelerated approval for ages 65 and older contingent on a required Phase 4 confirmatory trial.

The significance is manufacturing as much as efficacy: mRNA platforms allow strain composition to be updated in weeks rather than the six months required for egg-based production, which is the principal reason seasonal flu vaccine effectiveness collapses in years when the circulating strain drifts after strain selection. The approval follows a rare FDA refusal-to-file earlier this year that was subsequently reversed — an episode that took place against the backdrop of the administration’s broader hostility to mRNA technology, including the cancellation of federal mRNA development contracts.

Australia confirms first H5N1 mass mortality event

Australian authorities confirmed H5N1 on August 3 in a die-off of 49 dead and 35 sick crested terns near Cape Jaffa, south of Adelaide, with additional suspect cases in Victoria. Agriculture Minister Julie Collins warned of further spread among native wildlife.

Australia was the last continent to record H5N1, first confirmed in wildlife there in June 2026. No commercial farm infections have yet been detected, but the arrival of the panzootic in a continent with highly endemic and geographically restricted bird and marine mammal populations raises the prospect of extinction-level events among species with no evolutionary exposure. Global surveillance capacity has meanwhile been cut sharply, with US federal public health staffing reductions eliminating positions in influenza surveillance and outbreak response.

Arts and culture

Bayreuth’s AI-generated Ring booed at the festival’s 150th anniversary

The most significant cultural event of the week is the Bayreuth Festival’s new production of Wagner’s Der Ring des Nibelungen, titled Ring 10010110, staged for the 150th anniversary of the cycle’s premiere at the Festspielhaus. Curator Marcus Lobbes, working with Nils Corte and dramaturge Andri Hardmeier, fed roughly 1,000 images drawn from the fifteen previous Bayreuth Ring productions into a generative model, which then produced a continuous stream of associative imagery as the cycle’s visual environment. Singers appeared as floating sculptural figures in costumes by Pia Maria Mackert.

The audience booed. Lobbes and his design team were met with whistles at the curtain call, while conductor Christian Thielemann and the cast received a warm reception. Critical reaction identified the central failure precisely: the software could assemble images but could not construct drama. Reviewers described Das Rheingold as “stiff and dramatically inert,” with the performers only gradually reclaiming human presence over the course of the four evenings.

The episode deserves attention beyond the opera world. The Ring is, among other things, a work about the corrupting power of accumulated wealth and the destruction of the natural and social world by those who seize it — themes that have never been more directly relevant. To reduce its staging to a recombinant collage of prior stagings is to substitute a statistical average of the past for an artistic engagement with the present. Generative models can only interpolate within what has already been made; they cannot pose a question. The boos at Bayreuth were, in this sense, an audience defending art against its liquidation into content.

UNESCO places Palestinian and Lebanese sites on World Heritage in Danger list

At the 48th session of the World Heritage Committee in Busan, South Korea, UNESCO inscribed the Palestinian archaeological site of Sebastia under emergency procedures while simultaneously placing it on the List of World Heritage in Danger, over Israeli objections. The Lebanese Mount Amel castles — five fortifications documenting the evolution of regional defensive architecture from the Crusades to the nineteenth century — were added under the same dual procedure, as was the ancient Phoenician city of Tyre.

Writing on the WSWS, Sandy English notes that these designations amount to an official international acknowledgment that Israeli military operations are destroying irreplaceable cultural patrimony belonging to all humanity — and that the designation carries no enforcement mechanism whatsoever. The destruction of the material record of Palestinian and Lebanese history is not incidental to the war; the erasure of historical continuity is one of its objects.

A “socialism” for the New York Times: Jacobin founder Bhaskar Sunkara’s interview with Ross Douthat — Joseph Kishore

The interview’s political content — a socialism of upzoning and rent stabilization, DSA officeholders presented as moderating influences, and complete silence on war across 29 pages — is the clearest available statement of what the DSA offers the American ruling class.

Jacobin’s own output this week is characteristic:

  • “The Deadly Military-Vet-to-ICE Pipeline” (Suzanne Gordon and Steve Early) examines ICE and police recruitment of veterans with combat-related psychological injury. What the WSWS should cover today

1. Verizon and Kaiser Permanente contracts expire as unions refuse to call strikes

Twenty thousand Verizon workers have been without a contract since August 1 and 24,600 Kaiser Northern California nurses face expirations this month, with no strike deadline set by either the CWA/IBEW or National Nurses United. This is the largest concentration of expired-contract workers in the US at present and a direct test case for the rank-and-file committee perspective; the pattern of allowing contracts to lapse indefinitely while negotiations proceed in secret is the central mechanism by which the apparatus prevents a unified struggle.

2. WestJet strike and the fight against unpaid ground work in aviation

Four thousand four hundred CUPE flight attendants shut down Canada’s second-largest airline over unpaid boarding and ground time, cancelling 309 flights before a tentative agreement was reached within four days. The rapidity of the settlement, following the 2025 Air Canada strike, raises the question of whether CUPE moved to contain a struggle that had immediate national impact — and the “credit hour” system at issue is industry-wide, affecting flight attendants at every North American carrier.

Recommended reading CBC WestJet, CUPE reach tentative deal to end flight attendants’ strike

3. Sudan: famine and mass killing in El Fasher and Kordofan

The RSF’s capture of El Fasher after an 18-month siege has produced findings of “hallmarks of genocide” from a UN investigation and confirmed famine affecting 375,000 people in the most extreme category. The war is directly fuelled by UAE arms and Gulf capital’s interest in Sudanese gold and agricultural land, making it a case study in the neo-colonial recolonization of Africa that receives a small fraction of the coverage devoted to other theatres.

Recommended reading Amnesty International Sudan: RSF atrocities in El Fasher ‘a stain on the conscience of humanity’

4. Tariffs as a $900 tax increase on every US household

The Tax Foundation calculates that the 2026 tariff regime — including the 35 percent levy on Canadian goods effective August 1 — amounts to an average tax increase of roughly $900 per household this year, with no measurable improvement in the trade balance. Public attention to tariffs has collapsed even as the costs are passed through to consumers, making this an under-covered mechanism of downward wealth redistribution and a direct refutation of the “economic nationalism benefits workers” argument advanced by both the Trump administration and the UAW leadership.

Recommended reading Tax Foundation Tariff tracker: 2026 Trump tariffs and trade war by the numbers

5. Thai school shooting and the crisis of youth in Southeast Asia

A 15-year-old killed his grandparents and then seven people at his Bangkok-area high school, Thailand’s worst massacre since 2022. Youth violence of this kind is emerging across countries with sharply rising inequality, collapsing employment prospects for young people and militarized state cultures — a phenomenon requiring the same social analysis the WSWS has applied to school shootings in the United States rather than the individualized psychological framing offered by the press.

Recommended reading NPR Student kills at least 6 in a shooting at a high school in Thailand

6. FIFA’s aborted plan to sell equity in the World Cup

Infantino’s “FIFA Forward Enterprise” scheme would have sold private stakes in the commercial rights to the World Cup, negotiated without consultation with member associations, players or supporters. UEFA now has more than enough members to force an extraordinary congress. The affair is a concentrated example of the drive to convert every remaining collective cultural institution into a financial asset, and of the total exclusion of the players — who are workers — from decisions over the industry they produce.

7. Europe’s fourth heatwave and the absence of heat protection for workers

Italy issued red alerts for a record 25 cities and Austria broke its national temperature record twice in two days, with no corresponding national heat-stress standard mandating work stoppages for outdoor and agricultural labourers in most of Europe. The absence of enforceable protection — four years into consecutive record summers — is a direct expression of the subordination of workers’ lives to production schedules and an area where WSWS coverage of climate can be tied concretely to labour conditions.
