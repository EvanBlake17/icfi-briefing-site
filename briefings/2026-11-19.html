1. Trump meets Putin and Xi in Shenzhen as first three-way summit opens

On Thursday, ahead of the first-ever trilateral meeting of the US, Russian and Chinese heads of state on the sidelines of the APEC summit, Trump held separate talks with Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping (CNBC). Bloomberg reports it was his first meeting with Putin in more than a year. Trump called Putin “my friend” (BBC) and praised him during their talks (CBS). Before the event he declared it “already a win” (Reuters via U.S. News). Al Jazeera reports that the bilateral sessions focused on Ukraine, Iran and trade, and that AI is also on the agenda. The New York Times reports that the venue is a Shenzhen villa that once hosted Kim Jong-il. As of 07:50 GMT no outcome of the trilateral had been reported.

On Wednesday, Xi told Putin that Beijing and Moscow should “lead and advance the trend towards a multipolar world.” Speaking to the APEC summit after Xi, Putin called the Western powers “former hegemons” and described Europe as in “humiliating decline.” He said that “the traditional tools of economic dominance and de facto coercion have largely run their course,” and that sanctions, tariffs and export restrictions are “incapable of halting growth” (Al Jazeera).

In Washington, Trump’s critics are attacking him from the right. Andre Damon argues on the WSWS that the Democrats, the Wall Street Journal and the European governments do not object to war with Russia and China, only to the pace of escalation (see WSWS coverage).

Key facts: - Nuclear arms: NBC News reports that Trump “pushes for three-way nuclear deal as Russia and China show little interest.” Pusan National University’s Robert Kelly told Al Jazeera that a “tripolar world of nuclear powers” makes arms control unlikely in the near term. - Allies: Politico reports that the meeting has “spooked” US allies, one of whom called it “an absolute nightmare.” RFI reports that Europe has been “left out in the cold.” CNBC’s coverage of the summit’s final day notes that Japanese Prime Minister Takaichi was “kept at a distance.” - Trade: the US–China trade truce expires in January, and Al Jazeera reports it “may or may not be extended.” Analysts expect few concrete results. - Xi–Putin: the two leaders have met more than 40 times. China is Russia’s largest trading partner and a major buyer of its energy (Al Jazeera).

2. Iran war spreads to Kuwait and Bahrain as Trump weighs a new air campaign

On Wednesday, Iran retaliated for Tuesday’s US strike on an IRGC missile site on Qeshm Island. It said it targeted US drone and missile installations at Kuwait’s Ali Al Salem air base, and Kuwait said it repelled its first Iranian attack in two months (BBC). Bahrain, home to the US Fifth Fleet, reported intercepting Iranian aerial attacks for the third time in a week. The Wall Street Journal described the exchanges as “further escalating [the] fight over the strait.” The New York Post published video of a strike on a UAE oil tanker.

Axios reports that Trump is weighing new strikes as Israel warns of Iranian chemical weapons. The New York Times reports that Netanyahu is pressing for a joint US-Israeli air campaign lasting four to seven days, and that intelligence disputes and disagreements inside the administration remain. CNN cites seven US officials on intelligence about possible Iranian attacks on senior officials using a dirty bomb or chemical weapon. Officials disagree over whether Iran intends or is able to carry out such attacks, and White House spokesperson Lauren Bis said there were “no credible threats to the homeland.” Kevin Reed writes on the WSWS that the strikes expose Trump’s post-election claim that Iran’s military is “effectively over.”

Key facts: - Hormuz: Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao told a Senate hearing that insurance for a single transit now costs $64 million, against about $54,000 before the war. Analysts note that the figure is unverified. UKMTO reports that traffic is about 65 percent below pre-war levels, with 308 crossings in the week to November 11. Iran exempts ships from India, China, Iraq and Qatar (Al Jazeera). - Blockade: a new US advisory warns that ships taking cargo at sea from Iran-loaded vessels may be targeted. Task Force Iron Gate is trying to cut Iranian land and Caspian supply routes, and the USS Theodore Roosevelt has arrived to support it. - Hezbollah sanctions: on Wednesday, Treasury designated the Wa Ta’awanu Association as a Hezbollah front, along with three Lebanese individuals and two gold companies accused of moving Iranian money. Secretary of State Rubio told G7 foreign ministers the US will keep imposing “devastating” costs on Iran. - Yemen front: the Houthis fired missiles toward Riyadh as CENTCOM chief Brad Cooper met Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman (see International).

3. Europe meets in Berlin on Ukraine as the Kerch bridge is hit a third time

Ukraine hit the Kerch Strait bridge linking Russia to Crimea again overnight. Reuters says it was the third strike in a week, timed “ahead of [the] US-China-Russia summit,” and Ukrinform reports that train services on the bridge are suspended. According to its manufacturer, Destinus, seven Dutch-made Ruta missiles hit the bridge on Tuesday and one span fell (cited by WSWS). Moscow has asked whether the Netherlands has joined the conflict (Guardian). The Washington Post reports that Putin is touting Russia’s economy at APEC while Ukrainian strikes push the country toward a fuel crisis.

In Berlin on Thursday, Chancellor Friedrich Merz is hosting the “Coalition of the Willing” with Emmanuel Macron and UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham. More than 30 leaders, including Zelensky, are expected to discuss long-term security guarantees and further support. US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner are to meet Ukrainian officials and Europe’s new negotiating team. Washington supports European participation in the next round of talks, but Moscow has not agreed (Al Jazeera). Politico Europe calls the grouping the “Coalition of the Wobbling.”

Key facts: - EU money: the Commission has proposed €45 billion for 2027, with up to €25 billion for military aid and €20 billion for budget support. It is to be repaid only after Russia pays reparations (Reuters). Even if approved, it leaves a €25 billion gap. Bulgaria, Hungary, the Czech Republic and Slovakia will opt out of the next round, and a €1.71 billion tranche for drones, ammunition and air defence was paid Monday. - Arms: Germany and Norway have each pledged $300 million to PURL, the NATO scheme for buying US weapons, including Patriot missiles. Poland will add nothing for now. Zelensky is asking for 40 more F-16s and for Patriot and IRIS-T interceptors to be released from European stocks. - Split over deep strikes: at the Ukraine Defense Contact Group, Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby said the long-range strikes have neither ended the war nor slowed Russian advances, and that hits on civilian targets may harden Russian opinion. German Defence Minister Boris Pistorius called this “quite a stupid thing.” UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said the strikes had “helped to degrade Putin’s war machine,” and NATO’s Mark Rutte called hitting logistics targets “unavoidable.” - Ukraine’s defence minister says Kyiv plans to more than triple its deep-strike capacity next year.

4. Nvidia scandal reaches Commerce Secretary Lutnick as Washington tightens chip controls

The political crisis that followed Jensen Huang’s resignation as Nvidia CEO has reached the cabinet. In a five-page letter dated November 17, first reported by the Financial Times, House Democrats Jamie Raskin, Gregory Meeks and Sydney Kamlager-Dove asked Cantor Fitzgerald chairman Brandon Lutnick whether the firm’s financial exposure influenced his father’s decision to keep the Singapore firm Megaspeed off the Entity List. Cantor Fitzgerald is controlled by Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s sons. Megaspeed is linked to Alice Huang, who is alleged to be a Chinese intelligence asset and who reportedly had an affair with Jensen Huang. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says four agencies favoured blacklisting Megaspeed but Commerce, which led the process, did not. Reuters reports that senior White House officials have discussed whether Lutnick should be pushed out. Commerce says he has complied with his ethics agreement and divested. Cantor says it holds no material proprietary position in Nvidia (Al Jazeera).

The Commerce and Justice departments have confirmed a criminal investigation into Megaspeed on suspicion of diverting advanced chips to China, which the company denies. Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security has issued a 132-page interim final rule requiring data center operators in third countries to obtain US licences before buying advanced AI chips. It names Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil as high diversion-risk destinations. BIS says the rule was “written by and at the direction of” Treasury and the National Security Council. Nick Beams notes on the WSWS that Trump himself has described a split between an administration camp that wants friendly relations with Beijing and one that wants confrontation.

Key facts: - Board: Nvidia chair Harvey Jones said the board “unanimously accepted [Huang’s] resignation and does not intend to reinstate” him. Nearly 200,000 people, including Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney, have signed a letter calling for his return. Bill Ackman says the board “panicked,” and Palmer Luckey says China is “thrilled to have Jensen out.” - Hawks: the Wall Street Journal reports that “the Jensen Huang saga has reinvigorated Washington’s China hawks.” Bloomberg reports that Tencent expects US chip curbs to slow Chinese AI in the short run. - Allies: the New York Times describes Southeast Asian governments as “rattled.” Singapore and Indonesia say they are drawing up compliance frameworks. - Shares: Nvidia stock is down more than 30 percent over the past month, despite third-quarter net profit of $64.9 billion, up 109 percent.

5. Anthropic prices the largest IPO in history at a $1.5 trillion valuation

Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 a share, above the marketed range of $70–78, and sold 980 million shares instead of the planned 825 million. The IPO raised $81.3 billion, and a planned $26 billion private placement brings total capital raised to $107.3 billion. Bloomberg reports that this tops SpaceX’s June debut, which raised $85.7 billion including the over-allotment. At a market value of about $1.5 trillion, the company would be the world’s 12th largest, just behind Tesla. Shares under the ticker “ANT” begin trading on Nasdaq on Thursday, and Bloomberg reports grey-market indications about 20 percent above the offer price.

The offering comes in the middle of an AI selloff. The Nasdaq Composite has fallen 7.6 percent in a month, and Bloomberg’s Asia wrap reports that stocks there were set to fall ahead of the debut. Companies betting on frontier AI now make up about half the value of the S&P 500. The University of Florida’s Jay Ritter said Anthropic’s ability to justify its valuation depends on factors partly outside its control (Al Jazeera). The New York Times calls the listing an “experiment in corporate power.” The South China Morning Post presents the proceeds as a “war chest to undercut Chinese AI rivals,” and the IPO is being sold in Washington’s terms of the technology war with Beijing.

Key facts: - Gartner forecasts AI-related spending of $2.7 trillion in 2026 and $3.6 trillion in 2027. - Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives called the IPO “a big near-term win for the tech sector.” - The listing coincides with Fed minutes signalling further rate increases (see World economy).

6. Italy braces for Friday general strike as Europe-wide day of action approaches

Italy’s largest union confederation, the CGIL, has called a 24-hour general strike for Friday, November 20, against the Meloni government’s 2027 budget. The rank-and-file unions USB, COBAS and CUB are striking the same day. La Repubblica reports that transport, schools and health care are expected to come to a standstill, with CGIL rallies in about 100 squares. A Trenitalia rail strike will run for 23 hours across November 20–21, and further airport strikes are set for November 22 at Milan Malpensa and Venice. The draft budget raises defence spending to almost €38.8 billion while cutting public services. Defence Minister Guido Crosetto says: “There is only one war, and we must prepare to fight it at every level.”

The strike coincides with the first coordinated European youth day of action against war, conscription and cuts, with protests in more than 20 countries. Greece’s public sector is due to strike on November 25. Johannes Stern argues on the WSWS that the CGIL wants to steer the movement into budget negotiations and a future centre-left “Campo largo” government, and that the struggle needs an independent socialist perspective.

Key facts: - A CGIL delegation met the government on November 13. Its representative Christian Ferrari called the meeting “cordial but politically useless.” - After mass opposition, the government withdrew a plan to cut nearly 7,000 public-school posts. - Previous mobilizations drew more than 1 million people to Rome on October 4, and about 1 million joined actions in 70 cities on October 30, according to the rank-and-file unions.

World Court rules today on whether Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya

The International Court of Justice will begin reading its judgment in The Gambia v. Myanmar at 10am in The Hague (04:00 ET) on Thursday. The reading is expected to last up to 90 minutes. The Gambia filed the case in November 2019 with the backing of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, after the 2017 military offensive that drove at least 730,000 Rohingya into Bangladesh. A UN fact-finding mission concluded that the campaign included “genocidal acts.”

  • About 1.2 million Rohingya live in the Cox’s Bazar camps in Bangladesh. Some 550,000 remain in Myanmar under restrictions on movement, health care and livelihoods.
  • The Gambia is asking the court to order prosecutions, reparations and a safe return. Myanmar denies intent to destroy the group.
  • The ruling is final but practically unenforceable. A finding of state genocide would be the court’s first; in the 2007 Srebrenica case it found only that Serbia had failed to prevent genocide. Al Jazeera notes the implications for South Africa’s case against Israel.
  • The Guardian reports fears that the US will sanction the court’s Nobel-winning judge after the ruling.

  • Hegseth narrowly survived a House impeachment effort this month. Al Jazeera reports that Democrats plan to investigate Trump’s handling of Venezuela’s oil revenues, which one source describes as having “zero transparency.”

Houthis push toward Aden and fire missiles at Riyadh

On November 15, Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces announced that they were pulling back from positions overlooking the Bab el-Mandeb strait, saying troops were being redeployed to defend Aden (The National). Reports differ on the scale: some describe a full withdrawal, while others say government forces regrouped south of Dhubab and are still fighting nearby (Boston Globe). The Houthis took Mokha and the coastal strip in September and the Hanish islands after that. On Thursday the Saudi-led coalition said it had repelled Houthi missiles fired toward Riyadh and the south of the kingdom, as CENTCOM’s Cooper met Mohammed bin Salman (Al Jazeera).

  • Al Jazeera reports that the Houthi war economy, built on taxes, levies and fuel control, is feeding recruitment while poverty deepens and public services collapse.
  • With Hormuz closed to normal traffic, two of the world’s key shipping chokepoints are now under pressure at once.

Miami jury convicts plotter in Moïse assassination as Bukele bombs Haitian gang leader

A federal jury in Miami convicted Christian Emmanuel Sanon, a 68-year-old Haitian-American pastor, on all eight charges, including conspiracy to kill Haitian President Jovenel Moïse in July 2021. Prosecutors said about two dozen foreign mercenaries were paid with money from US COVID-19 business-aid programmes, and that South Florida was the plot’s planning and financing hub. Sentencing is set for February 10. More than 40 people have been charged and a dozen convicted. Seventeen Colombian ex-soldiers and former official Joseph Félix Badio are still awaiting trial.

  • Salvadoran President Nayib Bukele has confirmed an airstrike on the luxury mansion of Haitian gang leader “Jeff,” part of El Salvador’s US-aligned military deployment in Haiti (Euronews).
  • The UN documented 4,595 people killed in armed violence in Haiti from January 1 to October 31, and nearly 1.5 million displaced. Martine Moïse, the president’s widow, has registered as a candidate for the 2027 election.

Trump makes third Supreme Court bid to strip Venezuelans of protected status

On Wednesday the administration filed an emergency application, its third to the Supreme Court on the issue, to block a ruling by San Francisco District Judge Edward Chen. Chen found that the government lacked the power to end Temporary Protected Status for Venezuelans granted under Biden, and his ruling takes effect Friday unless it is stayed. The government says reinstatement would block up to 332 removal orders and could free about 1,800 Venezuelans from detention. The plaintiffs describe detentions, separation from US-citizen children, and deportations to third countries.

  • The administration has ended TPS for more than 1 million people from 13 countries. Biden had extended protection for about 600,000 Venezuelans through October 2026.
