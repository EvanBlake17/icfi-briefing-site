1. US halts Iran bombing for a fourth night as Trump claims talks are underway

Trump directed the US military not to conduct new strikes on Iran on Friday, breaking a nearly two-week streak of nightly attacks. The pause has now held for a fourth consecutive night, with Iran reciprocating by suspending its retaliatory strikes. Administration officials attributed the decision to two factors: dwindling munitions stockpiles, and the assessment by Admiral Brad Cooper, commander of US Central Command, that the bombing campaign around the Strait of Hormuz had reached the limit of its effectiveness.

Trump told reporters: “We’re talking to [the Iranians] right now. I think they’re getting more and more serious as the days go by. We’re locked and loaded and ready to go, but we’re talking to them.” Iran’s Foreign Ministry disputed the claim outright, stating that no negotiations are currently underway. UN Ambassador Mike Waltz said Trump was giving the talks “some space” while weighing further escalation.

Key facts:

  • Since the war was launched in February, 18 US troops have been killed and 624 wounded, according to figures compiled from Pentagon reporting. The Pentagon has moved casualties incurred since July 7 to a separate “overseas operations casualties” page, requiring anyone seeking a full accounting to combine multiple sources.
  • Ship traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains a fraction of prewar levels — fewer than 10 commodity vessels passed through as of Monday, against roughly 100 on a typical pre-war day.
  • Qatar and Pakistan are carrying indirect messages between Washington and Tehran; regional officials describe the informal channel as “working” to sustain the pause.
  • Senior administration officials conceded that renewed bombing was unlikely to force Iran back to the table, with one noting the strikes had the opposite of their intended effect — keeping Iran’s population cohesive and allowing the leadership to focus attention on an external enemy.
  • Trump hosts Netanyahu at the White House Tuesday to discuss Iran, the Lebanon negotiations, and expansion of the Abraham Accords. The next round of US-brokered Israel-Lebanon talks is scheduled for Rome next week. The administration also announced steps last week toward a civil nuclear partnership with Saudi Arabia.

2. Houthis strike Aramco facilities as Yemen front opens against Saudi Arabia

Yemen’s Houthi movement has opened a second front in the regional war, declaring a naval blockade of Saudi Arabia on July 20 and ending an informal truce that had held since 2022. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree said the group struck Aramco installations at Jizan and Yanbu on Sunday. A Patriot battery operated by Greek forces stationed in Saudi Arabia intercepted two ballistic missiles aimed at the Yanbu export terminal — placing NATO-member personnel in direct combat defense of the Saudi monarchy’s oil infrastructure.

Key facts:

  • Saudi Arabia struck Houthi-held Hodeidah on Friday in retaliation for the blockade declaration.
  • The Houthis earlier claimed strikes on two Saudi oil tankers, the Encelia and the Layla, for “violating” the blockade.
  • Officials warn that simultaneous disruption of the Bab al-Mandeb Strait and the Strait of Hormuz — the two chokepoints now under attack — risks a major global oil-supply shock.
  • Yemen’s population, already enduring one of the world’s worst humanitarian emergencies after a decade of Saudi-led, US-armed bombardment, absorbs the retaliatory violence.

3. Israel controls nearly 70 percent of Gaza nine months into the “ceasefire”

Nine months after the October 10, 2025 ceasefire, Israel now controls close to 70 percent of the Gaza Strip — up from roughly half when the truce was brokered — and is expanding a physical barrier system across the territory while continuing near-daily lethal strikes. What is presented in the Western press as “truce tensions” is the methodical consolidation of military occupation and depopulation.

Key facts:

  • The Palestinian Ministry of Health reports at least 1,185 Palestinians killed and 3,816 injured by Israeli fire since the ceasefire took effect.
  • Israel has violated the ceasefire at least 3,795 times, according to tallies compiled by Al Jazeera.
  • Israel killed two Hamas security officials in Deir al-Balah in the past 24 hours. Aid organizations warn that more than two-thirds of Gaza’s population will face acute hunger by year’s end as funding shortfalls deepen and Israel continues to restrict humanitarian entry.
  • In the occupied West Bank, settlers torched two mosques over the weekend and Israeli forces detained more than 70 Palestinians in raids. At least four Palestinians were killed Friday in Tal when settlers and soldiers opened fire.
  • Demolition of homes and civilian infrastructure continues as the barrier network is entrenched.

4. Europe’s “firewave”: 360,000 evacuated in France and Spain amid record heat

More than 360,000 people have been evacuated as wildfires swept southwestern France and northern Spain — among the largest evacuations in French history. Authorities deployed 2,500 firefighters, 1,500 military personnel and nearly 1,200 police. An incoming heat wave threatens temperatures above 40°C (104°F) across the affected zone.

The WSWS Perspective column by Thomas Scripps today reports that evacuation of Bordeaux itself — a city of 250,000, with 850,000 in the metropolitan area — was under active consideration, and that some 400,000 people in total have been displaced by the fires. A spokesperson for France’s National Firefighters Federation described the resource situation as “a David-versus-Goliath scenario.”

Key facts:

  • Estimates of heat-related deaths across Europe this summer range from 10,000 to 25,000, with some 277,000 workers injured in heat-related incidents.
  • Britain is experiencing its fourth heatwave of the year.
  • In East Africa, drought has left 1.5 million Ugandans affected, with 19 confirmed starvation deaths, while 40 million people across the Horn of Africa face acute food insecurity.
  • Simultaneous extreme-rainfall disasters struck the United States, Bangladesh (more than 50 deaths) and Chile in the same period.

5. Ukraine war internationalizes: Zelensky in Portsmouth, 30,000 more North Korean troops sought

Zelensky met new UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham aboard the carrier HMS Queen Elizabeth in Portsmouth on Monday — the first foreign-leader visit since Burnham took office a week earlier. Zelensky meets Trump at the White House Tuesday.

Zelensky also said Russia has requested an additional 30,000 troops and missile launchers from North Korea — more than double the number of North Koreans currently believed deployed. Preparations for the deployment have reportedly been underway in Russia’s Voronezh region, near the Ukrainian border, since June.

Kyiv claimed the vessel carried “drones and missiles from Iran to Russia”; Russia and Iran called it a civilian cargo ship. It had departed from Astrakhan.

The war has killed at least 59,000 people and displaced roughly 13 million since fighting began.

Key facts:

  • Drone strikes killed more than 1,000 civilians in the first five months of 2026 alone — an 81 percent increase in the number of drone attacks and a 600 percent jump in drone-related deaths compared with 2024-25, according to the UN human rights office.
  • More than 300 children have been killed or injured in the past six months, UNICEF reports. A strike on Al Musalamiya village in North Kordofan killed at least 16 civilians.
  • The proliferation of armed drones — supplied through the UAE and other external backers of the RSF, and through Egypt, Turkey and Iran on the army’s side — has transformed the conflict into a remote-killing war against the civilian population.

US measles cases hit a 35-year high

As of July 23, the CDC has confirmed 2,318 measles cases nationwide in 2026, already surpassing the 2,289 recorded for all of 2025 — the highest annual total since measles was declared eliminated in the United States in 2000. Seven states account for over 84 percent of cases, led by South Carolina (670) and Utah (522). 146 people (6 percent) have been hospitalized, and only 4 percent of cases occurred in fully vaccinated individuals.

The driver is straightforward: kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.5 percent, below the 95 percent herd-immunity threshold. This is the direct product of the anti-vaccine campaign now institutionalized at the top of the federal public health apparatus, combined with the gutting of local public health departments.

Inherited genetics shape cancer evolution after DNA damage (Nature)

Researchers led by Aitken et al., published July 22 in Nature, exposed four genetically distinct mouse strains to a single identical dose of the carcinogen diethylnitrosamine, then applied whole-genome sequencing, transcriptomics and histopathology. They found that inherited genetic background — not merely acquired mutations — determines the evolutionary trajectory of liver tumors. This provides direct experimental evidence for why individuals with comparable carcinogen exposure (smoking, UV radiation) develop radically different cancer outcomes, and strengthens the case for genetically stratified screening. The University of Cambridge, which led the work, framed it as explaining “why DNA damage from smoking and UV rays may cause cancer in some people but not others.”

Outbreak and surveillance roundup

  • COVID-19: As of July 22, infections are growing or likely growing in 37 states and declining in none, though national activity remains low. Circulating variants include the Omicron descendants XFG and NB.1.8.1 and other JN.1-lineage strains. The WHO reported 168 global COVID deaths in the four weeks ending June 28 — 143 of them in the United States, a figure that reflects both the US wave and the near-total collapse of global reporting.
  • H5N1: The virus has now been confirmed on every continent, following detection in a wild brown skua in Western Australia in June.   • Cyclosporiasis: Federal health officials announced a new outbreak affecting 72 people, part of a broader event now totaling roughly 4,100 infections across 41 states with 98 hospitalizations — the largest recorded US cyclosporiasis outbreak. The parasite is transmitted through contaminated fresh produce, and the outbreak follows sharp reductions in FDA produce-safety inspection capacity and mass deportations disrupting agricultural workforces.

First comprehensive federal AI framework lands in Congress

Representatives Jay Obernolte (R-CA) and Lori Trahan (D-MA) released a 269-page bipartisan discussion draft, the Great American Artificial Intelligence Act (GAAIA) — the first comprehensive federal AI governance framework proposed in Congress. The House Science Committee separately advanced 10 AI-related bills on June 25. The draft arrives days before the EU AI Act “Omnibus” transparency and cybersecurity provisions take effect on August 2. The essential question the draft does not address is the use of AI systems in military targeting and mass surveillance, where deployment has vastly outrun any regulatory framework.

Arts and culture

Bill Oddie, 1941-2026

Bill Oddie, the British comedian, musician, writer and naturalist, died Saturday at 85. Oddie belonged to the generation of Cambridge Footlights performers whose satire — beginning with the I’m Sorry, I’ll Read That Again radio company and reaching a mass audience with The Goodies (1970-82) — reflected the sharp erosion of deference in postwar British life. The Goodies was frequently dismissed as children’s slapstick, but its best episodes were pointed lampoons of the British establishment, the tabloid press, and the emergent racist right; the 1975 episode “South Africa,” on apartheid, was more explicitly political than anything then permitted in comparable US network comedy. Oddie wrote most of the series’ songs and scored a number of chart singles.

His second career, as a birder and wildlife broadcaster on Springwatch and Birding with Bill Oddie, was of a piece with the first. Chris Packham recalled that Oddie “was invariably funny and occasionally furious. He loved birds and loathed those who harmed them.” Co-star Graeme Garden remembered him as “a bit of a rebel” who would “challenge the status quo.” Oddie also spoke publicly about his clinical depression at a time when few figures in British broadcasting did.

The Lalique museum heist and the security of public collections

French investigators are still assessing losses from the July 5 burglary at the Musée Lalique in Wingen-sur-Moder, Alsace, where three masked men smashed six display cases and removed 27 pieces of jewelry and art in an eleven-minute operation. Losses are estimated at €4.5 million (roughly $5 million). Among the stolen works is René Lalique’s Femme libellule ailes ouvertes (Dragonfly Woman with Open Wings, 1898-1900), one of the defining objects of Art Nouveau — a work whose fusion of gold, enamel and glass deliberately refused the hierarchy separating “precious” materials from craft materials.

The museum’s vice-chairman disclosed that the alarm sounded but the contracted private security company failed to intervene immediately and did not alert the gendarmerie. Coming nine months after the Louvre jewel theft, the pattern points to a structural condition rather than a run of bad luck: publicly held collections outsourced to low-bid private security firms, in a period when art has become a preferred store of value for the wealthy and a target for organized theft. The immediate victims are the public, who lose access to works held in trust for them.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “Europe’s ‘firewave’ and global capitalism’s climate crisis” by Thomas Scripps — read in full on wsws.org.

Beyond the figures cited above, the Perspective makes a specific programmatic argument worth noting for editorial discussion: it rejects the reformist framing that the climate crisis can be resolved through wealth or income taxation on the existing oligarchy, on the grounds that the ruling class has demonstrated across two decades that it will not permit even minor concessions to be levied on accumulated wealth. Scripps argues that underfunded emergency services and disregarded scientific warnings are not administrative failures but the predictable output of a system in which the resources required for a planned response are privately owned. The conclusion drawn is the seizure of those resources by the working class and their subordination to scientific planning on a world scale.

As imperialist wars converge, Ukraine strikes Iranian cargo ship in the Caspian Sea — Kevin Reed

Reed’s article situates the Caspian strike within a specific material logic that the bourgeois coverage omits entirely. The Caspian corridor has become the essential artery for Russia-Iran trade under sanctions and the disruption of maritime routes; a Ukrainian strike there is not a tactical raid but an attack on the logistical spine linking two states now jointly targeted by Washington. Reed notes that Ukrainian long-range drones have damaged roughly one-third of Russian oil refining capacity, and that this — combined with the Hormuz and Bab al-Mandeb disruptions — is generating fuel shortages and inflationary pressure across the world market. The political conclusion is that the separate theatres are not separate: there is one developing world war, into which the working class of every country is being drawn on the terms of its own ruling class.

Trump Michigan speech: trade war and anti-communism — Patrick Martin

Martin’s analysis draws out what is distinct about the anti-communist rhetoric. The claim that communism exceeds two world wars and 9/11 as a threat is not directed at any existing organization commanding state power; it is a pre-emptive designation of domestic opposition. Martin compares the physical vocabulary applied to Democratic-run cities — “not clean,” “deformed” — to Nazi propaganda about degeneracy, and reads the speech as a declaration of political intent rather than campaign bombast. The article stresses that the anti-communist hysteria reflects ruling-class fear of the actual radicalization of American workers, not of any communist conspiracy — and that Trump’s claim to have been “better for autoworkers than your parents,” delivered in Michigan amid ongoing auto layoffs, is calculated as a generational wedge.

Chicago Public Schools announces 1,723 layoffs, exposing conspiracy between union and mayor — Joe Raymond

The article’s distinct contribution is its account of the April 2025 Chicago Teachers Union contract. Raymond argues the CTU leadership knew when it signed that the agreement would be followed by layoffs of this scale and concealed that from the membership. Mayor Brandon Johnson, a former CTU official, was installed precisely to be able to “deliver bad news to a friend” — Johnson’s own campaign phrase. CPS CEO Macquline King, Johnson’s appointee, has characterized the cuts as merely “disruptive.” Raymond describes the CTU’s public response as theatrical: complaints about overcrowded classrooms paired with a proposed remedy of further electoral effort rather than strike action. He connects this directly to the ICE seizure of custodian Josue Pire Hidalgo, arguing that layoffs and deportations are two prongs of the same offensive and that rank-and-file committees independent of the union apparatus are required to fight either.

The war at home: Record 2026-27 school closures — Jane Wise

Wise documents that more than 1,000 public schools closed during 2025-26 — up 35 percent from 755 four years earlier — with over 200 additional closures or consolidations approved for 2026-27. She tracks 14 districts: Cleveland (29 closures), Philadelphia (17), Atlanta (16), Pittsburgh and Houston (12 each), Austin (10), Miami-Dade (9), with further closures pending in St. Louis, Alaska and West Virginia. NCES projects public school enrollment will fall by nearly 4 million students between 2019 and 2031, driven not only by demographics but by deportations, childcare costs and diversion to charters and vouchers. Wise’s central comparison: the Trump administration’s FY2027 Pentagon request is $1.5 trillion, a 50 percent increase over the record $1 trillion FY2026 budget, while the entire Education Department budget of $80 billion amounts to roughly 5 percent of the Pentagon request. She notes that Democratic officials voted for every one of Trump’s war budgets while executing closures in the states they control.

UAW secretary-treasurer debate lays bare sharp conflicts in the apparatus — Shannon Jones

The July 23 UAW Monitor forum featured incumbent Margaret Mock, Region 4 Director Brandon Campbell, and Local 869 Financial Secretary Roc Ciers. The office controls $1.2 billion in union assets plus a $63 billion Retiree Health Care Trust Fund with roughly $58 billion in investments; the trust generated $202 million in net income in 2024. The conflict centers on Mock’s refusal to approve $500,000 in no-bid billboard contracts tied to Fain’s organizing drive. Mock’s quotations are the most revealing material: “What should I do when the president says, ‘Sign the fucking checks’?” and, on the power imbalance, “Shawn Fain… has tanks, grenades, AK-47s… What do I have? Rocks and dust.” Campbell alleged Mock caused $80 million in investment losses — charges the Monitor had previously discredited. Jones’s assessment: despite the venom, none of the three articulated any difference on the union’s orientation to the corporations or the Democratic Party. The fight is over control of the funds.

Maritime Union of Australia helps quash debate of Gaza genocide at Labor conference — Oscar Grenfell

At Labor’s 50th national conference in Adelaide, backbencher Ed Husic planned to move an amendment acknowledging the International Court of Justice’s 2024 finding of a “plausible case” that Israel is committing genocide. Anticipating pressure, two identical motions were submitted with different seconders — Allen Hicks of the NSW Electrical Trades Union and Paul Garrett of the MUA Sydney branch. Both withdrew on Saturday morning, killing the debate before it began. Grenfell’s point is that the MUA’s public pro-Palestinian posturing is precisely inverted in practice: the union that could physically halt Israeli shipping and arms exports through Australian ports instead functioned as the enforcer of Albanese’s prohibition on discussing the genocide at all.

Australian government plans harsher foreign interference laws — Mike Head

The Independent National Security Legislation Monitor has issued 20 recommendations to strengthen Australia’s 2018 foreign interference laws, which criminalize conduct undertaken “on behalf of, or in collaboration with, a foreign principal” and carry penalties up to 20 years. Head’s analysis focuses on the undefined term “collaboration,” which could encompass consultation, information-sharing or online communication with an overseas organization — meaning that campaigning against Australian participation in a US-led war could be criminalized if contact were made with any organization in the targeted country. Only two convictions have been obtained under the existing law, both in cases critics regard as dubious. The proposed expansions add offences for coerced repatriation and an aggravated interference charge.

Australia: Labor’s $10 billion housing scheme delivers under 1,500 homes in two years — Vicki Mylonas

The Housing Australia Future Fund, presented as the Albanese government’s flagship social program, has produced fewer than 1,500 completed dwellings in two years against a $10 billion allocation — a demonstration that the scheme’s actual function is the channeling of public capital into financial markets, with housing output as an incidental byproduct.

Deadly flooding strikes West Virginia and Texas as global climate catastrophe intensifies — Gavin Green

Storms dropped up to seven inches of rain in hours in West Virginia, producing flash floods comparable to the catastrophic 1985 event: at least two confirmed dead, an unknown number unaccounted for, 85 water rescues and over 300 National Guard members deployed. A local weather station has documented a 28 percent increase in the amount of water in hourly rainfall since 1970. In Texas, three died in the same region struck by last July’s floods that killed 135; nearly one trillion gallons of water fell over three days across three counties, over 200 people were rescued by boat and helicopter, and hundreds of homes and businesses were destroyed.

1 year since the IWA-RFC hearing on the death of Ronald Adams Sr. — Jerry White

White marks the anniversary of the International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees hearing into the death of Stellantis Dundee Engine worker Ronald Adams Sr., crushed on the job in April 2025. The article reviews what the hearing established that no corporate or government investigation would: the role of the UAW apparatus in suppressing safety complaints, and the pattern of preventable deaths across the auto industry. It is a reference point for the ICFI’s argument that workers’ own investigative bodies — not OSHA, not the union — are the only mechanism through which industrial killings are actually documented.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output over the past day is organized around the New York administration of Zohran Mamdani. “Trump and Mamdani Are Running Polar Opposite Bureaucracies” by Conor Lynch contrasts Trump’s administrative expansion on behalf of the wealthy with Mamdani’s effort to reduce bureaucratic obstacles to benefits access. The framework is administrative competence: two bureaucracies, one better managed than the other. The class character of the capitalist state apparatus — which Mamdani now administers, including the NYPD — does not enter the analysis. This is the essential function of Jacobin’s Mamdani coverage: to present the occupation of a municipal executive office within the Democratic Party as the concrete content of “socialism.”

“Socialists Aren’t Naive About Human Nature” by Matt McManus answers the perennial conservative objection by arguing that socialists “want a system that guards against tendencies to greed and domination.” The formulation is revealing: socialism is presented as a superior institutional design for managing fixed human dispositions, rather than as the resolution of a specific historical contradiction between socialized production and private appropriation. This is the language of liberal political philosophy, not Marxism, and it dovetails with the journal’s practical orientation toward reform of the existing state.

“DOGE May Have Given You Diarrhea” by Hayley Brown links the administration’s assault on farmworkers and federal employees to the record cyclosporiasis outbreak — a genuinely useful connection, though the article’s remedy is restored agency staffing rather than any mobilization of the workers involved.

Also notable: “In Morocco, Royalist Authorities Silence Critical Rap Lyrics” by Rona Lorimer documents the arrest of rapper Mehdi Black Wind, and Ben Burgis’s “Mamdani Is Right. Netanyahu Belongs Behind Bars” (July 25), which endorses the mayor’s arrest rhetoric.

Assessment: The day’s output consolidates the line that the Mamdani administration is the model for American socialism, with theoretical material tailored to make that identification philosophically respectable.

Left Voice

“Stand against the State’s Attack on The People’s Forum and BreakThrough News” (July 23) responds to House Ways and Means Committee subpoenas issued July 22 demanding internal communications and financial records from both organizations. Left Voice’s opposition is unequivocal and correct. But the article frames the attack as an assault on free speech and civil liberties by a “MAGA-led” committee, rather than situating it within the bipartisan construction of a state apparatus for suppressing anti-war opposition — an apparatus built up under Democratic as well as Republican administrations, and one whose most consequential recent applications have been against opponents of the Gaza genocide.

A companion piece by Emma Lee and M. Carlstad (July 24) examines Vietnam’s military-industrial cooperation with Israel alongside its rhetorical support for the Palestinian struggle — a genuinely worthwhile subject, treated as an exposure of hypocrisy rather than as a demonstration of what national-bourgeois “anti-imperialism” amounts to in the era of globalized production.

Assessment: More willing than most of the American pseudo-left to take up state repression directly, but consistently frames these as democratic-rights questions detachable from the war drive that generates them.

Socialist Alternative

“Zohran Says Netanyahu Not Welcome In NYC: Kick Him Out With Mass Action!” (July 23) addresses Mamdani’s campaign pledge to arrest Netanyahu, who faces ICC charges, should he visit New York for September’s UN General Assembly. Mamdani has now announced that his administration explored legal options and concluded the city lacks the authority. Socialist Alternative’s response is to accept this without criticism and substitute a call for “mass action” to make Netanyahu unwelcome. The organization has thereby converted a broken promise into an occasion for a mobilization it does not intend to lead in political opposition to the administration that broke it.

“The Democrats Won’t Save Us — The Case For A New Party” (July 8) argues for a new party of the left. The proposal is not accompanied by any assessment of the program such a party would fight for, or of the record of the DSA-Democratic Party fusion that Socialist Alternative has itself accommodated to in New York.

Assessment: The organization is attempting to maintain formal independence from the Democrats while functioning as a left flank of the Mamdani administration.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

Socialist Worker’s coverage of the Burnham premiership runs across three weeks and one register: hopeful uncertainty. Judy Cox’s “Burnham promises change, but can he deliver?” (July 17) reports his promise of “the biggest change in 40 years.” Alex Callinicos’s “Burnham will need more than words” (July 20) allows that Burnham is “a much more skilful politician” than Starmer. “Burnham cabinet is a Starmer tribute act” (July 22) and “Will Burnham drive Labour in a new direction?” (July 23) continue posing as an open question what the composition of the cabinet has already answered.

Assessment: Three weeks of asking whether a Labour prime minister installed by the same apparatus that installed Starmer, retaining his cabinet and his commitments to NATO rearmament, will deliver change. The persistence of the question is the political line: the SWP’s orientation requires that Labour remain a potential vehicle.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

“The many faces of Andy Burnham — how long until the mask slips?” and “Burnham’s ‘Number 10 North’ and the myth behind ‘Manchesterism’” are considerably sharper than the SWP’s treatment, dismissing Burnham’s regional devolution record as insufficient reform and predicting rapid crisis. “Farage’s sham resignation: bin all the liars, crooks, and racists!” treats the Clacton manoeuvre. The weakness is that the analysis terminates in the prediction that workers will be disappointed and then turn to the RCP — a syndicalist-flavoured substitute for a worked-out revolutionary program and any accounting of the Labour Party’s role in the imperialist war drive.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL’s July output centers on its independent congressional campaign in the Bronx — Andre Easton, a teacher, standing against Ritchie Torres in November — and on opposition to National Guard deployments and ICE raids in Los Angeles. The campaign is presented as the concrete alternative to the Democrats. What is absent is any programmatic content beyond a list of demands, or any perspective for the international unification of the working class; the orientation remains toward pressure politics in a single district.
