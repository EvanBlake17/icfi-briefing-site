1. US-Iran war enters day 104 as Iran closes Strait of Hormuz after second day of US strikes

The US war on Iran entered its 104th day with a second consecutive round of American airstrikes and a formal Iranian declaration that the Strait of Hormuz is closed to all shipping. US Central Command fired 49 Tomahawk cruise missiles at Iranian targets on June 10, some striking within 40 miles of Tehran, hitting radar, air defense, surveillance and communications systems. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps retaliated against US bases in Kuwait, Bahrain and Jordan; Jordan said it intercepted some 20 missiles near a US air base, reporting no casualties.

Iran’s joint military command announced the complete closure of the strait, through which roughly 20% of global oil trade passes, warning that any vessel attempting passage “will be shot at.” US forces disabled the Palau-flagged tanker M/T Settebello for a second straight day in the Gulf of Oman, firing precision munitions into its engine room for violating the blockade.

On the WSWS, Andre Damon documented that the renewed offensive — beginning at 5:15 p.m. Eastern Wednesday — followed a campaign that opened with the February assassination of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, noting that after 100 days “the Iranian government remains intact, the Strait of Hormuz is uncompromised, and no signed agreements have been produced.”

Key facts: - Trump warned Iran would be bombed “tomorrow night” if it rejected the US deal: “now they will have to pay the price.” - Tuesday’s strikes destroyed two water reservoirs in Hormozgan province, leaving 20,000 residents without safe water in 45–50°C heat (WSWS). - Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth declared US willingness to “negotiate with bombs.” - Kuwait closed its airspace and suspended commercial flights; US embassies in Jordan ordered citizens to shelter in place. - An Israeli strike in Sidon, Lebanon killed two; projectiles from Lebanon fell near Israeli troops in the south.

2. US consumer inflation jumps to 4.2 percent; real wages fall

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported consumer price inflation accelerated to 4.2 percent in May 2026 — the highest in three years — up from 2.4% in February, before the war on Iran began, then 3.3% in March and 3.8% in April. Energy drove the surge: fuel oil rose 58.9% year-over-year, gasoline 40.5%, airline fares 26.7% and overall energy 23.5%. But the squeeze extends across the economy: meat is up 7.6%, fruits and vegetables 6.1% (tomatoes alone 25%), electricity 5.9% and hospital services 5.7%.

A separate BLS report found real wages fell behind prices for a second consecutive month, with the gap widening to 0.7% in May from 0.3% in April — the largest one-month real wage cut since 2023. In today’s Perspective, Patrick Martin argues the gasoline spike alone has erased more than a year of nominal wage gains, returning real wages to where they stood at Trump’s January 2025 inauguration.

Asked about the CPI at a White House briefing, Trump said: “You know what I really love? I love inflation,” calling gas prices “the one bad thing” in “the best economy we’ve ever hit.” As he spoke, the Dow fell more than 900 points on fears war-driven inflation would block Federal Reserve rate cuts.

3. Energy shock: Fitch downgrades global outlook as markets price a “long grind”

The Hormuz closure — in effect intermittently since February 28 and now fully reimposed — has produced what energy analysts call the worst global supply shock in decades. Fitch Ratings cut its global sovereign sector outlook from “neutral” to “deteriorating,” citing the war’s drag on growth, elevated inflation and higher bond yields. Middle East producers have cut crude output by more than 11 million barrels per day versus pre-conflict levels.

Key facts: - Brent crude averaged $107/barrel in May; Wood Mackenzie and the EIA forecast roughly $105 for June–July. - Markets are shifting from pricing a near-term ceasefire to a prolonged conflict, per CNBC/Morgan Stanley analysis. - The IMF warned the war “darkens the global economic outlook”; global growth is now forecast at 2.7% for 2026, below the ~3.5% pre-pandemic average. - Flows are projected to resume only slowly in Q3 2026, with prices potentially falling to $79/barrel by 2027 if hostilities ease.

4. Anti-immigrant riots spread across the UK after Belfast stabbing

Racist riots that erupted in Belfast on June 9 spread to Glasgow, Edinburgh and Southampton, with police deploying water cannon for a second consecutive night on June 10–11. The unrest followed a June 8 knife attack in which Hadi Alodid, a 30-year-old Sudanese asylum seeker, attacked Stephen Ogilvie with a kitchen knife; the victim lost his left eye and sustained damage to the right. Masked protesters set buses, vehicles and homes on fire across Northern Ireland.

Far-right groups seized on the incident to mobilize nationally, echoing the pattern of the 2024 UK riots. Alodid, granted a five-year UK visa in 2023 after applying for asylum, is charged with attempted murder, possession of a knife and making threats to kill. Prime Minister Keir Starmer called the attack “sickening” but condemned the riots as “abhorrent scenes of violence.”

The underlying drivers — chronic housing shortages, stagnant wages and austerity under successive governments — create the conditions far-right organizers exploit, conditions to which the Starmer Labour government offers no answer.

5. Trump signs $70 billion ICE and Border Patrol funding bill

The House passed the “Secure America Act” 214–212 on a party-line vote, allocating $38 billion to ICE, $26 billion to Border Patrol/CBP and $6 billion to DOJ/DHS — bringing total immigration enforcement spending since Trump’s return to roughly $246 billion. Trump signed the bill Wednesday morning. The legislation contains no operational restrictions: no warrant requirements, no ban on masked agents, no limits on school or workplace deployments.

On the WSWS, Jacob Crosse notes that while every Democrat voted against the final bill, the party earlier engineered a procedural separation of ICE/CBP funding from broader DHS appropriations, effectively guaranteeing passage through reconciliation. Democratic leadership’s public criticism focused on the absence of “violent felon” targeting requirements rather than opposing mass deportation as such.

6. Gaza ceasefire violated more than 3,200 times as toll passes 72,991

The third Gaza ceasefire, in effect since October 10, 2025, has been violated at least 3,201 times through June 9, according to Gaza’s Government Media Office. The total killed since October 7, 2023 reached 72,991, including 20,179 children, per the Palestinian Ministry of Health; Israeli attacks since the ceasefire have killed at least 981 Palestinians and injured 3,104. The UN Security Council held an open debate June 10 calling for consolidation of the ceasefire and a halt to West Bank settlement activity.

Key facts: - 94% of Gaza’s hospitals are destroyed or damaged; 51% of essential medicines are at zero stock. - Only 36% of allocated aid trucks (52,129 of 144,000) have entered Gaza since the ceasefire began. - Approximately 1.6 million people — 77% of the population — face acute food insecurity. - The UN Secretary-General warned the regional escalation “reverberates across borders and continents.”

7. World Cup opens at Estadio Azteca amid Mexican teacher protests

The 2026 FIFA World Cup opened June 11 at Mexico City’s Estadio Azteca, the first venue in history to host three World Cups (1970, 1986, 2026), with an opening ceremony featuring Shakira, J Balvin and Maná before a crowd approaching 100,000. Mexico faced South Africa in the opener; the expanded 48-team tournament is being held across the US, Canada and Mexico.

The spectacle unfolded against protests by Mexican teachers’ unions, who demonstrated outside official fan zones over education funding and chronic underpayment. President Claudia Sheinbaum faced criticism for heavy tournament expenditures while public-sector workers remain underpaid; Mexico City set up 18 fan zones, with the Zócalo fan fest at risk of disruption. The contrast between FIFA and corporate profits and the living standards of Mexican workers is a direct class story buried beneath the sports coverage.

8. Global armed conflicts reach highest level since World War II

Data published June 9 shows the number of active armed conflicts worldwide is at its highest level since World War II, driven by the US-Iran war, Ukraine-Russia, Gaza, Sudan and the DRC, among others. The IMF calculates that the Hormuz closure and attacks on Gulf energy infrastructure have removed a large share of global hydrocarbon supply, raising the prospect of a major energy crisis if hostilities continue. Trade uncertainty is now cited as the top concern by more than 75% of manufacturers. The proliferation of wars reflects a systemic crisis of the capitalist order and the breakdown of the post-1945 framework of inter-state relations.

Magnitude 7.8 earthquake kills at least 46 in the southern Philippines

A 7.8-magnitude earthquake struck off the coast of Sarangani on June 8, killing at least 46, injuring nearly 500 and displacing 32,000 in and around General Santos City and Sarangani Province on Mindanao. Hundreds of aftershocks complicated rescue efforts, and more than 6,000 schools were affected on the first day of the academic year for 3.2 million students. As Ben McGrath notes on the WSWS, building codes requiring structures to withstand magnitude-8 quakes were systematically violated through cost-cutting and inadequate oversight, and a projected 7.2-magnitude quake along the West Valley Fault beneath Manila’s 14 million residents could kill 33,000 and destroy 168,000 buildings.

Peru’s runoff election too close to call amid US pressure

Peru’s June 7 presidential runoff produced an extraordinarily tight result: with most ballots counted, Roberto Sánchez of Juntos por el Perú led Keiko Fujimori roughly 50.1% to 49.9%, separated by fewer than 5,000 votes, with 1.25 million blank or null ballots — roughly three of every ten voters rejecting both candidates. US Ambassador Bernie Navarro openly warned Washington would monitor the outcome, linking it to American strategic interests over the Chinese-built Chancay megaport and copper trade. As Francis Portocarrero details on the WSWS, the US has invested $1.5 billion to relocate Peru’s naval base near Chancay and deploy 1,200 troops on Peruvian territory; neither candidate represents a genuine alternative amid roughly 30% poverty and 43% underemployment.

Kosovo holds its third election in a year

Kosovo held its third parliamentary election in just over a year on June 7. PM Albin Kurti’s Vetëvendosje won approximately 43% — down from 51% in December 2025 — while the Democratic Party of Kosovo took 21.7% and the Democratic League of Kosovo 18%. Turnout collapsed to 36.88%, about eight points below December. The election was triggered by the assembly’s failure to elect a president on March 5, which led President Vjosa Osmani to dissolve parliament. Kurti cannot govern alone, and the EU integration framework both major parties endorse imposes fiscal austerity, privatization and labor deregulation — a context obscured by horse-race coverage.

Franco-German fighter jet program collapses

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz scrapped the manned-fighter component of the Future Combat Air System (FCAS), the €100+ billion Franco-German sixth-generation program agreed by Macron and Merkel in 2017, informing Macron on June 6 on the sidelines of the EU Western Balkans Summit before leaking the decision and leaving Spain uninformed. The two countries will continue jointly developing the “combat cloud” architecture. As Peter Schwarz writes on the WSWS, the failure stems not merely from years of Airbus–Dassault disputes over workshare and intellectual property — Dassault CEO Eric Trappier demanding a prime-contractor role — but from fundamental Franco-German strategic divergence, with Germany committing €96.5 billion to Ukraine and pursuing Eastern European dominance while France fears German military capabilities eclipsing its own. Der Spiegel defended abandoning the project to avoid “new dependencies on France,” signaling accelerating unilateral German rearmament.

India-Pakistan tensions persist after May aerial war

The four-day aerial conflict in May 2026 between nuclear-armed India and Pakistan ended with both sides claiming victory and structural causes unresolved. India has put the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance — Pakistan calls it an “existential threat” to a population dependent on the Indus, Chenab and Jhelum rivers. Military modernization is accelerating on both sides, and the Council on Foreign Relations flags a high risk of renewed confrontation. Washington-New Delhi ties have soured under Trump’s second term, and the US has not played its prior stabilizing mediating role.

FBI raids pro-Palestinian activists at University of Michigan

Federal agents raided homes across Southeast Michigan, unsealing a secretly filed 63-page indictment against eight activists aged 21–28 tied to the University of Michigan groups TAHRIR Coalition and SAFE; seven were arrested. The Justice Department charged them with conspiracy to transmit threats — two facing witness-intimidation charges carrying 20-year maximums — in what it called a coordinated campaign to force the university to divest from Israel, citing spray-painting, broken windows and threatening notes at the homes of regents and university officials between 2024 and 2025. CAIR-Michigan called the raids disproportionate and politically motivated. As Barry Grey writes on the WSWS, this continues a bipartisan repression campaign and sets a precedent threatening labor organizing and political dissent broadly.

Auto parts and transit workers move toward strikes

A wave of industrial action is building as inflation erodes wages. In Saginaw, 1,700 Nexteer Automotive workers rejected a third consecutive UAW-backed contract and voted 86% to strike, with the union refusing to authorize a walkout; 1,000 American Axle workers are on strike in Three Rivers, Michigan; and more than 90% of Dana Corp. workers across Indiana, Ohio and Kansas City rejected UAW-negotiated deals. In New York, more than 40,000 MTA subway and bus workers have remained on the job since their contract expired May 16; the MTA is offering a 2% annual raise — less than half the city’s 4.6% inflation rate — while seeking to more than double health insurance costs and curb overtime and sick-leave eligibility. TWU International is pushing state legislation to block the MTA’s expansion of one-person train operation.

Science, technology, and public health

  • US measles cases pass 2,030 — worst year in three decades; 92% of cases unvaccinated, putting elimination status at risk.
  • H5N1 confirmed in poultry across 12 states — cumulative bird losses past 100 million; 71 human cases since February 2024.
  • Triple-journal release validates finerenone beyond diabetes — NEJM, JAMA and Lancet publish simultaneously, broadening the eligible population to tens of millions.
  • AI compute arms race accelerates on debt financing — OpenAI eyes a 10-GW Ohio campus; China plans a ~$295B domestic network.

The most significant public health story is the continuing US measles resurgence. As of June 4, the CDC reported 2,030 confirmed cases across 40 jurisdictions and 30 separate outbreaks, with 93% (1,890) outbreak-associated and 127 patients (6%) hospitalized. Over 92% of cases were in unvaccinated individuals. At this pace 2026 is on track to be the worst US measles year in more than three decades, threatening the country’s elimination status — a direct consequence of collapsing vaccination rates and the gutting of public health infrastructure.

In a notable scientific development, three studies published simultaneously in the New England Journal of Medicine, JAMA and The Lancet — timed to the European Renal Association Congress in Glasgow — show finerenone, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, significantly slows kidney function decline and reduces risk of kidney failure, CKD progression, heart failure and cardiovascular death in both diabetic and non-diabetic chronic kidney disease. The JAMA arm extended findings to glomerular-disease CKD; the Lancet pooled individual-participant data across trials. The simultaneous triple-journal release is unprecedented for the drug class and broadens the eligible population from millions to tens of millions globally.

Roundup: - H5N1 bird flu: USDA confirmed HPAI in commercial poultry across 12 states, pushing cumulative bird losses past 100 million; 71 human cases have been reported since February 2024, all occupational. NIOSH expanded its “Gear Up, Wash Up, Step Up” campaign nationally to protect an estimated 500,000 agricultural workers. The CDC rates public health risk as low but occupational risk remains real amid multi-host spread across poultry, dairy cattle and sporadic human spillover. - COVID-19: Activity is declining or likely declining in 41 states; forecast ED visits for the week ending June 13 are ~0.087%, down from 0.11% the week of May 30. CDC modeling flags the South and West for possible summer increases; no dominant immune-evasive variant has emerged. - AI policy: Trump’s June 2 executive order “Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security” asks companies to voluntarily submit frontier models for government testing 30 days before release — narrowed from a mandatory version after industry objections; the NSA has 60 days to develop a classified benchmarking process. Anthropic warned this week its systems may soon reach a threshold of self-improvement without human direction, even as Trump floated direct government equity stakes in OpenAI, Anthropic and xAI. - AI infrastructure: OpenAI is negotiating a 10-gigawatt Ohio campus with potential Nvidia backing; China is preparing a ~$295 billion national AI data center network using domestic suppliers (chiefly Huawei) to reduce US chip dependence.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural story is the deepening confrontation between artists and the Trump administration’s assault on federal cultural institutions. Painter Amy Sherald — known for her official portrait of Michelle Obama — canceled her upcoming exhibition at the Smithsonian’s National Portrait Gallery, citing censorship concerns tied to content directives at federal museums. It is the highest-profile artist refusal yet in a widening standoff. The cancellation comes as the National Endowment for the Arts has terminated more than $27 million in grants nationwide while the administration moves to eliminate the NEA, NEH and the Institute of Museum and Library Services entirely and rebrand the Kennedy Center — the most sweeping dismantling of federal arts infrastructure since the endowments were established in 1965. This is not bureaucratic restructuring but a deliberate effort to subordinate cultural production to a nationalist political agenda and starve independent and critical art of resources.

A sharply contrasting model of cultural work was visible at the WSWS, where Andrea Peters reviewed the Oscar-winning documentary Mr. Nobody Against Putin (Pavel Talankin, David Borenstein). The film portrays life in Karabash, a heavily polluted Russian industrial town, with genuine humanist sympathy — “Kids, neatly dressed in their uniforms, sing off-key in the school chorus” — but, Peters argues, ultimately serves Western geopolitical interests by exposing Russian militarism while remaining silent on Ukraine’s elevation of fascists, NATO’s role and US policy. She notes the film was “an operation organized in London” by the BBC, and that director Talankin secured asylum in Prague while the Karabash youth he filmed “remain stuck in an industrial wasteland.” The review is a model of distinguishing real artistic content from the imperialist framing imposed upon it.

Roundup: - Anthony Stewart Head, the British actor beloved as Giles in Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Rupert Mannion in Ted Lasso, died at 72 — a generational touchstone across two decades of television. - The PEN/Faulkner Award for Fiction went to Mahreen Sohail’s debut Small Scale Sinners, while Virginia Evans took the PEN/Hemingway Award for The Correspondent — an unusually strong year for first-time novelists. - National Queer Theater’s Criminal Queerness Festival (June 9–27), the official theater event of NYC Pride, this year centers Arab queer voices from countries where queerness is criminalized — a direct artistic response to repression. - Bonnaroo opened in Manchester, Tennessee (June 11–14) with headliners including Skrillex, The Strokes, Noah Kahan, Turnstile and Kesha, marking the start of a packed summer festival season conducted, as ever, under the logic of the corporate concert industry.

(Covered in Top Stories.) ICE abducts mother at Ann Arbor middle school — Anthony Callahan

ICE agents detained a mother as she dropped her child at Scarlett Middle School in Ann Arbor on June 3 — the second known arrest of a parent at that school — and nearly $5,000 was raised for the family within days. Callahan reports that nearly two-thirds of the more than 3,100 people arrested by Detroit ICE from January to July 2025 had no criminal convictions, underscoring that enforcement targets the working class broadly rather than “criminals.” He criticizes Democratic responses as performative given the party’s support for expanded ICE funding, and argues immigration enforcement is designed to divide workers and establish authoritarian precedents affecting all citizens. The incident concretizes the consequences of the $70 billion ICE/CBP bill signed the same week.

Preface to “The Ukraine War and the Fight for Socialism: The Case of Bogdan Syrotiuk” — Johannes Stern

Stern introduces a compiled volume on Bogdan Syrotiuk, a Ukrainian socialist arrested by the SBU in April 2024 on charges of “high treason under martial law” for opposing the war from a socialist internationalist standpoint. The prosecution’s case has largely collapsed after independent linguistic experts refuted claims that his writings supported the Kremlin. The book is organized in three sections: ICFI defense statements, Syrotiuk’s own writings and speeches, and broader Trotskyist analysis of the Ukraine war. Stern stresses Germany’s particular responsibility given Berlin’s central role in supporting the Kiev regime, and concludes that Syrotiuk’s freedom “will not be won through appeals to the governments that are waging and supporting this war.” The case has received no significant coverage in mainstream Western media, making the WSWS the sole consistent international defender.

Students across Chile protest criminalization of youth and education cuts — Mauricio Saavedra

Thousands of high school and university students marched across Chilean cities on June 3 against President José Antonio Kast’s austerity measures and a legislative package — the Protected Schools Bill, Registry of Vandals Bill, and Criminal Liability of Adolescents Reform — that Saavedra characterizes as the criminalization of youth protest. He traces the crisis to Chile’s privatized, Pinochet-era voucher system, under which municipal school enrollment fell from 58% to 33% of students between 1990 and 2010, and notes the country spends only 2–3% of its health budget on mental health versus OECD standards. The March 2026 killing of school inspector María Reyes by a student with documented mental health conditions is contextualized within the youth mental health crisis rather than used to justify repressive legislation. (Anglophone bourgeois press did not cover the demonstrations.)

Sri Lankan President pledges separate courts for Buddhist monks — Pani Wijesiriwardena

President Anura Kumara Dissanayake announced at the Vesak festival plans to amend the Temporalities Ordinance so monks accused of crimes would first face internal Sangha courts before civil prosecution — driven by chief prelates seeking to avoid the “embarrassment” of seeing robed monks arrested. The proposal follows 22 monk arrests for cannabis smuggling, a senior monk’s child-abuse charges, and nearly 300 abuse allegations against monks in three years per the State Child Protection Bureau. Wijesiriwardena argues this represents the JVP/NPP government’s capitulation to Buddhist institutional interests — invoking ancient Sinhala kingship as precedent — and is incompatible with democratic equality and secular governance, situating it within broader patterns of religious supremacism used to consolidate state power against the working class.

4 workers dead at Palmetto: the safety crisis and the privatization drive — USPS Workers Rank-and-File Committee

The committee documents four deaths at the Palmetto Regional Processing and Distribution Center over two years — Shannon Barnes (August 2024), Eric Smith (June 2025), Russell Scruggs Jr. (November 2025), and most recently Demarcus Little, a 45-year-old father of two. An independent inquiry found deaths were prolonged by blocked cell signals delaying 911 calls and an absence of first aid kits. The statement argues Postmaster General David Steiner — a former FedEx board member — is using a manufactured financial crisis to freeze hiring, suspend pension contributions, plan office closures and execute a Wells Fargo-outlined five-step privatization roadmap, while union officials collaborate by accepting pension suspensions and ignoring safety failures. It advances seven demands, including keeping USPS fully public, worker control over safety, and inflation-busting wages, within the framework of the International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees.

Hersheypark workers reject 4th tentative agreement, vote to strike — Douglas Lyons

Over 200 skilled trades workers at Hersheypark rejected a fourth tentative agreement on June 3, and more than 500 Local 464 Bakery, Confectionery and Tobacco Workers members subsequently voted to authorize a strike. The workers have been without a contract since mid-March; management’s successive proposals offer “largely cosmetic changes,” leaving retroactive pay, shift differentials, health insurance premiums, retirement benefits and job security unresolved. Lyons connects the struggle to auto-parts militancy at Nexteer and American Axle and criticizes the union bureaucracy for deliberately delaying strike authorization to protect management interests.

Pseudo-left press review

  • Jacobin / DSA — opposes the Iran war as Trump’s “illegal and ill-planned” blunder while framing it as mismanaged statecraft rather than systemic imperialism.
  • Left Voice — calls for a “mass movement” against the war but subordinates it to a defensist “defend Iran” line and feminist/identity framings.
  • Liberation News / PSL — channels anti-war sentiment into ANSWER Coalition street protests while crediting “Iranian resistance” and the theocracy’s survival.
  • Socialist Worker / SWP (UK) — reduces opposition to the war to public meetings and pressure on Starmer rather than independent working-class struggle.
  • Socialist Alternative / IMT — combine anti-war rhetoric with orientation to left-reformist electoralism and pressure politics.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin continues to frame the Iran war chiefly as a Trump policy failure. Its recent analyses — “Why the US Is Losing the Iran War” and “US, Israel Target Civilians as Iran Escalates War to Survive” — characterize Trump as having “started an illegal and ill-planned war on Tehran” that he is “losing control of,” emphasizing that the assassination campaign “did not cause the Islamic Republic to crumble.” The political line treats imperialist war as a question of incompetent strategy and lost hegemony rather than the objective drive of US capitalism in crisis. By locating the problem in Trump’s recklessness, Jacobin implicitly counterposes a more rational, managed imperialism — the standpoint of the Democratic Party wing it orbits. There is no call for the independent mobilization of the working class against both parties; the assumed agency remains electoral pressure and a restoration of “competent” foreign policy. Overall orientation: left cover for a section of the foreign-policy establishment dismayed at the war’s mismanagement.

Left Voice

Left Voice’s coverage — “We Need a Mass Movement to Defeat Trump’s Imperialist War on Iran” and “Defend Iran, but Don’t Support the Theocracy” — correctly identifies the war as imperialist and rooted in US decline and inter-imperialist rivalry, and formally calls for working-class independence. But its “defend Iran” defensism risks adapting to bourgeois-nationalist frameworks, and its parallel emphasis on “Feminists Must Fight Imperialism in Iran” channels the question into identity categories rather than the unified international class struggle. The Morenoite PTS tradition substitutes a stream of “mass movement” appeals and protest activism for the patient construction of a revolutionary party. Overall orientation: radical phraseology combined with an essentially propagandistic, movementist practice.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL, through Liberation News and the ANSWER Coalition, has organized “Hands Off Iran!” protests in major cities (see “PSL Statement: Stop Trump’s War on Iran!” and “Why Trump failed in his criminal war against Iran”). The line credits the war’s stalling to “public pressure and the Iranian people’s resistance,” lending uncritical political support to the bourgeois Iranian regime and presenting the theocracy’s survival as a victory for the oppressed. This campist orientation subordinates the Iranian and US working classes to one capitalist state against another. Overall orientation: anti-imperialism reduced to support for a rival bourgeois government, with protest pressure substituting for a revolutionary perspective.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP’s output remains organized around public meetings — “Terror in Iran: Resisting imperialist war in the Middle East” and “Will the war on Iran crash the world economy?” — that correctly note Starmer’s collusion in the war. But the practical perspective amounts to pressure on the Labour government and broad “stop the war” coalition-building rather than a struggle to break workers from Labour and the trade union apparatus. Notably absent, amid the Belfast riots, is any call to mobilize the working class independently against the far right. Overall orientation: protest politics oriented toward reforming, not breaking with, the Labour bureaucracy.

Socialist Alternative and the IMT

Socialist Alternative (ISA) frames the war and the cost-of-living crisis through demands on existing institutions and electoral openings for left candidates, while the IMT/Revolutionary Communist Party press emphasizes abstract calls for “communism” alongside an orientation to recruiting from radicalizing youth. Neither advances a concrete program for rank-and-file committees independent of the union apparatus of the kind raised in today’s WSWS reporting on Nexteer, USPS and the MTA. Overall orientation: both adapt, in different registers, to left-reformist and movementist pressure politics rather than the independent organization of the working class. None of the tendencies surveyed referenced the ICFI or the Syrotiuk case.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Belfast riots, the housing crisis, and Starmer’s failure

The anti-immigrant riots spreading across the UK are being framed by the press as a law-and-order problem, obscuring the material roots — chronic housing shortages, stagnant wages and austerity — that far-right organizers exploit. The Starmer government’s repressive response, with no answer to the housing crisis, echoes the dynamics of the 2024 riots and warrants a class analysis distinguishing it from both far-right reaction and Labour’s law-and-order posture.

Recommended reading CBS News Belfast protests erupt again as police deploy water cannons

2. Working-class impact of the Hormuz energy shock in the Global South

The Hormuz closure and Brent near $105/barrel are reported as financial-market stories, but the removal of 11 million barrels per day hits the global poor hardest through fuel, transport, fertilizer and food costs. A piece tracing the energy shock into household budgets across the Global South would concretize the WSWS analysis of who pays for imperialist war.

Recommended reading Wood Mackenzie Strait of Hormuz closure risks greatest global energy supply shock in decades

3. Mega-bankruptcies and the tariff-driven destruction of jobs

Chapter 11 filings by companies with over $1 billion in assets rose 33% year-over-year, with tariffs cited as a distress driver in clean energy, aviation and auto parts. Behind each filing — Spirit Airlines, Sunnova, Marelli — are mass layoffs and pension threats. A report connecting the bankruptcy wave to trade-war policy and its toll on workers would complement today’s labor coverage.

Recommended reading CFO Dive ‘Mega bankruptcies’ jump as tariffs add pressure

4. US measles resurgence and the collapse of public health

With 2,030 confirmed cases and 92% in the unvaccinated, 2026 is on track to be the worst US measles year in three decades, threatening elimination status. This is the direct product of anti-vaccine policy and the gutting of public health under the current administration — a story the WSWS is well positioned to analyze in class and scientific terms.

Recommended reading NBC News Measles outbreaks tracker, 2026

5. Attack on federal arts institutions and artist resistance

Amy Sherald’s cancellation of her National Portrait Gallery show, alongside $27 million in terminated NEA grants and moves to abolish the NEA, NEH and IMLS, represents the most sweeping assault on US cultural infrastructure since 1965. The WSWS framework on the social role of art and the defense of culture against authoritarian and nationalist attack makes this a natural subject.

Recommended reading artnet Trump’s impact on the arts

6. Wars at the highest level since 1945 — a systemic indictment of capitalism

New data confirms more active armed conflicts worldwide than at any point since World War II. Rather than treating each war in isolation, an integrating analysis tying the US-Iran war, Ukraine, Gaza, Sudan and the India-Pakistan flashpoint to the breakdown of the capitalist nation-state system would arm the working class with a global perspective.

Recommended reading NPR Data: armed conflicts at highest level since WWII
