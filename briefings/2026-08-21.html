1. Trump launches “economic D-Day” against Iran as UAE imposes total trade embargo

President Trump announced what he called “the most crushing economic operation ever” against Iran, declaring on social media that “This will be economic warfare and isolation on an unprecedented scale” and threatening “TREMENDOUS Economic Consequences” for any country whose financial institutions provide Tehran with a “lifeline.” The declaration came as the United Arab Emirates announced an indefinite suspension of all trade and financial transactions with Iran — accusing Iranian forces of firing two ballistic missiles at Emirati territory — and as the Treasury imposed a fresh round of sanctions on Hezbollah, redesignating the organization as an Iranian proxy. The measures come on roughly day 173 of the US-Iran war.

The escalation is the sharpest turn yet in a conflict that has already drawn Iranian missile fire against Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Jordan and the UAE, and has left the Strait of Hormuz under intermittent blockade. Iran’s foreign minister said the announcement was an attempt to distract from the crisis of US public finances.

Key facts:

  • Bilateral UAE-Iran trade was worth roughly $28 billion in 2024 — $21 billion in Iranian imports from the Emirates and $7 billion in Emirati imports from Iran. The UAE has functioned as Iran’s principal re-export channel under sanctions.
  • A Brandeis University economist told Foreign Policy that Washington “strongly encouraged or induced” the Emirati decision, pointing to a call between Secretary of State Marco Rubio and the UAE’s national security adviser.
  • A former adviser to Iran’s central bank warned the embargo could deepen the country’s economic contraction to roughly 5 percent this year.
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent promised “the toughest sanctions in history” on Iran — a threat that has been a principal driver of the oil price surge.

2. Russian missile and drone barrage kills at least 16 in Kyiv region

An overnight Russian attack combining dozens of cruise and ballistic missiles with 168 drones killed at least 16 people and wounded dozens more across Kyiv and the surrounding region. Residential buildings, warehouses, a school and a children’s hospital were among the sites struck. Ukrainian air defense claimed interception of nearly 90 percent of the drones and most of the cruise missiles; the ballistic interception rate was not disclosed.

Separately, the Zaporizhzhia nuclear power station lost external power for the 27th time since the beginning of the war, forcing operators to run emergency diesel generators to maintain cooling and avert a fuel meltdown.

Key facts:

  • July 2026 was the deadliest month for Ukraine since May 2022, with a record 376 missiles launched. Russian forces are reportedly preparing to scale up to 200 missiles in a single strike package.
  • Zelensky is appealing to roughly 40 governments for interceptor missiles; Ukraine currently holds about one-tenth of the interceptors its air defense doctrine requires.
  • Former Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov publicly called for presidential elections, saying “Democracy cannot be held hostage by Russia.” Elections have been suspended under martial law since 2022.

3. US national debt passes 40 trillion as Bessent bond intervention falls flat

The US national debt crossed $40 trillion on August 19 — months earlier than official projections — amounting to roughly $117,000 per person and $297,000 per household. July alone posted a $432 billion monthly deficit, the fourth-highest single month in US history. Annual interest payments have reached $1.1 trillion, now exceeding total Pentagon appropriations for the first time.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent responded by doubling the Treasury’s long-dated bond buyback program from $2 billion to $4 billion. Yields fell briefly, then returned to multi-year highs. Analysts quoted in the financial press dismissed the move as a “band aid” and “a sticking plaster.” As Nick Beams wrote on the WSWS, the government is now caught in a “doom loop” in which it must borrow at rising rates simply to service existing debt.

Key facts:

  • Debt has risen $11.6 trillion across Trump’s two terms, against $8.4 trillion under Biden.
  • The Treasury is refunding more than $160 billion to corporations after the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s tariffs as illegal — a major driver of the earlier-than-expected milestone.
  • Margaret Spellings of the Bipartisan Policy Center: “Federal debt is already raising the cost of living and choking out other spending and investment.”
  • House Budget Chairman Jodey Arrington used the milestone to call for an Article V constitutional convention — an opening for a far-reaching assault on social spending.
  • Quantitative hedge funds recorded their worst single day in two years amid the bond turbulence.

4. Israel opens bidding on 1,234 E1 settlement homes as Ben-Gvir unveils execution facility

Israel’s Construction and Housing Ministry opened tenders for seven residential compounds totaling 1,234 units in the E1 corridor of the occupied West Bank, part of a 3,401-unit project approved last year. The bidding deadline of October 19 falls one week before Israel’s October 27 parliamentary election. The project would link the settlement of Ma’ale Adumim to Jerusalem, bisecting the West Bank and severing East Jerusalem from Palestinian population centers to its south. European governments issued a joint statement demanding the project be halted.

Simultaneously, National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir’s office announced construction of a gallows facility inside a newly built death row wing at a central Israeli prison, to be used under the “Death Penalty for Terrorists” law passed by the Knesset 62-48 on March 30. The facility is designed with viewing booths so that families of victims may witness hangings.

Key facts:

  • Israel rejected the 15-point Gaza governance plan promoted by Trump’s “Board of Peace,” which envisioned phased Hamas disarmament and transfer to an international force in exchange for Israeli withdrawal. Jared Kushner met Netanyahu after the rejection.
  • Israeli strikes killed 10 Palestinians in two attacks — one on a Gaza City police post, one at Nuseirat refugee camp — days after Kushner reportedly asked Netanyahu to limit strikes to “imminent threats.” The Board of Peace has said nothing.
  • The Israeli military admitted firing more than 300 bullets at the vehicle carrying five-year-old Hind Rajab and her family in January 2024, along with the Palestine Red Crescent paramedics sent to rescue her.
  • Israeli warplanes struck multiple areas of southern Lebanon overnight.
  • Ben-Gvir: “I promised to pass the Death Penalty for Terrorists Law — we did. And now the death row and hanging facility are also starting to take shape.”

5. North Korea fires 10 ballistic missiles hours after Trump announces summit plans

North Korea launched approximately 10 short-range ballistic missiles from the Pyongyang area toward the East Sea at around 5 p.m. local time, each traveling roughly 300 kilometers. The barrage came within hours of Trump stating publicly that he intends to meet Kim Jong Un later this year, and coincided with the final days of the scaled-back US-South Korea Ulchi Freedom Shield exercises, which Trump had ordered shortened.

South Korea’s presidential office convened an emergency national security meeting, condemned the launches as violations of UN Security Council resolutions, and ordered heightened readiness.

Key facts:

  • The launch was the largest single salvo from the Pyongyang area this year.
  • Trump’s shortening of Ulchi Freedom Shield has produced open friction with sections of the South Korean military and US Indo-Pacific Command.
  • The episode underscores that summitry between Trump and Kim proceeds alongside, not instead of, the intensifying US military buildup directed at China across the region.

6. Hyundai workers stage first full strike in a decade

Some 39,000 members of the Hyundai Motor union in South Korea staged a full eight-hour strike on Friday — the first complete work stoppage in ten years — after 41 days of resumed wage negotiations failed. Production lines at Ulsan, Jeonju and Asan were halted for a combined 16 hours across two shifts. Kia’s union simultaneously halted all overtime work. Strikers from Hyundai, Kia and their suppliers rallied outside Hyundai’s Seoul headquarters.

The strikes have disrupted production of 55,200 vehicles worth more than 2.3 trillion won ($1.67 billion). Beyond wages, the union is demanding that the mandatory retirement age be raised above 60, that bonuses rise from 750 percent to 800 percent of monthly base pay, and — most significantly — that management guarantee job protections against artificial intelligence and automation.

Key facts:

  • Ulsan is the largest single automobile manufacturing complex in the world.
  • The AI and automation demand places South Korean autoworkers on the same terrain as workers at Ford, GM, Stellantis and Volkswagen, where employers are using AI-driven restructuring to eliminate tens of thousands of jobs.
  • Four-hour partial strikes by shift preceded Friday’s full walkout, with further partial action scheduled for Monday and Tuesday.

7. Tyson Foods shutters two beef plants, eliminating 3,200 jobs

Tyson Foods closed its beef slaughter and processing complex in Joslin, Illinois, which employed nearly 2,500 workers drawn from across the Quad Cities and surrounding rural counties in western Illinois and eastern Iowa, and its packaging plant in Eagle Mountain, Utah, employing 723. Both closures took effect immediately following an August 13 announcement. Tyson also plans to sell its Pasco, Washington beef plant.

Management attributed the closures to a multi-year contraction in the national cattle herd that has raised procurement costs and compressed packing margins; the company projects a $500-650 million loss in its beef division for fiscal 2026. CEO Donnie King is consolidating beef operations into three mega-facilities at Dakota City, Nebraska; Holcomb, Kansas; and Amarillo, Texas — shifting production out of unionized Midwestern plants toward Southern Plains hubs.

Key facts:

  • Tyson has closed six chicken plants, one pork plant and other facilities since 2023, cutting more than 10,000 jobs, while remaining highly profitable in chicken, pork and prepared foods.
  • UFCW Local 1546 offered no resistance to the shutdown. Its officials proposed a job fair.
  • Recently hired workers are ineligible for unemployment benefits entirely; others face immediate gaps in healthcare coverage.

8. National Guard occupation of Washington DC extended through January 2029

The Pentagon confirmed that the National Guard deployment to Washington DC, first ordered in August 2025, will now continue until January 20, 2029 — the final day of Trump’s term. The extension carries an additional cost of roughly $1.4 billion. South Carolina National Guard troops were posted at the Foggy Bottom Metro station as of August 20.

Guard personnel have carried out policing-adjacent functions including assisting in arrests, alongside routine tasks such as snow removal. The open-ended character of the deployment converts what was presented as an emergency anti-crime measure into a permanent military presence in the national capital.

Key facts:

  • The deployment has now been extended three times without congressional authorization.
  • Troops from multiple Republican-governed states have rotated through the capital, establishing a mechanism by which state Guard forces are placed at the disposal of federal executive power.
  • The Socialist Equality Party’s Ninth Congress resolution, published today, situates this within “a systematic plan to establish a presidential dictatorship” with “overtly fascistic characteristics.”

Sudan: 200,000 newly displaced in Kordofan as health facilities close

More than 200,000 people have been newly displaced across Sudan’s Kordofan region since fighting between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces intensified in late 2025, the UN reported. Drone attacks continue to target water and electricity infrastructure. Nationally, more than 30 million people require humanitarian assistance and over 9 million are internally displaced.

  • Funding shortfalls have forced the closure of three health facilities in Central Darfur, cutting off care for approximately 75,000 displaced people.
  • Heavy rains in the Tawila area of North Darfur damaged nearly 1,350 homes, displacing over 1,000 additional families.
  • The war continues to be fueled by Gulf state arms flows and competing great-power interests, yet receives a fraction of the coverage devoted to Ukraine or Gaza despite comparable scale of death and displacement.

UK jury refuses to convict Palestine Action defendants over Elbit factory raid

Judge Patrick Field discharged the Old Bailey jury in the two-month trial of eight Palestine Action supporters accused of organizing the August 2024 action against the Elbit Systems arms plant at Filton, near Bristol. After more than 37 hours of deliberation the jury returned a single guilty verdict — on one count of criminal damage, by a 9-1 majority — and was unable to reach any verdict on the remaining 14 charges.

  • None of the defendants were present at the raid itself; the prosecution case rested on alleged reconnaissance, equipment lists and tool purchases.
  • The identity of the convicted defendant cannot be reported for legal reasons. The Crown Prosecution Service must decide on a retrial; a hearing is set for September 14.
  • The outcome follows the earlier acquittal of the “Filton 6” and represents another failure of the British state’s attempt to criminalize opposition to the Gaza genocide through terrorism-adjacent prosecutions.

Mexico: five journalists murdered in two months

The Committee to Protect Journalists documented five murders of journalists in Mexico over the past two months — the worst concentration of anti-press violence in the country in more than a decade. CPJ called on the Sheinbaum government to federalize the investigation of journalist killings and to strengthen the national protection mechanism.

  • Mexico has remained among the deadliest countries in the world for journalists outside active war zones for over fifteen years, with impunity rates above 90 percent.

Magnitude 6.7 earthquake hits southern Peru as Spanish seismic swarm passes 600 tremors

A magnitude 6.7 earthquake struck Peru’s southern Andes on August 20, injuring at least two people and damaging homes, health centers and schools across multiple regions. Alarms were triggered across southern Peru.

  • The quake occurred amid a broader period of seismic activity, including a shallow swarm near Granada, Spain that has produced more than 600 events since August 14, including two magnitude 4.8 shocks, prompting the evacuation of roughly 600 residents.
  • As in every such event, the scale of damage in Peru reflects decades of under-investment in seismic-resistant construction in working-class and rural districts.

Nearly 800 teachers resign after Texas takeover of Fort Worth schools

mRNA cancer vaccine succeeds in phase 3 trial as Trump cancels mRNA contracts

Merck and Moderna announced on August 19 that their personalized mRNA melanoma vaccine, intismeran autogene, combined with Keytruda, cut the risk of cancer recurrence or death by 49 percent and the risk of distant metastasis or death by 59 percent compared with Keytruda alone. This is the first late-stage clinical success for an mRNA-based cancer treatment, opening a path to regulatory approval for individualized therapeutic cancer vaccines manufactured from a patient’s own tumor mutations.

The result arrives as the Trump administration cancels $500 million in federal contracts for mRNA vaccine development against respiratory viruses. The same platform being validated in oncology is being defunded in infectious disease on the basis of anti-vaccine ideology.

Fauci adviser David Morens pleads guilty in criminalization of scientific dissent

David Morens, 78, former senior adviser to Anthony Fauci at NIAID, pleaded guilty to conspiracy to defraud the United States. The charge centers on his routing of official correspondence through a personal email account in alleged violation of the Federal Records Act. He faces up to five years in prison and will be sentenced on November 12.

As Benjamin Mateus and Evan Blake wrote on the WSWS, the underlying object of the prosecution is Morens’s scientific position — he co-authored a commentary arguing SARS-CoV-2 had natural origins — and the government has effectively “written a scientific conclusion into a criminal indictment and called it corruption.” The prosecution follows earlier actions against Peter Daszak and others.

US measles total passes 2,566 as elimination status faces review

As of August 13, the CDC has confirmed 2,566 measles cases across 47 jurisdictions in 2026 — already exceeding the 2,289 cases recorded in all of 2025, itself the worst year since 1991.

  • 94 percent of cases are outbreak-associated; 93 percent of patients were unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status; 7 percent were hospitalized. No deaths have been reported this year.
  • A national verification committee meets this month to assess whether the United States retains the “measles elimination” status it has held since 2000, with a final determination expected from the Pan American Health Organization in November.

COVID wastewater levels up 106 percent as summer wave builds

  • SARS-CoV-2 concentrations in wastewater are up 106 percent compared with July, with infections growing or likely growing in all 50 states as of August 12.
  • The FDA has voted to recommend an updated 2026-2027 vaccine targeting the XFG variant, expected in September. Hospitalizations remain below prior-year levels, but the wave coincides with the return to school.

H5N1 now detected on all seven continents

  • H5N1 is present in over 50 countries this season and has now been recorded on all seven continents.
  • Australia’s confirmed wild-bird detections jumped from 101 on August 5 to 236 on August 11, including the country’s first mass mortality event: 49 dead and 35 sick terns.
  • CDC surveillance showed no unusual human influenza activity as of August 7, but the scale of mammalian spread — seals, cats, cattle — continues to raise reassortment concerns.

  • The disclosures are feeding into US and EU policy debates, including the EU AI Act’s newly effective transparency rules and a 269-page bipartisan US federal AI governance draft.
  • The incidents demonstrate that “safety evaluation” of frontier systems is being conducted by private firms with commercial incentives, without independent public oversight.

Arts and culture

Venice opens with a Murdoch origin story. The 83rd Venice International Film Festival, running September 2-12 on the Lido, will open with Danny Boyle’s Ink, a dramatization of Rupert Murdoch’s acquisition of The Sun and the birth of the tabloid press as a political weapon. That a major festival should open in 2026 with a film about the manufacture of reactionary public opinion is not accidental; the subject has acquired an obvious contemporary charge. Maggie Gyllenhaal presides over the main competition jury. George Clooney and Ellen Burstyn — the latter one of the last surviving major figures of the American New Wave, whose performances in The Last Picture Show, Alice Doesn’t Live Here Anymore and Requiem for a Dream trace three decades of American social decline — will receive Golden Lions for lifetime achievement.

The competition includes Florian Zeller’s thriller Bunker, Martin McDonagh’s Wild Horse Nine, Lee Chang-dong’s Possible Love — the South Korean director’s first feature since Burning — and Hirokazu Koreeda’s Look Back, his second festival entry of the year. Koreeda and Lee are among the few working filmmakers who have consistently made the material conditions of ordinary life the substance of serious cinema rather than its decorative background. The closing film, out of competition, is Giovanni Veronesi’s Dio ride, with Pierfrancesco Favino and Silvio Orlando. Online ticket reservations open August 27.

Hollywood’s unions surrender. The Directors Guild and IATSE have concluded agreements that, as Marc Wells wrote on the WSWS, hand the industry over to the studio-tech oligarchy. The contracts contain no meaningful barrier to the substitution of generative AI for the labor of writers, animators, editors and craft workers, and they were pushed through against a membership that has watched employment in the industry collapse since 2023. The pattern — union officials negotiating the terms of their own members’ technological displacement — is identical to that facing Hyundai workers in Ulsan, whose central demand this week is precisely the job protection against AI that the American entertainment unions declined to fight for.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: On the 86th anniversary of the assassination of Leon Trotsky by Andre Damon — read in full on wsws.org

Damon’s perspective marks the 86th anniversary of Trotsky’s murder by arguing that the entire subsequent history of the twentieth century confirmed his analysis of Stalinism. Where Trotsky wrote in The Revolution Betrayed that “the bureaucracy has betrayed the revolution,” and posed the alternative that either “the bureaucracy… will overthrow the new forms of property and plunge the country back to capitalism; or the working class will crush the bureaucracy,” the events of 1991 supplied the answer. Damon situates the persecution of Bogdan Syrotiuk in Ukraine today in the direct line of descent from the Moscow Trials, which Trotsky characterized as “the greatest frame-up the world had ever seen” — the same method of criminalizing Marxist opposition through fabricated allegations of service to a foreign power.

A case of political sabotage: Duncan Chapel’s filthy provocation against imprisoned Ukrainian socialist Bogdan Syrotiuk — WSWS International Editorial Board

The Editorial Board responds to Duncan Chapel, a British activist associated with the Pabloite network around International Viewpoint and Anticapitalist Resistance, who published on his “Red Mole” Substack the claim that Syrotiuk is “a Russian-backed Ukrainian paid to submit pro-Russian war propaganda to the WSWS website.”

Points not covered above:

  • Syrotiuk, founder of the Young Guard of Bolshevik-Leninists, is serving a 15-year sentence for treason under martial law. His conviction rests on his collaboration with the ICFI, not on any allegation of Russian payment.
  • The court judgment contains a single oblique sentence referencing monetary remuneration, never returned to anywhere else in the document. Russian military items allegedly discovered were at a different address and were never produced in court.
  • Chapel requested a response from the ICFI by August 21 but published his attack on August 16, then asserted that the ICFI “did not reply.”
  • The timing — during preparation of Syrotiuk’s appeal — serves the prosecution directly. The Editorial Board treats this as a case study in how the Pabloite tendencies, having integrated themselves into the pro-NATO milieu, function as auxiliaries of state repression against Trotskyists.

The drive to dictatorship and the political radicalization in the United States — Socialist Equality Party (US), Ninth Congress resolution

Points not covered above:

  • The resolution’s structural argument is that the drive to dictatorship arises from the incompatibility of oligarchic wealth concentration with democratic forms: “Such extreme levels of inequality are incompatible with democratic forms of rule.” It is not an aberration produced by one man but the political expression of capitalism’s breakdown.
  • It applies Trotsky’s analysis of Bonapartism: such regimes emerge organically from class polarization as executive power concentrates around the military-police apparatus. Concrete markers include the weaponization of federal agencies, the designation of political dissent as terrorism by executive memoranda, and the conversion of immigration enforcement into a paramilitary force.
  • On the other pole: the standing of capitalism among young people has fallen to historic lows, with majorities viewing socialism favorably, and conditions of impossible living costs and endless war are driving millions toward politics.
  • The resolution identifies the DSA as the principal political obstacle — an initial beneficiary of radicalization that functions as a faction of the Democratic Party, blocking the emergence of an independent movement of the working class.

The political legacy of Fidel Castro on the 100th anniversary of his birth — Bill Van Auken

Van Auken assesses Castro’s revolution as the highest and therefore most revealing expression of the bankruptcy of postwar bourgeois nationalism. He traces Castro’s emergence from Cuban nationalist student politics, his initial denial of communist sympathies, and the way US imperialism’s refusal to tolerate even modest reform drove him toward Moscow. The nationalization of property under the July 26 Movement created a workers’ state deformed from birth by the absence of workers’ democratic control — a distinction Van Auken insists is not academic but decisive.

He quotes Castro’s declaration that Cuba was “capable of producing the food and material riches we need,” and sets against it the trajectory that ended in Castro’s embrace of rapprochement with Washington under Obama — the historical exhaustion of the nationalist project. The central polemical target is Pabloism: the theories that promoted Castroism as a viable socialist model diverted a generation of radicalized Latin American youth into guerrillaist adventures that cost thousands of lives, in place of the ICFI’s foundational principle that “the emancipation of the working class is the task of the workers themselves.”

General Motors Mexico workers defend Bogdan Syrotiuk and support Will Lehman’s campaign — Andrea Lobo

Lobo reports on discussions with workers at GM’s Silao complex, who have taken up the defense of Syrotiuk alongside support for Will Lehman’s campaign. The article documents the international character of the defense campaign and connects it to the experiences of Mexican autoworkers with SINTTIA and the state labor apparatus, which have functioned to suppress the wage struggle at Silao in the interests of North American auto capital.

WSWS readers and left press in Türkiye demand the release of Bogdan Syrotiuk — Our reporters

A report on the growth of the Syrotiuk defense campaign in Türkiye, including statements from readers and from sections of the Turkish left press. The article notes the significance of the campaign gaining traction in a country where the state itself imprisons political opponents in large numbers.

“The state has to make a precedent that no worker will rise”: Interview with a defender of victimized workers in Noida, India — Sathish Simon

An interview with an activist defending workers victimized in Noida, one of the principal industrial zones of the National Capital Region. The interviewee’s formulation — that the state’s purpose is “to make a precedent that no worker will rise” — captures the function of criminal prosecution and blacklisting in Indian industrial relations under the Modi government’s labor codes, which have effectively legalized hire-and-fire and gutted the right to strike across most of the manufacturing sector.

Australia: Allied health workers strike against Victorian Labor’s deepening cuts — Our reporters

Allied health workers — physiotherapists, occupational therapists, social workers, speech pathologists — struck against the Victorian Labor government’s health budget cuts. The article stresses that the cuts are being imposed by a Labor government with the assistance of the health unions, and connects them to the same austerity agenda driving the assault on the National Disability Insurance Scheme documented in yesterday’s report on meetings of disabled people and their families.

Jaffna fishermen protest against bottom trawling, Tamil nationalists divert anger along communal lines — Navin Dewage and M. Muruganandan

Fishermen in Jaffna protested against destructive bottom trawling by Indian trawlers in northern Sri Lankan waters. The authors document how Tamil nationalist parties are working to channel the fishermen’s legitimate grievances into communal hostility toward Indian fishermen — themselves impoverished workers — rather than a unified struggle of South Asian fishing communities against the corporate interests and state policies destroying their livelihoods.

Jacobin and DSA

Jacobin’s output is dominated by long-form academic material with no engagement in the immediate political crisis.

  • “Utopia Is a Promise Worth Fighting For” by Matt McManus argues explicitly against Marx’s critique of utopian socialism, presenting utopianism as offering “hope without guarantees.” This is a direct repudiation of scientific socialism — the substitution of moral aspiration for the analysis of objective historical laws and the revolutionary role of the working class. It is the theoretical form appropriate to a tendency whose practice is limited to Democratic Party electoralism.
  • “Immanuel Wallerstein Predicted the Fall of the US Empire” by Gregory P. Williams recasts imperialist decline through world-systems theory, in which the relevant actors are core and periphery states rather than classes. The framework systematically obscures the class struggle inside the imperialist centers.
  • “Toward Simp Liberation?” by Evelina Johansson Wilén and Meagan Day analyzes “red-pill” ideology in terms of gendered status hierarchies — the characteristic Jacobin move of translating a social crisis rooted in economic devastation into the vocabulary of identity.

Assessment: On a day when the United States declared unprecedented economic warfare against Iran, the national debt crossed $40 trillion, and the National Guard occupation of the capital was extended to 2029, Jacobin published essays on utopian philosophy and Chinese university entrance exams. The silence is itself the political line: nothing may be said that would place demands on the Democratic Party.

Socialist Worker (UK)

  • “Win for Filton 25 members as jury refuses to convict” presents the Old Bailey outcome as a vindication of the mass movement, concluding that the “mass movement for Palestine has shifted public opinion.” The formulation reduces the task to shifting opinion within the framework of the existing state — precisely the perspective that left Palestine Action proscribed as a terrorist organization in the first place, and that left the SWP tailing the Labour Party’s “left” MPs rather than fighting for a break with Labour.
  • “Arriva bus strikes suspended while workers consider a new offer” reports the suspension of action over dangerous heat conditions without a word of criticism of the union officials who suspended it, or any call for rank-and-file control of the negotiations.
  • “The far right feed us lies on crime” correctly identifies far-right misinformation on crime and migration but frames the response as an anti-racist moral campaign rather than a struggle to unify workers across national lines against the Labour government implementing the anti-migrant agenda.

Assessment: The SWP’s method throughout is to record struggles sympathetically while systematically declining to name the union bureaucracy and the Labour Party as obstacles.

Communist (RCP, IMT)

  • “Mau Mau Uprising — how the British mutilated Kenya” is a substantive historical exposure of British colonial atrocities, and the strongest material published by any of these tendencies today.
  • “Air ‘con’: Climate culture war heats up as temperatures rise” argues that capitalists exploit climate debates by “pitting climate activists against ordinary people.” The framing accepts the premise that environmental measures are experienced as an imposition on workers, adapting to right-wing populist agitation instead of advancing a socialist program for the rational reorganization of energy and production.
  • “AI, data centres, and the quiet hum of revolution” catalogues environmental damage and unemployment from AI expansion, but the conclusion — “revolutionary responses” in the abstract — is unconnected to any concrete intervention in struggles like the Hyundai strike, where AI displacement is the actual bargaining issue.

Assessment: Strong historical material coexists with a present-day perspective built around national-populist framing and recruitment appeals in place of a program for the working class.

Left Voice

  • Coverage of the cancellation of Temporary Protected Status, which places 300,000 additional immigrant workers at risk of deportation, centers on a resolution passed by NYC Teamsters Local 804. Left Voice presents resolutions passed by union bodies as the substance of the fight, without addressing that the Teamsters’ national leadership under Sean O’Brien has openly aligned itself with the Trump administration.
  • Coverage of the Bolivian uprising emphasizes assemblies and roadblocks as expressions of “Latin American resistance to Trump and the international Far Right” — a framing that subordinates the independent class movement of Bolivian workers to a continental anti-Trump front.

Assessment: Left Voice registers militant struggles more accurately than most of these tendencies, but its perspective consistently stops at the boundary of the existing union apparatus.

International Socialist Alternative

  • ISA’s report on its International School, held July 25-30 in Kiel, Germany, with over 100 participants, features a World Perspectives plenary titled “Trump’s reactionary offensive deepens capitalist crises — class battles incoming.” The formulation is characteristic: struggles are always impending, never present, and no organizational conclusion — above all no break with the trade union bureaucracy or the parties of the “broad left” — is drawn.
  • The report devotes prominent space to a €42,218 finance appeal.

Assessment: An organization whose international gathering produces perspectives documents anticipating battles rather than intervening in the ones underway at Hyundai, Tyson and across the Victorian health system.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Hyundai’s 39,000 strikers pose the question of AI and jobs on a world scale

The first full strike at Hyundai in a decade, with 39,000 workers halting production worth $1.67 billion, centers on demands for job guarantees against artificial intelligence and automation and for raising the retirement age above 60. This is the same question confronting autoworkers at Ford, GM, Stellantis and Volkswagen, and the same question the Hollywood unions just capitulated on. It demands an intervention advancing the perspective of an international rank-and-file network in the auto industry.

Recommended reading Korea Herald Hyundai Motor union stages first full-day strike in 10 years

2. Who pays for the $40 trillion debt?

Mainstream coverage of the debt milestone is confined to abstract fiscal alarm. The class content — that interest payments now exceeding the Pentagon budget are being used to prepare a frontal assault on Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid, and that House Budget Chairman Arrington has already invoked an Article V constitutional convention — requires a sustained exposure. The $160 billion tariff refund to corporations is a particularly sharp illustration of who the state serves.

Recommended reading Washington Post US debt hits $40 trillion, months earlier than expected

3. Sudan’s Kordofan catastrophe and the great-power scramble behind it

With over 200,000 newly displaced in Kordofan, 9 million internally displaced nationally, and 30 million requiring aid, Sudan represents a humanitarian catastrophe on the scale of Ukraine and Gaza combined, driven by a proxy conflict between forces armed by rival Gulf states and great powers. The near-total silence of the Western press is itself a political fact requiring analysis.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Over 200,000 people newly displaced in Sudan’s Kordofan as fighting rages

4. The human cost of the UAE-Iran embargo

The severing of $28 billion in annual trade, combined with Trump’s “economic D-Day” threats against any bank that deals with Tehran, will fall on Iranian workers already subject to water rationing, blackouts and the gallows. A projected 5 percent contraction means mass destitution. This is collective punishment as an instrument of imperialist policy, and it requires the same treatment the WSWS has given to the Gaza blockade.

Recommended reading Foreign Policy UAE imposes trade and financial embargo on Iran

5. Minnesota v. Abbott: a state governor shields an ICE agent from felony charges

Texas Governor Abbott’s refusal to extradite ICE agent Christian Castro, charged with shooting a Venezuelan immigrant through a door and then filing a false report, establishes that federal immigration agents are to be placed beyond the reach of criminal law by Republican state governments. The constitutional dimensions — an open breach of the interstate rendition clause — connect directly to the SEP congress resolution’s analysis of the paramilitarization of ICE.

Recommended reading Democracy Now Minnesota sues Texas Gov. Abbott over ICE agent extradition

6. The DOJ whistleblower complaint on the “antisemitism” extortion racket

A former Justice Department attorney has now stated on the record that the antisemitism task force forced Brown into a $50 million settlement and Columbia into a $200 million settlement on the basis of minimal evidence. This vindicates the WSWS’s analysis of the campaign as a pretext for the destruction of academic freedom and the suppression of opposition to genocide, and provides documentary ammunition for the defense of victimized students and faculty.

Recommended reading Democracy Now Former DOJ attorney files complaint over antisemitism task force settlements

7. Zaporizhzhia loses power for the 27th time

Europe’s largest nuclear plant has now lost external power 27 times since 2022, each time relying on emergency diesel generators to prevent a fuel meltdown. This recurring near-catastrophe is treated by the world’s press as routine. The reckless indifference of all the belligerents and of NATO to the risk of a radiological disaster affecting tens of millions is a devastating indictment of the war.
