1. US-Iran war: Pakistan says “final agreed” peace text reached as strikes continue

Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif announced on June 12 that “a final, agreed upon text of the peace deal has been reached,” declaring that “peace has never been as close as it is now.” Iran’s foreign ministry pointedly declined to confirm any agreement, saying only that “final internal deliberations” were underway. A sign-off from Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei remains the last missing piece, and Iranian officials publicly called Trump “deceitful,” insisting nothing has been finalized.

The diplomatic maneuvering unfolds against continued military operations. Trump canceled a fresh wave of strikes on June 11 after having warned Iran it would be hit “very hard tonight.” The war, now in its fourth month, began February 28 when Trump announced “major combat operations” with massive joint US-Israeli strikes; Iran retaliated against US embassies, Gulf military installations, and Strait of Hormuz shipping. As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, the threats have escalated to Trump menacing Iran with “civilizational destruction” and proposing the seizure of Iranian oil infrastructure — exposing the colonial-plunder logic driving the war.

The human toll is staggering. Iran’s Health Ministry reports at least 3,468 killed and more than 26,500 injured since February 28, including at least 1,621 children. The conflict has produced what the IEA called “the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market,” with traffic through the Strait of Hormuz down more than 90 percent.

Key facts: - US equipment losses are estimated at $5.14 billion; at least 13–15 US personnel have been killed in action. - Cross-theater deaths are estimated at 8,336–17,685 across all sides. - Gulf states face a “grocery supply emergency,” with 70 percent of food imports disrupted and consumer prices spiking 40–120 percent. - There is near-total bipartisan support in Washington for the war despite the civilian death toll receiving minimal US prime-time coverage.

2. Musk becomes world’s first trillionaire as SpaceX IPO debuts

SpaceX debuted on the Nasdaq, opening at $150 (above its $135 IPO price) and closing its first day at $161.11, up 19.3 percent, reaching a market capitalization of roughly $2.1 trillion. Elon Musk’s personal fortune thereby exceeded $1.1 trillion — a gain of more than $300 billion in two days — making him the world’s first trillionaire. The financial press reported the milestones; the WSWS supplied the analysis the bourgeois coverage omitted.

In the day’s Perspective, Joseph Kishore argued the valuation was “conjured out of nothing.” SpaceX lost $4.9 billion in 2025 on $18.7 billion in revenue, yet trades at roughly 95 times revenue, against an S&P 500 average of under four times. To dramatize the absurdity, Kishore noted that General Motors — with ten times SpaceX’s revenue — is valued at about $70 billion; priced like SpaceX, GM would be worth $17.7 trillion, more than half of annual US GDP.

Key facts: - The valuation rests on monopoly: since 2023 SpaceX has controlled more than 80 percent of mass launched to orbit annually, plus the Starlink network now operating in war zones from Ukraine to the Middle East. - Peter Thiel’s Founders Fund turned a $20 million 2008 investment into roughly $60 billion; Google’s stake approaches $100 billion. - The US now counts 935 billionaires — more than the next nine countries combined; the 14 wealthiest hold more than all US billionaires combined did in 2020. - Total US market capitalization stands at 232 percent of GDP, exceeding dot-com peaks; Nvidia alone is valued above $5 trillion.

Sources Yahoo Finance Markets rebound as SpaceX debuts · WSWS Elon Musk the world’s first trillionaire: The SpaceX IPO and the social physiognomy of oligarchy

3. World Bank cuts global growth forecast to lowest since COVID-19

The World Bank’s June 11 Global Economic Prospects report cut its global growth forecast to 2.5 percent for 2026, down from 2.9 percent in 2025 — the weakest pace since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Two-thirds of the world’s economies have had their forecasts downgraded since January. The report attributes the deterioration substantially to the Middle East war and the resulting energy shock.

Key facts: - Brent crude is projected to average $94/barrel in 2026, 36 percent above 2025 levels; global inflation is forecast to rise to 4.0 percent, from 3.3 percent. - Gulf-region growth collapses to nearly zero in 2026, down from 3.9 percent in 2025. - Developing economies are at a “post-pandemic low,” with nearly a decade now passed without progress narrowing the per-capita income gap between rich and poor nations. - The Bank made $50–60 billion immediately available, with a maximum potential of $80–100 billion over 15 months. - UN Secretary-General Guterres has warned of a “Hormuz crisis strangling the global economy.”

Sources World Bank Global Economic Prospects, June 2026 · UN News Hormuz crisis strangling global economy, Guterres warns

4. South Korea’s Yoon sentenced to 30 years in drone-over-Pyongyang case

The Seoul Central District Court sentenced former President Yoon Suk Yeol, 65, to 30 years in prison on June 11 on charges of abuse of power and “aiding the enemy” for a covert October 2024 drone operation over Pyongyang. The court found that Yoon conspired to use the drone incursion to heighten national-security fears, provoke a North Korean response, and manufacture a pretext for his December 2024 martial law declaration. Former Defense Minister Kim Yong Hyun was sentenced alongside him.

The new sentence comes on top of a life sentence already handed down for leading an insurrection after the failed martial law bid, which the constitutional court cited in upholding his removal from office. The case is an extraordinary documentation of a sitting head of state using covert military operations as an instrument of domestic political repression — a measure of the depth of South Korea’s ruling-class crisis.

5. Hezbollah rejects Israel-Lebanon ceasefire as Iran links its own deal to Lebanon

A US-brokered ceasefire agreed June 1 between Israel and the Lebanese government — under which Israel would not target Beirut’s southern suburbs and Hezbollah would not attack Israeli border towns — has effectively collapsed. Hezbollah rejected the framework and continued cross-border attacks; at least 10 people were killed in southern Lebanon within hours of the announcement, and air-raid alarms sounded in northern Israel. Iran has declared that Lebanon must be included in any Iran-US ceasefire framework, directly linking the two theaters and complicating Washington’s diplomacy. Further talks are scheduled for the week of June 22.

Key facts: - Lebanon’s death toll stands at 3,696–3,711 killed and more than 11,413 wounded as of June 11. - The linkage of the Lebanese and Iranian tracks gives Tehran additional leverage over any settlement.

6. FIFA World Cup 2026 opens across three nations against a war backdrop

The 2026 FIFA World Cup opened June 11 in Mexico City — the first tournament ever co-hosted by three nations simultaneously: the United States, Mexico, and Canada. The opening ceremony featured Shakira, Burna Boy, J Balvin, Maná, Tyla, and Alejandro Fernández, with Shakira and Burna Boy performing the official song “Dai Dai.” In opening-day matches South Korea defeated Czechia and Mexico defeated South Africa.

The festivities proceed amid the ongoing US-Iran war, with travel disruptions across the Middle East affecting fans and the logistics of regional teams. The spectacle’s underside has been the suppression of workers’ struggles: as the WSWS reported, UNITE HERE Local 11 canceled a strike by 2,000 hospitality workers days before kickoff, and the Sheinbaum government directed tens of millions of pesos to World Cup infrastructure while Mexican teachers marched against wage and pension cuts.

Gaza “ceasefire” in name only as Israeli violations top 3,201

Israel has violated the October 10, 2025 Gaza ceasefire at least 3,201 times through June 9, via air strikes, artillery, and direct shootings. Since the ceasefire took effect, Israeli attacks have killed at least 981 Palestinians and injured 3,104. Only 52,129 of 144,000 allocated aid trucks — 36 percent — have entered Gaza, with Israel blocking meat, dairy, and vegetables while permitting snacks and soft drinks. Analysts at Chatham House warn that “Phase Two” of the deal will fail absent a political vision for Palestinian statehood. The October prisoner exchange saw Hamas release all 20 remaining living Israeli captives for 250 long-sentence Palestinian prisoners and 1,700 Palestinians disappeared since October 2023.

Ukraine-Russia truce holds amid Oreshnik missile warning

The 2026 Russo-Ukrainian truce remains formally in place, but Ukraine’s Air Force has warned of a “high probability” of an imminent Oreshnik ballistic missile launch from Russia’s Kapustin Yar site. Cumulative equipment losses since 2022 are tracked at roughly 14,023 Russian tanks and armored vehicles (Oryx) versus 5,834 for Ukraine. The truce has eased sanctions pressure under the Trump administration’s overtures to Moscow — a shift reflected in financial markets (see World economy).

India-Pakistan: Siachen standoff persists as Kashmir protests spread

The fragile India-Pakistan ceasefire in place since May 10, 2025 — after the worst military confrontation since the 1999 Kargil War, triggered by the Pahalgam attack that killed 26 civilians — continues without any troop withdrawals, a “frozen status quo.” The Siachen Glacier remains occupied by both armies; more than 2,000 soldiers have died there since 1984, fewer than 3 percent from combat and the rest from extreme cold (−50°C), avalanches, and altitude sickness. Meanwhile, protests are erupting in Pakistan-administered Kashmir over a rule reserving legislative seats for refugees from Indian-administered Kashmir, which the Joint Awami Action Committee says hands demographically small communities disproportionate power. Islamabad’s response — that change would require constitutional reform — has fueled further demonstrations.

Germany’s Merz declares “war abroad, social counterrevolution at home”

In a June 11 government statement to the Bundestag ahead of the June 18–19 European Council, Chancellor Friedrich Merz reaffirmed Germany’s commitment to Ukraine, insisted “pressure on Russia must continue,” and pressed for major increases in EU and German defense spending alongside tighter migration controls. As Johannes Stern wrote on the WSWS, the statement is the most explicit expression yet of European imperialism’s twin priorities: rearmament directed at Russia and the systematic dismantling of social gains won since World War II. The shift is already hitting industry — IG Metall has aligned with corporate management and the state amid a wave of steel-sector layoffs as resources flow to the military.

Britain’s defence and armed-forces ministers resign demanding higher military spending

UK Defence Secretary John Healey abruptly resigned on June 11, telling Prime Minister Keir Starmer that the government’s defense investment plan “falls well short of what is required at this dangerous time.” Armed Forces Minister Al Carns quit alongside him, saying “we are asking our Armed Forces to operate in a more dangerous world on a budget written for a calmer one.” Starmer has pledged to lift defense spending to 2.5 percent of GDP by 2027 and 3 percent by 2034; Security Minister Dan Jarvis was named the new defense secretary. As Robert Stevens noted on the WSWS, the resignations reflect an intra-ruling-class conflict over the pace — not the direction — of British rearmament.

World economy

US equities closed higher Friday, June 12 (June 13 is a Saturday with markets closed). The Dow Jones rose 354 points (+0.70%) to 51,202, the S&P 500 added 0.50%, and the Nasdaq gained 0.30%. Sentiment was lifted by the SpaceX debut and by reports of a possible US-Iran deal that could reopen the Strait of Hormuz and lift oil sanctions. The same prospect pressured energy prices, sending Brent down 3.44% to about $87.27 and dragging WTI into an $83–87 range.

The European Central Bank delivered its first interest-rate increase since 2023, responding to a renewed surge in inflation. As Nick Beams wrote on the WSWS, higher rates protect financial asset-holders while raising debt-servicing costs that compress social spending and wages — austerity by monetary means. The hike sets a marker for the Fed and Bank of England.

Data roundup: - Oracle shares fell roughly 11% on concerns over a large capital raise and cash burn, a drag on the tech complex. - Gold slumped to a six-month low despite persistent inflation fears; Polymarket priced ~12% odds of gold reaching $3,800 by end-June. - Bitcoin rose about 3.40% to ~$63,718 and Ethereum gained ~3.20% to ~$1,671, both rebounding after Trump claimed the Iran war had ended. - Asian markets: the Hang Seng fell 0.65% (159 points) to 24,249, a seventh consecutive decline and its lowest since July 2025 on tech-led selling; the Nikkei 225 sat near 38,402 and the Shanghai Composite tested resistance at 4,040. - Russian ruble was the world’s best-performing currency in Q2 2026, up ~12% since April to about 72.6 per dollar — its strongest since early 2023 — as sanctions pressure eased. - University of Michigan consumer sentiment (preliminary June) was seen at 46, up from 44.8 in May but still deeply depressed.

Trump’s “Big Beautiful Bill” impacts hit as SNAP rolls are slashed

Major provisions of the One Big Beautiful Bill, signed July 4, 2025, are now taking effect, including elimination of residential clean-energy tax credits and the rollout of Medicaid and SNAP restrictions. New “work or starve” requirements are stripping food assistance from disabled workers, caregivers, and people in high-unemployment areas, driving millions off the rolls, as Kate Randall documented on the WSWS. A June Quinnipiac poll found only 29 percent of voters support the law, with 55 percent opposed.

Key facts: - Congress added $69.5 billion to ICE and CBP through 2029 via reconciliation (the “Secure America Act”). - Parent PLUS student loans will be capped at $65,000 lifetime / $20,000 annually beginning July 2026. - The law pairs safety-net cuts with tax cuts skewed to corporations and the wealthiest households.

UAW rushes American Axle deal as Nexteer worker is fired for dissent

The UAW announced a four-year tentative agreement on June 10 to end the 10-day strike by roughly 1,000 American Axle workers in Three Rivers, Michigan. The deal promises a top wage of $30/hour by 2030 (a 36 percent increase to the top rate), an immediate $8/hour raise for legacy members, a $2,000 ratification bonus, and no healthcare-premium increases. Kevin Reed argues on the WSWS that the agreement nonetheless locks in concessions and was rushed to a vote before workers could coordinate broader solidarity. The move coincided with the firing of Nexteer Automotive worker Antwiane Sanders after he publicly opposed a UAW tentative agreement; the Nexteer Workers Rank-and-File Committee is demanding his immediate reinstatement, framing both events as coordinated bureaucratic-management discipline.

US measles cases hit 2,063 with 30 active outbreaks

The CDC reports 2,063 confirmed measles cases across 40 jurisdictions year-to-date, with 93 percent (1,929) outbreak-associated and 30 new outbreaks declared in 2026 alone. The pace continues the surge from 2025’s 2,286 full-year total and is on track to surpass it before summer ends — a direct consequence of falling vaccination rates amid anti-vaccine policy from federal health authorities.

H5N1 confirmed in poultry across 12 states; first human H5N5 case

The USDA has confirmed H5N1 highly pathogenic avian influenza in commercial poultry flocks across 12 states, with more than 100 million birds depopulated since the current cycle began. Separately, an NEJM correspondence documented the first confirmed human H5N5 infection — a 75-year-old immunocompromised woman whose lower-respiratory specimens showed higher viral loads than upper-tract specimens. The case broadens the catalogue of avian-influenza subtypes that have crossed to humans, raising surveillance concerns even as general public risk remains low.

Three landmark finerenone trials published simultaneously

Three major studies on finerenone appeared at once, presented at the European Renal Association Congress in Glasgow: - NEJM (FIND-CKD, Phase 3): finerenone cut the risk of kidney failure, CKD progression, heart failure, or cardiovascular death by 23% in non-diabetic CKD patients. - JAMA (glomerular disease): a 26% reduction in kidney failure or CKD progression and a 42% drop in albuminuria at 12 months. - The Lancet (pooled analysis): consistent benefit across diabetic and non-diabetic populations.

Together the publications dramatically expand finerenone’s candidate population from diabetic CKD to potentially millions more with non-diabetic kidney disease.

House draft would preempt state AI laws for three years

Reps. Jay Obernolte (R-CA) and Lori Trahan (D-MA) released a 269-page bipartisan discussion draft that would preempt state laws regulating AI model development (not deployment) for three years, while requiring firms with over $500 million in revenue to publish frontier-AI safety frameworks. The bill directly targets New York’s and California’s recently passed AI transparency and safety laws, setting up a federal-vs-state battle over who governs the industry. Separately, a June White House action on “Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security” pairs a push for AI leadership with a CISA directive requiring agencies to adopt AI-driven vulnerability management — framing AI as simultaneously a strategic asset and an infrastructure threat.

On COVID-19: the disease is growing in only 2 states and declining in 38 as of June 9; the CDC ensemble model forecasts roughly 1,000 new lab-confirmed hospital admissions for the week ending June 13 (range 420–2,400), with ED-visit share easing to a forecast 0.087%. No surge is underway.

Arts and culture

The death of historian Gordon Wood at 92 — struck and killed by a motorist on June 8 — removes one of the most influential American historians of his generation. Wood won the 1993 Pulitzer Prize for History for The Radicalism of the American Revolution, which argued that the Revolution was a genuinely transformative social upheaval that dissolved the hierarchical, monarchical order of colonial society. His passing lands amid a renewed contest over the meaning of the Revolution’s 250th anniversary. The WSWS this week took up that contest directly, with Paul Mitchell sharply criticizing the BBC documentary Lucy Worsley Investigates: The American Revolution for substituting celebrity-presenter superficiality and personality-driven narrative for any class analysis of the revolutionary period’s causes and contradictions — treating one of the most decisive events in world history as costume drama. Wood’s scholarship, whatever its limitations from a Marxist standpoint, at least grasped the Revolution as a real social transformation rather than a pageant of great men.

The death of psychiatrist and author Robert Coles on June 7, a Pulitzer winner whose Children of Crisis series documented the inner lives of poor and segregated children through the civil-rights era, marks the loss of a writer who insisted that the testimony of the oppressed belonged at the center of American letters.

Roundup: - Questlove’s documentary on Earth, Wind & Fire — exploring the band’s utopian, cosmically themed fusion of soul, funk, and jazz — is now streaming on HBO, the latest in the director’s project of treating Black popular music as serious cultural history rather than nostalgia. - The 45th anniversary of Raiders of the Lost Ark (June 12) prompted retrospectives on the Spielberg-Lucas blockbuster that, for better and worse, helped define the modern franchise-driven Hollywood economy now dominated by intellectual-property recycling. - New US releases dated June 12 — including Scary Movie 6 and an untitled film from the Daniels (the Everything Everywhere All at Once duo) — illustrate the industry’s bifurcation between exhausted parody franchises and the occasional auteur project squeezed into the summer calendar. - In the orchestral repertory, programs this week paired Copland’s Lincoln Portrait — a wartime work built on Lincoln’s own words about democracy and union — with Gershwin and Barber, a reminder of how mid-century American composers wrestled openly with national identity at a moment when that question is again politically charged.

The throughline across these items is the struggle over historical memory: who controls the narrative of the American Revolution, of the civil-rights era, of national identity itself. As the WSWS framework insists, cultural production is never neutral entertainment — it is a terrain on which class forces contend over how a society understands its own past and possibilities.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: Elon Musk the world’s first trillionaire: The SpaceX IPO and the social physiognomy of oligarchy by Joseph Kishore — read in full on wsws.org. Bernie Sanders’ AI “sovereign wealth fund”: a public-private partnership to bail out the tech giants — Evan Blake

Blake dissects Sanders’ proposal for a government AI “sovereign wealth fund,” arguing it is structurally a state subsidy to existing AI monopolies — Nvidia, Microsoft, Google, Amazon — and military-industrial infrastructure, not a democratic alternative to private control. Read alongside Sanders’ “tax the rich” rhetoric, the piece situates the proposal as fundamentally reformist: it accepts the existing ownership structure and merely seeks a government stake within it. The political conclusion is that only the socialist expropriation of AI infrastructure, not a public-private partnership, can serve working-class interests. The article complements the Kishore Perspective by showing how the “left” wing of the Democratic Party offers oligarchy a friendlier face rather than a challenge.

Four Michigan anti-genocide protesters plead not guilty in federal frame-up — Stephen St. Clair

Four University of Michigan students who took part in anti-genocide protests pleaded not guilty to federal conspiracy charges and were released on bond. The WSWS characterizes the prosecution as part of the Trump administration’s broader criminalization of anti-war and left-wing opposition — a political frame-up designed to intimidate the campus movement and establish precedent for suppressing organizing, proceeding even as the same administration prosecutes the Iran war abroad. St. Clair argues the defense of democratic rights requires a socialist, working-class movement, not appeals to the Democratic Party.

APWU webinar on post office closures diverts workers into appeals to Congress — Tom Hall

Hall dissects an American Postal Workers Union webinar on post-office closures, arguing it channeled worker anger into petitions and lobbying aimed at the very institutions dismantling the postal service. This connects to the prior day’s WSWS investigation into the Palmetto Regional Processing and Distribution Center, where four workers have died in two years amid documented safety failures and management throughput pressure — findings of the WSWS-initiated USPS Workers Rank-and-File Inquiry. The structural point: bureaucratic politics converts mass opposition into harmless institutional appeals.

Germany: steelworkers need international unity to defend jobs — Dietmar Gaisenkersting, Peter Schwarz

Building on the Merz coverage above, this piece documents IG Metall’s specific alignment with management amid steel-sector layoffs. The authors argue German steelworkers can defend jobs only through coordinated international unity, not the national “social partnership” framework the union enforces — the unions having become instruments of the ruling class. It is a concrete national application of the ICFI’s call for internationalist rank-and-file organization.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s recent output foregrounds three pieces. “Building 21st-Century Rank-and-File Unionism” urges organizers to emulate the New Left’s “industrial turn” to cultivate a militant current — but within the existing union structures and, ultimately, the DSA’s electoral orientation, leaving the bureaucracy’s grip on the apparatus unchallenged. “The End of the Old Economic Order Is an Opening for the Left” treats Trump’s wrecking of the neoliberal trade order as a strategic opportunity for a revived industrial policy — adapting to economic nationalism and recasting state-managed capitalism as a “left” goal rather than opposing the turn to autarky and war. “Putting the Marxism Back Into ‘Cultural Marxism’” makes a genuine gesture toward historical materialism, but functions largely as an academic recuperation of Marxist categories detached from any revolutionary program or independent party. The day’s orientation: a magazine that deploys socialist vocabulary to refurbish the left flank of the Democratic Party and the union officialdom.

Sources Jacobin Building 21st-Century Rank-and-File Unionism · Jacobin The End of the Old Economic Order Is an Opening for the Left

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL’s coverage of the Iran war runs through the prism of its statement “Why Trump failed in his criminal war against Iran,” which celebrates Iran keeping the Strait of Hormuz closed and its defensive capacity “intact.” While correctly opposing the US-Israeli aggression, the PSL slides from anti-imperialism into political support for the bourgeois-clerical regime in Tehran, presenting the Iranian capitalist state as an anti-imperialist protagonist rather than a class enemy of Iranian workers. Its earlier “Stop Trump’s War on Iran!” and Palestine-solidarity statements similarly subordinate the working class to a “camp” framework in which the enemies of US imperialism are treated as allies. The orientation: militant-sounding anti-war agitation that disarms workers by tying them to bourgeois nationalism.

Sources Liberation News Why Trump failed in his criminal war against Iran · Liberation News Stop Trump’s War on Iran!

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP highlights “Palestine activists jailed in unprecedented sentencing,” reporting on four activists hit with terror-law-adjacent charges — a genuine democratic-rights issue, but framed as a call to pressure and “march” rather than to build an independent political movement. “Reform UK and the global far right’s war on abortion rights” correctly links Farage’s party to the international far right, yet the SWP’s prescription remains broad “unite the resistance” fronts that in practice route opposition back toward Labour and the trade-union tops. The orientation: activist mobilization around real grievances, channeled into protest coalitions that avoid a break with Labourism.

Sources Socialist Worker Palestine activists jailed in unprecedented sentencing · Socialist Worker Reform UK and the global far right’s war on abortion rights

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

The IMT’s British section responds to a wave of far-right riots in Belfast, Glasgow, and Southampton with “RCP statement: Smash the right!”, calling for “community defense organizations” and demonstrations to physically confront the rioters, and with “Pogroms in the North of Ireland: Socialism or barbarism.” The analysis correctly identifies Farage’s demagogy and British imperialism as responsible, but the “community defense” line substitutes localized physical confrontation for the construction of a politically armed working-class movement, while the rebranded “Revolutionary Communist Party” pours energy into electoral self-promotion. The orientation: radical phrasemongering around a real fascist danger, detached from a worked-out strategy in the organized working class.

Sources communist.red RCP statement: Smash the right! · communist.red Pogroms in the North of Ireland: Socialism or barbarism

Left Voice

Left Voice (Trotskyist Fraction / PTS) covers the Bolivian uprising (Martínez and Maiello) and a piece on Trump’s “new offensive in Latin America” targeting Cuba and Venezuela, alongside reporting on Mexico City’s World Cup opening amid teacher protests and police repression. The coverage is militant and internationalist in tone, but the PTS’s signature “united front” formulations — recently criticized by the WSWS as a means of propping up the union bureaucracy and the Democratic Party — blunt the call for political independence, leaving the door open to blocs with reformist and bureaucratic forces.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Gulf migrant workers face starvation as Hormuz blockade severs food supply

The closure of the Strait of Hormuz has disrupted more than 80 percent of food imports to Gulf Cooperation Council states, spiking consumer prices 40–120 percent. The burden falls hardest on the millions of South Asian migrant workers who form the majority of the labor force in the UAE, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia, with minimal savings and no social safety net — a humanitarian dimension of the war the bourgeois press has largely ignored.

Recommended reading UN News Hormuz crisis strangling global economy, Guterres warns

2. World Bank report reveals a lost decade of development in the Global South

Behind the headline growth downgrade, the World Bank concedes that nearly a decade has passed with no progress narrowing the per-capita income gap between rich and poor nations. The energy and food shock is reversing years of poverty reduction across South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa — a concrete indictment of the imperialist world order that warrants socialist analysis.

Recommended reading World Bank Global Economic Prospects, June 2026

3. Yoon’s 30-year sentence and the use of military force for domestic repression

The drone-over-Pyongyang verdict establishes that a sitting South Korean president deployed covert military operations to manufacture a pretext for martial law. This is a case study in how ruling-class crisis drives the resort to authoritarian methods — directly relevant to the WSWS’s analysis of the global turn to dictatorship and its earlier coverage of the Korean martial law crisis.

Recommended reading CNN Yoon Suk Yeol sentenced to 30 years in drone case

4. Pakistan-administered Kashmir protests expose the partition’s democratic deficit

Mass protests by the Joint Awami Action Committee over reserved legislative seats highlight the political repression and democratic deficits built into the Kashmir partition structure itself. The unrest connects the colonial roots of the dispute to the contemporary suppression of dissent on both sides of the Line of Control — a story largely absent from English-language coverage.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Fallout from unrest in Pakistan-administered Kashmir

5. First human H5N5 case and the measles surge expose a gutted public-health system

The first documented human H5N5 infection, alongside 2,063 measles cases and 30 active outbreaks, unfolds as federal health authorities pursue anti-vaccine policy and slash surveillance funding. The convergence of a novel avian-flu spillover and the return of an eliminated childhood disease is a direct consequence of the dismantling of public health — a recurring WSWS theme deserving sustained coverage.

Recommended reading NEJM First confirmed human H5N5 infection

6. Death of Gordon Wood and the contested memory of the American Revolution

The death of the Pulitzer-winning historian of the American Revolution, at the 250th-anniversary moment, opens an opportunity to advance a Marxist assessment of the Revolution as a bourgeois revolution with unresolved class content — extending the WSWS’s critique of personality-driven popular history.

Recommended reading PBS NewsHour Gordon Wood dies at 92
