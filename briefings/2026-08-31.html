1. Iran strikes US bases in Jordan and UAE after American attack on Larak Island

The five-month US-Israeli war against Iran, which had gone comparatively quiet through August, escalated sharply over the weekend. On Sunday, US Central Command struck Larak Island — an Iranian-held islet southeast of Qeshm at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz — in the first American attack on Iranian territory in a month. CENTCOM spokesman Captain Tim Hawkins said Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps units were preparing to fire rockets carrying sea mines into the strait.

Iran retaliated overnight into Monday. The IRGC said it hit two US air bases in Jordan with ballistic missiles, causing “heavy damage,” struck US personnel at an air base in the United Arab Emirates, and shot down a US aircraft. Iran also reported seizing a bulk carrier near Bandar Abbas. Gulf states publicly condemned the Iranian strikes while continuing to host the American forces conducting the bombing.

Key facts:

  • The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed since early August; Brent crude climbed above $90 on Monday and both main contracts rose more than 2 percent.
  • Over the course of the war Iran has struck US facilities and allied territory in Kuwait, Oman, Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the UAE.
  • Andre Damon noted on the WSWS that US gasoline prices are up 88 cents year-over-year and consumer prices 3.4 percent, with 61 percent of Americans disapproving of the war — and that congressional Democrats have voted for every war appropriation while criticizing only its execution.
  • Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has opened a parallel front, “Operation Economic Outcast,” targeting third countries that trade with Iran.

2. Pentagon takes 35% equity stake in Venezuela’s oil under 25-year deal

Trump announced Friday night what he called “the biggest oil deal in world history”: a 25-year arrangement giving the United States a direct ownership position in Venezuela’s petroleum reserves. Interim president Delcy Rodríguez — installed after US special forces seized Nicolás Maduro in a January 3 night raid — detailed the terms over the weekend, saying the venture targets 1.5 million barrels per day across 17 fields with 65 billion barrels of proven potential, and promising $209 billion in revenue.

The structure is without modern precedent. The Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital is to hold a 35 percent equity stake acquired through “penny warrants” requiring essentially no capital outlay, plus preferential rights to purchase 20 percent of output at cost. The Trump administration says the US takes 55 percent of the joint venture’s oil. A private vehicle led by Venezuelan businessman Alejandro Betancourt — under money-laundering investigation in Spain and Switzerland, and whose extradition Trump officials moved to block — receives century-long development rights. The chief Pentagon spokesman has publicly denied that the Office of Strategic Capital “takes equity stakes in private companies,” contradicting the reported structure.

Key facts:

  • Former PDVSA president Rafael Ramírez calculates the $209 billion figure amounts to a 3.7 percent royalty rate — below the terms granted Standard Oil and Shell under the US-backed dictator Juan Vicente Gómez (1908–1935).
  • No vote was taken in any Venezuelan body. PDVSA workers and executives learned of the deal from press reports. The 1999 Constitution designates hydrocarbons “inalienable property of the Republic.”
  • Bloomberg reports Venezuela is weighing withdrawal from OPEC, of which it was a founding member in 1960.
  • Senate Democrats’ objection was narrow: one said the deal “is proof Trump put our service members at risk to get Venezuelan oil for his billionaire buddies” — a complaint about beneficiaries, not about seizure.
  • Chinese purchases of Venezuelan crude have been cut to zero and Russian joint ventures terminated.
  • Rodríguez says she intends to conclude further agreements with Chevron, Repsol and Shell. Andrea Lobo’s WSWS Perspective describes the arrangement as the Pentagon’s direct colonial ownership of a nation’s principal resource.

3. Nepal-China flood toll passes 750 with 3,000 missing

The confirmed death toll from the August 26 flash flood that swept down the Bhote Koshi River from Tibet into Nepal’s Rasuwa district has reached at least 750, with more than 3,000 people still missing. Nepal’s disaster agency reported 734 dead and 2,498 missing, including 589 foreign nationals; Chinese authorities confirmed 16 dead and 546 missing in Tibet. Authorities have still not determined a definitive cause — the weather was clear when the wall of water arrived, pointing toward a glacial lake outburst or landslide dam collapse upstream.

Reporting from Kathmandu indicates rescuers lost critical hours reaching hundreds of people trapped in hydropower tunnels along the river. The disaster destroyed bridges, schools, hospitals and 431.1 MW of generating capacity. Reconstruction is estimated at $4–5 billion.

Key facts:

  • Aid pledges from the United States, United Kingdom and European Union amount to a few million dollars each — a fraction of one percent of the reconstruction cost, and a rounding error against the military budgets those same governments are expanding.
  • The Rasuwa corridor carries the main Nepal-China trade route; its destruction cuts off a principal supply artery.
  • Rohantha De Silva’s WSWS report situates the catastrophe in the systematic absence of glacial-lake monitoring and early-warning infrastructure across the Himalayan belt.

4. ICE expands third-country deportation flights to eight African states

Internal federal documents obtained by CBS News show that over a ten-day period Immigration and Customs Enforcement operated three flights removing more than 100 people to Burundi, Cameroon, the Central African Republic, Equatorial Guinea, Eswatini, Liberia, Rwanda and Sierra Leone. None of the deportees were nationals of the countries to which they were sent. Those removed included Afghans and Cubans.

The American Prospect published first-person accounts from deportees on Monday. They describe being shackled at wrists, ankles and waist for the duration of flights, being refused bathroom access and forced to urinate on themselves, and — when they protested being sent to countries where they had no ties — being beaten, slammed to the floor and dragged by their chains, leaving scratches on their wrists and bruises on their necks. One Sierra Leonean man with a withholding-of-removal order was told he could not disembark in his own country and remained shackled aboard for another ten hours.

Key facts:

  • Rolling Stone reports that a portion of these removals are being conducted on military aircraft that are effectively untrackable by civilian flight-monitoring services.
  • Liberia joined the third-country deportation network on August 20, according to the Prospect.
  • Jacob Crosse’s WSWS report ties the flights to the buildout of a detention-contracting apparatus, noting that congressional Democrats have raised procedural objections while funding the agency conducting the removals.

5. NPR investigation finds US heat deaths five times the official count

NPR published Monday the results of a two-year investigation conducted with Boston University into heat mortality in the United States. Building a statistical model covering nearly every county in the country, the researchers estimate that on average between 2018 and 2025 roughly 9,000 deaths per year in the US were influenced by heat — approximately five times the official federal average. The full analysis is pending publication in a peer-reviewed journal.

The undercount is structural rather than accidental. Death certificate data is compiled inconsistently across jurisdictions; most coroners and medical examiners never test for or record heat as a contributing factor, and there is no national standard requiring them to. Deaths from cardiovascular and renal failure triggered or accelerated by extreme heat are recorded under those causes alone. The investigation was published as triple-digit temperatures settled over the central United States, with highs near 100°F forecast across eastern Kansas through at least Thursday.

Key facts:

  • The victims are overwhelmingly outdoor and warehouse workers, the elderly poor, and residents of housing without functioning air conditioning — a class distribution the official statistics obscure.
  • There is still no federal OSHA heat standard in force covering outdoor and indoor workers.
  • The finding directly bears on federal cuts to National Weather Service warning capacity, which the WSWS has documented in reporting on this summer’s storm and outage crises.

The European Council on Foreign Relations describes the German political centre as “crumbling,” with its leaders unable to get ahead of the crisis. Contracts expire tomorrow for 23,000 US Steel and Cleveland-Cliffs workers

Master agreements covering roughly 23,000 United Steelworkers members at US Steel and Cleveland-Cliffs expire on September 1. Trade press reporting through August indicates the two sides remain far apart. At Cleveland-Cliffs, the USW had not begun bargaining over economic issues including wages as of mid-August, with talks confined to attendance, paid time off, investment commitments, training, and safety and health. At US Steel, healthcare cost-shifting is the principal sticking point.

The deadline falls in the middle of Trump’s tariff war with Canada, which both companies have used to press for concessions on the argument that they need flexibility to capture protected market share. Samuel Davidson and Tom Hall argue on the WSWS that the USW’s nationalist framework disarms workers precisely when the companies are using the trade conflict as leverage, and that the answer is coordinated action with the 1,200 National Steel Car strikers in Hamilton and with Canadian steel locals — not a bidding war between national workforces.

FAA moves to fire two LaGuardia controllers before NTSB completes its investigation

The Federal Aviation Administration is moving to terminate two LaGuardia air traffic controllers who left their shifts roughly an hour early on the night of March 22, when Jazz Aviation flight 646 — operating as Air Canada Express 8646 from Montreal — touched down on runway 4 at 11:37 p.m. and struck an Oshkosh Striker 1500 airport firefighting vehicle crossing the runway. Both pilots were killed and 41 people were injured.

The agency is treating the early departures as timecard fraud. The controllers retain appeal rights. The NTSB investigation remains open and has not determined whether the early departures contributed to the accident.

The staffing picture is the salient fact: two controllers were on duty in the tower that night — the standard complement for that shift — with one working two positions while the other coordinated a separate emergency. Claude Delphian argues on the WSWS that the FAA is scapegoating individual workers for a chronic understaffing crisis whose origins lie in the 1981 mass firing of PATCO strikers, and that the aviation unions’ response has been to accept the disciplinary framework rather than contest it.

National Guard deployment in Washington extended through the end of 2026

Federalized National Guard troops will remain in Washington, D.C. through the end of the year, with roughly 2,400 currently stationed in the District. The Center for American Progress found the deployments produced no measurable reduction in urban crime. The normalization of standing military garrisons in American cities — with no sunset provision and bipartisan acquiescence in Congress — remains the essential democratic-rights question of the period.

US measles cases hit 2,777 as Pennsylvania reports the first deaths of 2026

The CDC’s latest weekly update records 2,777 confirmed US measles cases for 2026 — 211 added in the past week — spread across 43 states and the District of Columbia with 36 active outbreaks. Ninety-four percent of cases are in people who are unvaccinated or whose vaccination status is unknown. This makes 2026 the worst US measles year since 1991 and places the country’s elimination status, held since 2000, in jeopardy.

Pennsylvania reported the first two US measles deaths of 2026, both unvaccinated residents of Lancaster County. These are the state’s first measles deaths in 35 years. Pennsylvania’s case total now stands at 393. The collapse of childhood vaccination coverage is the direct product of the anti-vaccine campaign now conducted from within the federal public health apparatus itself.

Roman Space Telescope reaches orbit on Falcon Heavy

The $4.3 billion Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope launched at 7:26 a.m. EDT on August 30 aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy, separating successfully 31 minutes later and heading for the Sun-Earth L2 point roughly one million miles out. Its 2.4-metre primary mirror matches Hubble’s, but its survey speed is more than 1,000 times faster, covering 200 times more sky per image. Over five years it will survey more than a billion galaxies and roughly 100,000 exoplanets, mapping the expansion history of the universe to constrain the equation of state of dark energy and the distribution of dark matter.

Bryan Dyne’s WSWS analysis sets the telescope’s entire lifetime cost against the $1.5 trillion US military budget request — the observatory costs less than three-tenths of one percent of a single year’s war spending.

COVID rising in 48 states as updated vaccines clear the FDA

CDC data as of August 26 show COVID-19 infections growing or likely growing in 48 states, with none declining. On August 28 the FDA approved updated 2026–2027 monovalent COVID vaccines from Moderna, Pfizer-BioNTech and Novavax-Sanofi targeting the XFG variant, ahead of a September rollout. The late-summer wave is thus arriving in advance of the vaccination campaign meant to blunt it — a sequencing failure that has recurred every year since the abandonment of elimination.

H5N1 jumps to a mammal in Australia

Australia confirmed its first H5N1 infection in a mammal — a long-nosed fur seal in South Australia. Confirmed wild-bird detections rose from 101 to 236 in under a week, including 49 dead and 35 sick terns near Adelaide. Australia had been the last inhabited continent spared by the panzootic. The CDC’s August 7 update reported no unusual human influenza activity in the United States.

Arts and culture

The world continues to absorb the death of Dolly Parton, who died in Nashville on August 25 at 80 after what her publicist described as a brief battle with cancer. James Brewer’s WSWS appreciation traces her trajectory from grinding poverty in the Smoky Mountains of east Tennessee — one of twelve children in a one-room cabin — to a body of songwriting that includes “Jolene,” “Coat of Many Colors” and “I Will Always Love You,” a song she famously declined to sell outright to Elvis Presley’s management, retaining a copyright that made her fortune.

Parton’s significance is not adequately captured by the rags-to-riches formula that has dominated the obituaries. Her strongest material is rooted in a specific social experience — rural Appalachian poverty, the position of women within it, the migration of that population into wage labor. “Coat of Many Colors” is a small masterpiece of class feeling written without a trace of self-pity or condescension. Her 1980 film debut in 9 to 5, and the title song she wrote for it, entered mass consciousness as one of the very few popular American works of its decade to treat the workplace as a site of collective grievance and collective action. The Imagination Library, which has distributed hundreds of millions of books to children, reflected genuine conviction; it also stands as a monument to what philanthropy is called upon to substitute for where public provision has been dismantled.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “The Pentagon takes control of Venezuela’s oil: Imperialist thievery and the bankruptcy of bourgeois nationalism” by Andrea Lobo — read in full on wsws.org

Beyond the terms of the deal reported above, the Perspective’s principal contribution is historical and programmatic. Lobo places the seizure in a century-long arc: the 1902 European gunboat blockade of Venezuelan ports over debts; the 1904 Roosevelt Corollary asserting US “police power” in the hemisphere; Gómez’s handover of the Maracaibo fields to Standard Oil and Shell; the overthrow of Rómulo Gallegos within months of his 50-50 tax-split law; and the 1989 Caracazo uprising against IMF austerity, which Lobo calls “the decisive experience of the Venezuelan working class in the 20th century.”

The core of the article is a systematic demolition of Chavismo. “Socialism of the 21st Century,” Lobo writes, was in fact “national reformism funded by oil rent.” Under Chávez the private sector’s share of GDP reached 70 percent; employers’ share of national wealth hit a record 48.8 percent in 2008; Venezuela had the world’s most profitable banks and, by 2012, its best-performing stock market. Invoking Trotsky on Cárdenas’s Mexican nationalizations — bourgeois nationalist governments “veer between foreign and domestic capital,” so equating state ownership with socialism is “an outright deception” — Lobo argues Chavismo suppressed the only social force capable of resisting imperialism.

Lobo then cites David North’s 1990 assessment that the dissolution of the USSR ended the “post-colonial era”: the Soviet Union’s existence had given bourgeois nationalist regimes room to maneuver, and Stalinism’s support was always self-preservation, never principled anti-imperialism. Three and a half decades of continuous war followed. Venezuela is the sharpest case. With the opposition despised and the Chavistas openly administering the plunder, Lobo concludes that Washington has exhausted its political options and is left with “brute force through the Bolivarian National Guard and US Marines.” Notable detail: TIME reports Rubio effectively supervises Venezuela’s oil revenues and public finances by near-daily WhatsApp contact, and Maduro’s son has publicly thanked Trump and Rubio while his father sits in a New York prison.

Pro-government Turkish journalist calls for extermination of Syria’s Arab Alawites — Hakan Özal

The one article in today’s output with no counterpart anywhere in the Western press. Kemal Öztürk, a journalist closely tied to Erdoğan’s circle, called on Turkish television for the “slaughter” of Syria’s Alawite population, while simultaneously denying that the HTS regime has carried out massacres. Reuters has documented approximately 1,479 Alawites killed in the March 2025 coastal attacks, in which Turkish-backed militias participated.

Özal’s analysis places this incitement in the logic of the post-Assad settlement: the HTS government in Damascus, installed with Turkish and tacit US-Israeli support, rests on no popular base and can only consolidate itself by mobilizing sectarian and ethnic hatreds — against Alawites, Kurds, Druze and Christians in turn. Ankara’s interest is a Syrian client state weak enough to be dependent and fragmented enough to prevent Kurdish consolidation on its southern border. The article’s political conclusion is that the systematic incitement of Sunni-Alawite antagonism is aimed at preventing the emergence of any unified working-class opposition across Syria, Turkey, Lebanon and Iraq, and that only an internationalist perspective can cut across it. The complete silence of the Western media on this call for extermination — issued on television by a figure with government ties, in a NATO member state — is itself the point.

German auto industry moves into arms production—just as under the Nazis — Ludwig Weller

Weller documents the conversion of German automotive capacity to weapons manufacture against the backdrop of 142,500 auto-sector jobs eliminated since 2018. Rheinmetall’s market capitalization has risen from €4 billion in 2022 to €54 billion — a more than thirteen-fold increase driven entirely by state rearmament orders.

The historical parallel is drawn deliberately and with specifics: the same conversion of civilian industrial capacity, the same argument about national necessity, and in some cases the same corporate families involved in the Nazi rearmament programme of the 1930s. Weller’s political point is directed at the IG Metall bureaucracy, which has responded to the destruction of auto employment by promoting arms conversion as a jobs programme — accepting the premise that German workers must accept militarism as the price of employment. The article insists that the answer to job destruction in auto is not the manufacture of shells but a unified struggle of European and international autoworkers against the corporations and the war drive together.

Australia: Teachers speak out against union sellout in Victoria — Our reporters

Victorian teachers narrowly approved, on a third ballot, a four-year AEU-negotiated agreement providing 28–32 percent pay increases that opponents calculate as a real wage cut against cumulative inflation. Approval reached 79 percent only after members were told that rejection would send the dispute to the Fair Work Commission, where the outcome would be worse — the bureaucracy’s standard mechanism for converting fear into ratification.

The agreement contains nothing on class sizes or workload, the two issues teachers identified as decisive. WSWS reporters document the emergence of an independent rank-and-file network organizing outside the AEU structure, and the article generalizes from Victoria to the international pattern: educators’ organizations functioning as instruments for enforcing government wage caps under conditions where public education is being systematically defunded.

Gary, Indiana residents still without power weeks after storm — Kathleen Martin, Usman Khan

Two weeks after a derecho, hundreds of residents of Gary, Indiana — an overwhelmingly working-class and low-income city — remain without electricity. Martin and Khan attribute the duration of the outage to NIPSCO’s deliberate cost-cutting on distribution maintenance: reduced vegetation management and tree trimming, and understaffing of line crews, decisions made to protect shareholder returns in a regulated utility with guaranteed rates of return.

The report connects the outage to federal reductions in National Weather Service warning and forecasting capacity, which curtailed advance notice of the storm. The residents interviewed describe spoiled food they cannot afford to replace, medical equipment that cannot be run, and elderly neighbors in unairconditioned homes — the concrete texture of what the aggregate mortality statistics measure.
