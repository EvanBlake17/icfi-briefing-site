1. Iran strikes Kuwait airport as US-Iran ceasefire reaches “life support”

Iranian drones and missiles struck Kuwait International Airport on June 3, killing one person — confirmed as an Indian national — and wounding at least 63, including passengers and airport workers. The attack marks the most serious escalation since the April 8 ceasefire that paused hostilities begun February 28 with joint US-Israeli strikes on Iran. Trump now describes the ceasefire as being on “life support.”

Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps claimed the terminal was damaged by a failed US-made interceptor; US Central Command called the claim “false” and described the strike as “deliberate, calculated and unjustified.” Iran suspended indirect negotiations on June 1, citing ongoing ceasefire violations, and a senior Iranian military officer declared renewed war “inevitable.”

Key facts: - US forces intercepted two Iranian ballistic missiles targeting American troops in Kuwait earlier in the week and conducted “self-defense strikes” on Iranian radar and drone sites. - CENTCOM reports redirecting 121 commercial vessels and disabling 5 ships during its ongoing blockade. - Gulf states including Bahrain condemned the Kuwait strike, deepening regional fault lines. - The lone fatality — a migrant worker — underscores the war’s toll on the more than one million Indian nationals laboring in Kuwait, a class with no political voice in the conflict.

2. House passes Iran war powers resolution in rebuke of Trump

The Republican-controlled House voted 215–208 on June 3 to direct Trump to end hostilities with Iran — the first time such a measure has passed either chamber since the war began over three months ago. Four Republicans broke with their party to join unanimous Democratic support. Trump dismissed the vote as “meaningless,” branding the four defectors “grandstanders.”

The measure is largely symbolic: Democrats lack the votes to pass it through the Senate, and Trump would veto it regardless. Secretary of State Marco Rubio told Congress that peace talks are “faltering,” with Iran insisting any deal address Lebanon, even as Trump claimed talks were proceeding at a “rapid pace.”

The vote exposes the absence of any structural antiwar opposition within the US state. Both parties have prosecuted the war for over three months; the resolution became possible only because of a narrow Republican fracture, not a genuine challenge to the war drive.

3. Israel-Lebanon ceasefire framework agreed without Hezbollah at the table

Israeli and Lebanese negotiators meeting in Washington on June 3 announced an expanded ceasefire framework requiring Hezbollah to achieve a “complete cessation of fire” and evacuate all operatives south of the Litani River. Hezbollah was not represented in the talks and reportedly continued firing rockets at Israeli positions after the announcement, raising immediate doubts about implementation.

Key facts: - Israeli forces killed at least 9 people in Lebanon on June 3, including one soldier; strikes approached Beirut’s southern outskirts. - At least 3,433 people have been killed and 10,395 wounded in Lebanon since the conflict escalated March 2; 26 Israeli soldiers have died in fighting with Hezbollah. - Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi warned that “any attack on Beirut will have grave consequences and lead to a full resumption of war.” - Netanyahu is pressing to keep the Lebanon and Iran negotiations separate as he prepares for Israeli elections this fall.

4. US proposes forced-labor tariffs on 60 economies

The Trump administration announced June 3 that it plans 10% tariffs on 16 economies — including Canada, Mexico, the EU, Taiwan and the UK — for allegedly failing to enforce forced-labor bans. An additional 44 partners, among them China, Japan, India, South Korea and Switzerland, would face 12.5% tariffs; Brazil faces separate 25% duties under Section 301.

The legal basis is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, deployed after the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s IEEPA tariffs in February 2026. Current temporary 10% tariffs expire July 24, and public hearings on the new duties begin July 7.

Key facts: - EU Parliament trade chief Bernd Lange called the accusations “absurd,” saying the EU has “the world’s most stringent rules against products made with forced labour.” - China’s Foreign Ministry denied all allegations; Canadian PM Mark Carney pledged forthcoming forced-labor legislation. - Trump’s tariffs now amount to the largest US tax increase as a share of GDP since 1993 — an average of $1,500 per US household in 2026. - The “labor protection” framing obscures the mechanism: broad consumer-price increases borne overwhelmingly by the working class.

Sources PBS NewsHour US plans extra tariffs after forced-labor probe · Al Jazeera US cites forced labour concerns as grounds for new tariffs

5. Musk set to become world’s first trillionaire on SpaceX IPO

Within roughly ten days, Elon Musk is poised to become the world’s first trillionaire when SpaceX prices what would be the largest initial public offering in history. The company is targeting a $1.75 trillion valuation and a $75 billion raise, with Goldman Sachs leading a 21-bank syndicate and the roadshow beginning around June 4. Musk’s roughly 41% stake would be worth some $700 billion at the offering price; the Wall Street Journal already pegs his wealth at $970 billion — about 3% of US economic output, double the share John D. Rockefeller held at his death in 1937.

The offering sits at the center of a tech-IPO bubble. Anthropic has moved toward a public listing at roughly $900 billion, and OpenAI is expected to follow; together the three firms are worth more than $3.5 trillion, about 10% of US GDP. SpaceX’s price is 93.7 times annual revenue — more than five times Tesla’s multiple — even as its own prospectus admits “a history of net losses”: $4.94 billion lost in 2025, $4.28 billion in Q1 2026, and $41.3 billion cumulatively since founding. Only Starlink is profitable, yet $26.5 trillion of the company’s claimed $28.5 trillion addressable market is attributed to AI, a money-losing line.

As Andre Damon wrote in the WSWS Perspective, “The investors bidding SpaceX toward $1.75 trillion are paying for the promise that the working class will be impoverished” — technology wielded to automate jobs and suppress wages, with the tech sector already cutting more than 140,000 jobs this year.

6. Russia hits Chernihiv; Ukraine sets St. Petersburg oil terminal ablaze

Russia struck Ukraine’s Chernihiv region on June 4, damaging buildings and triggering rescue operations. President Zelensky confirmed that Ukrainian drones struck and set ablaze an oil terminal in St. Petersburg — timed to coincide with the city’s hosting of Putin’s annual St. Petersburg International Economic Forum, his flagship showcase.

Key facts: - Over the past year (June 2025–June 2026), Russia made a net territorial gain of approximately 1,427 square miles — roughly 0.6% of Ukraine’s 1991 territory. - Competing recent estimates underscore the front’s near-stalemate: the Economist reported Ukraine recaptured ~97 sq mi in 30 days; ISW recorded Russia losing a net 14 sq mi last week; DeepState OSINT showed Russia gaining a net 3 sq mi over four weeks. - The strike on Russian energy infrastructure during the forum was a calculated blow to Putin’s effort to project economic stability.

7. Gaza death toll passes 72,900 as Israel violates ceasefire

As of June 1, at least 72,941 Palestinians have been killed, including 20,179 children, according to the Palestinian Health Ministry; 72,004 individual names have been confirmed. Israel has violated the October 2025 ceasefire at least 3,005 times through May 27 via air strikes, artillery and direct shootings.

At least 900 Palestinians have been killed since the October 10, 2025 ceasefire announcement, with at least 200 additional civilians killed since the Iran war began February 28. UN Human Rights Chief Volker Türk stated that “Palestinians across Gaza remain unsafe six months on from the ceasefire announcement.” A UN Commission of Inquiry noted in April that the US-Iran war and the Lebanon conflict have largely eclipsed international attention to Gaza — a structural reduction in scrutiny tied directly to Washington’s military and diplomatic engagement with Israel.

8. Sudan’s drone war systematically targets hospitals

More than 500 civilians were killed by drone strikes in Sudan between January 1 and March 15, 2026 alone — one of the deadliest active conflicts in the world, yet covered only sporadically in Western media preoccupied with Iran. On March 20 (Eid al-Fitr), a strike on El Daein Teaching Hospital in East Darfur killed at least 70 people — including 7 women, 13 children, 1 doctor and 2 nurses — and wounded 146, an attack attributed to the Rapid Support Forces.

Key facts: - MSF has documented a systematic pattern of drone strikes rendering health facilities non-functional, including Al Jabalain Hospital (10 killed, maternity ward struck), El-Obeid British Hospital (12 wounded) and a Kouik military hospital (22 killed). - OHCHR warns the strikes show the “devastating impact of high-tech and relatively cheap weapons in populated areas.” - The civil war has now lasted over two years with no ceasefire in sight.

CBS fires Scott Pelley after clash with Bari Weiss management

CBS News fired veteran “60 Minutes” correspondent Scott Pelley on June 2, a day after he confronted newly installed leadership at a staff meeting, accusing editor-in-chief Bari Weiss of trying to “kill” the newsmagazine and charging that management had ordered him to “inject falsehoods and bias into a politically sensitive story.” Pelley’s termination letter, signed by new executive producer Nick Bilton, cited dismissal “for cause.” His firing ends a nearly 40-year run at the network.

The Pelley firing caps a wave of departures — correspondents Sharyn Alfonsi and Cecilia Vega, executive producer Tanya Simon, and anchor Anderson Cooper. As Patrick Martin notes in the WSWS, the editorial dismantling traces to billionaire David Ellison, who orchestrated CBS’s rightward turn after Paramount’s acquisition and installed Weiss — part of a broader authoritarian consolidation of major media under figures aligned with the Trump administration.

New York probes Compass after $1.6 billion real estate merger

The New York attorney general’s antitrust bureau has opened an investigation into Compass after it acquired rival Anywhere Real Estate for $1.6 billion. Coming after years of National Association of Realtors commission-fixing scandals and class-action settlements, the investigation adds to scrutiny of an industry that exerts decisive influence over housing costs for tens of millions.

US measles cases near 2,000; elimination status at risk

As of May 28, the US has recorded 1,983 confirmed measles cases in 2026 across 40 jurisdictions and 30 outbreaks, with 92% in unvaccinated or vaccination-unknown individuals. The 2025 full-year total was 2,288 — meaning 2026 is on track to surpass it well before year-end. The CDC has formally warned that summer travel will accelerate spread, and researchers say the US is now at real risk of losing its measles-elimination status, a public-health achievement dating to 2000.

Long COVID affects ~18 million Americans, double official estimates

A Mass General Brigham study published in JAMA Network Open, applying an AI algorithm to electronic health records from 460,000 patients across 58 hospitals, found that roughly 16% (1 in 6) developed long COVID — over 18 million Americans, about twice current surveillance estimates. More than 10 million cases would be entirely invisible to standard diagnostic-code tracking. The gap between true burden and official counts carries major implications for healthcare resourcing and disability policy.

Trump signs narrowed AI executive order

On June 2, Trump signed an executive order requiring AI companies to voluntarily submit frontier models for government security review up to 30 days before public release. An earlier draft had proposed a 90-day mandatory window, which industry successfully opposed. The narrowed, voluntary scope marks a significant concession to the AI industry — and sets the US approach in sharp contrast to the EU AI Act’s binding requirements, even as SpaceX, Anthropic and OpenAI race toward trillion-dollar valuations built on automation.

Roundup: - H5N1 bird flu: The US has confirmed 71 cumulative human cases since 2024 (2 deaths), including 3 with no known animal exposure; WHO confirmed a previously unreported fatal H5N1 case in a child in Bangladesh in Q1 2026. - COVID-19: Hospitalizations hit a historic low of 0.2 per 100,000 (week ending May 23) and test positivity held at 0.79% — the lowest since spring 2021 — but wastewater shows early upticks in the Midwest and Northeast. - GLP-1 drugs: A study of over 600,000 US veterans found semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) associated with meaningfully reduced addiction and overdose rates, pointing toward new uses in addiction medicine. - Photonics: Scientists unveiled a chip integrating light generation, steering and detection on a single device — a long-standing barrier to practical photonic computing for energy-hungry AI workloads. - Climate: NOAA data shows an 82% probability of El Niño emerging by July and 96% it persists into winter 2026–27, potentially the strongest on record (see WSWS coverage).

Arts and culture

The collapse of Trump’s “Great American State Fair” is the day’s most revealing cultural story. The planned 16-day spectacle on the National Mall, marking the United States’ 250th anniversary, unraveled as performer after performer withdrew on learning of the event’s partisan affiliation with the Trump-backed “Freedom 250” organization. The Commodores, Martina McBride, Morris Day and the Time, Bret Michaels, Young MC, and — most pointedly — Milli Vanilli’s Fab Morvan, who called it a “circus,” all distanced themselves. McBride said she had agreed to a “nonpartisan event but that turned out to be misleading”; Young MC noted “the artists were never told about any political involvement.”

Trump responded by suggesting he personally replace the performers and confirmed he will headline the June 24 opening himself. As David Walsh argues in the WSWS, the debacle “indicates something about the administration’s intense isolation” — a regime that cannot fill a stage marshaling state pageantry it lacks the popular support to sustain. The episode is a study in the gap between official nationalism and a population that overwhelmingly rejects it; even commercial entertainers, calculating reputational risk, would not lend their names.

The week also brought significant losses: - Peabo Bryson, the two-time Grammy-winning R&B singer whose lush balladry defined a generation of film duets (“A Whole New World,” “Beauty and the Beast”), died June 2 at 75 in Marietta, Georgia, following a stroke. His career bridged soul, pop and the orchestral arrangements of late-20th-century American popular music. - Marcia Lucas, the Academy Award-winning film editor whose work on Star Wars (and American Graffiti and Taxi Driver) shaped the rhythm of New Hollywood, died May 27 at 80. Long underrecognized relative to her collaborators, Lucas’s editing was decisive to the pace and emotional clarity of some of the era’s most influential films.

These deaths are a reminder of an era of mass cultural craft — singers and editors working within, but often transcending, the commercial apparatus — at a moment when the culture industry is increasingly dominated by speculative tech valuations and AI-driven cost-cutting. The destruction of “60 Minutes” (above) belongs to the same process: the subordination of journalism and art alike to billionaire ownership and political diktat.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “Elon Musk to become the world’s first trillionaire: The case for expropriation” by Andre Damon — read in full on wsws.org. (Summarized in Top stories above.) Beyond the market analysis, the Perspective’s programmatic core is its conclusion: Musk’s wealth must be “seized and made the common property of society,” and AI and automation, in working-class hands, could “enormously improve productivity and the living standards of the population.” Damon directly attacks Bernie Sanders’ New York Times proposal for an “American AI sovereign wealth fund,” noting Sanders favorably quoted Musk endorsing “Universal HIGH INCOME” — a scheme oligarchs float “to forestall more radical demands.” He cites Marx’s Capital Vol. III on the credit system’s “double character,” the USDA’s finding that 47.9 million Americans cannot reliably afford food, and that the richest 1% now own a record 31.7% of national wealth. Workers and youth oppose Iran war and Labor’s austerity at meetings in Australia and NZ — Our reporters

The SEP (Australia) and Socialist Equality Group (NZ) held public meetings opposing the US-led war on Iran and linking it to domestic Labor austerity. Attendees — students, workers, retirees — connected military spending increases to social cuts; one retired worker stressed that it is “young people… 16- to 28-year-olds” being recruited to the front lines. Speakers rejected the Labor Party as a vehicle for workers’ interests, with a final meeting set for Newcastle and online that Saturday.

New EU return regulation: Attacks on democratic rights and mass expulsions — Peter Schwarz

Schwarz examines a new EU return regulation taking effect June 12, alongside broader asylum reforms. Rejected asylum seekers lose appeal rights, detention can extend to 30 months, and “return hubs” in countries like Rwanda and Libya are authorized for offshore detention. Crucially, Schwarz documents that the regulation was drafted with the active participation of the far-right AfD and National Rally — direct collaboration between conservatives and fascists, dismantling post-WWII refugee protections. He frames it as a ruling-class response to growing class resistance.

UAW bureaucracy releases “highlights” of fourth sellout deal at Nexteer — Jerry White

The UAW has presented a fourth tentative agreement to 1,700 Nexteer Automotive workers in Saginaw, Michigan — who already rejected three contracts. The starting wage of $19.50/hour reaches only $27/hour by 2030; adjusted for inflation, that top rate equals what workers earned in 2005, when its real value was $45.65 in today’s dollars. The signing bonus rises only from $2,500 to $3,000, with no layoff protections for 400 threatened jobs. White calls for unity with striking American Axle workers and the formation of independent rank-and-file committees.

Victorian teachers oppose AEU/Labor deal on wages and conditions — Our reporters

Three Victorian teachers — Lily (25+ years), Kristy and Joshua (seven years) — publicly oppose an Australian Education Union deal with Victoria’s Labor government offering 28.3–32.4% increases over four years. All three argue it ignores workload, class sizes and the real cost of living; Kristy says she survives “week to week,” and Joshua criticizes the union executive’s “quarter million-dollar salaries.” The article advocates rank-and-file committees and contact with the Committee for Public Education.

Sri Lanka receives further IMF installments and demands for escalating austerity — Saman Gunadasa

The IMF’s $695 million disbursement (total ~$2.4 billion) is conditioned on phasing out energy subsidies by September and maintaining “cost-recovery pricing.” Strikes by government printing workers and unrest among electricity board employees reflect mounting anger, even as the government strengthens Emergency Regulations restricting labor organizing. Gunadasa presents the IMF program as a mechanism for extracting austerity on behalf of international finance capital. (Macroeconomic figures appear in World economy above.)

New York Democrats and Mamdani administration provide platform for Israel Day pro-genocide march — Jacob Crosse

New York’s May 31 Israel Day parade featured Israeli Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, Governor Kathy Hochul, Senator Chuck Schumer and thousands of police. Mayor Zohran Mamdani (DSA) did not attend, but his administration issued permits and provided security, and Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch marched. Crosse argues Mamdani’s absence was performative — his administration “ensured that wanted war criminals and fascistic Zionist officials could march freely” — and that the DSA and Ocasio-Cortez supply “left cover” for Democratic complicity in the Gaza genocide.

Philippine workers hit as Iran war continues — Dante Pastrana

The Philippines depends on the Middle East for 98% of crude imports. Inflation hit 7.2% (April), with gasoline inflation at 59.6%, diesel at 122.7%, fertilizer up 48.7%, and rice production projected to fall 20–50%. Mass transport strikes erupted in March and April as drivers reported earning “barely 200 to 300 pesos a day”; Marcos declared a national energy emergency in March, granting authority to suppress labor “disruptions.” Pastrana frames the crisis as a direct product of imperialism, not circumstance.

The criminalization of the Noida worker protests — Kranti Kumara

Tens of thousands of workers in Noida and Manesar rose up in April demanding adequate wages, humane hours (against 12–14 hour shifts) and enforcement of overtime law. The BJP state government responded with mass arrests, torture, and preventive detention under the colonial-era National Security Act (up to 12 months without trial), branding the movement “outsiders” with “Pakistan connections” — a narrative evidence suggests was manufactured after police provoked violence with a baton charge. Kumara documents the NSA detention of software engineer Aditya Anand, journalist Satyam Verma and theatre practitioner Aakriti Choudhary, and draws parallels to the 2012 Maruti Suzuki frame-up.

Nationwide hospital protests against planned cuts in Germany — Markus Salzmann

Over 50 German hospitals held Verdi-backed protests against the “Contribution Rate Stabilisation Act,” which would cut €5.1 billion from hospitals in 2027. A Hospital Rating Report projects 67% of hospitals could face annual losses by 2027 and 36% of locations may close; DKG chief Gerald Gaß called it “a kind of endgame for hospitals,” while Chancellor Merz termed the cuts a “historic reform.” Salzmann argues the protest cannot succeed if left to Verdi’s symbolic actions and ties the cuts to rearmament priorities over social spending.

Canadian border authorities handing refugees over to Trump’s ICE — Félix Gauthier

Canadian authorities are systematically transferring asylum seekers — including Haitian student Markens Appolon and Tibetan refugee Tenzin, left without medical care — directly to US ICE custody under the Safe Third Country Agreement, even as US policy hardens. Bill C-12 expands ministerial discretion over enforcement and facilitates data-sharing with foreign agencies. Gauthier rejects “Team Canada” nationalism as a cover for complicity and calls for cross-border working-class solidarity.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s current output is dominated by the consolidation of the Zohran Mamdani mayoralty into an electoral machine. “NYC Socialists Are Trying to Expand Their Electoral Wins” reports NYC-DSA’s slate for this month’s Democratic primaries — one state legislative candidate in Buffalo, seven in the city, and two for Congress — using Mamdani’s victory as a springboard. The political line is explicit: socialism is to be built through Democratic Party ballot lines and the gradual accumulation of offices.

A companion piece, “Zohran Mamdani and the Contradiction of Democratic Socialism,” concedes that his record is “filled with successes, but also evidence of the contradictions between socialist politics and governing the capitalist state.” This admission is presented not as grounds to break with the capitalist state but as a managerial problem to be navigated. The anti-Marxist content is the identification of socialism with the administration of a capitalist municipality — precisely the perspective the WSWS exposed in its coverage of the Israel Day parade, where Mamdani’s administration issued permits and security for a pro-genocide march. Orientation: the integration of the “democratic socialist” movement into the Democratic Party as its left flank.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL maintains an active antiwar profile, headlined by its standing statement “Stop Trump’s War on Iran!” and related agitation against the US-Israeli assault. Its denunciations of imperialism are sharp, and it correctly identifies the war as serving ruling-class interests. The political weakness lies in its programmatic conclusion: the PSL calls for a broad protest “movement” and pressure on Washington, while its orientation toward the Democratic Party milieu and popular-frontism leaves the strategic question — the independent mobilization of the working class against the capitalist state that wages the war — unanswered. Orientation: militant in rhetoric, but channeling antiwar sentiment into protest politics rather than a struggle for power.

Socialist Alternative

Socialist Alternative’s recent pieces — “Iran War: Deal Or No Deal, The World Is In Crisis” and “Labor Must Stand Against War!” — document the war’s economic devastation, noting UK petrol up 24p and diesel 46p per litre since the war began, and warning that “working people are told to prepare for sacrifice while corporations profit.” The analysis of the energy shock is concrete. But the prescription — that “labor” and the existing unions must “stand against war” — adapts to the very trade-union bureaucracies that have suppressed independent struggle, substituting appeals to the apparatus for the building of rank-and-file organizations. Orientation: correct identification of the crisis, reformist channeling of the response through the union officialdom.

Socialist Worker / SWP

The SWP’s Iran coverage emphasizes diaspora “voices” and the wave of protests inside Iran triggered by the currency crisis, interviewing an Iranian historian and activists who say “let us decide our own future.” The framing centers democratic protest and national self-determination. Absent is any analysis of the political vacuum filled by bourgeois-nationalist and pro-imperialist forces, or of the need for a Trotskyist leadership in the working class. The SWP’s “movementism” treats spontaneous protest as sufficient. Orientation: democratic and antiwar in sympathy, but evading the question of revolutionary perspective and leadership.

Left Voice

Left Voice continues to promote a “united front” tactic that the WSWS, in a June 2 polemic, identified as aiming “to prop up the union bureaucracy and Democratic Party.” While Left Voice presents itself as Trotskyist and offers more class-struggle language than its rivals, its practical orientation — alliances with reformist and bureaucratic forces in the name of “unity” — subordinates the independent program of the working class to the apparatuses the WSWS insists must be politically defeated. Orientation: left-Trotskyist phraseology covering an adaptation to the existing bureaucratic-reformist structures.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Indian migrant worker killed in Iran’s strike on Kuwait airport

The sole confirmed fatality in Iran’s June 3 attack was an Indian national — a migrant worker among the more than one million Indians laboring in Kuwait, largely in low-wage construction and service jobs. Their legal precarity and total absence of political voice make them disproportionately exposed to the war’s collateral effects, a class dimension entirely absent from mainstream coverage.

Recommended reading PBS NewsHour Iran strikes Kuwait’s main airport and kills 1

2. Sudan’s drone war on hospitals — a civilian catastrophe ignored by the West

Over 500 civilians were killed by drone strikes in Sudan in the first ten weeks of 2026, including systematic RSF attacks on hospitals such as the 70-killed Eid strike in El Daein. One of the world’s deadliest active conflicts proceeds with virtually no international political response, eclipsed by the Iran war. A class analysis of how cheap drone warfare dismantles medical infrastructure is warranted.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Drone strike on Sudan hospital kills 10, MSF says

3. Compass real estate monopoly and the housing crisis

The Compass–Anywhere merger created a single brokerage controlling over 200,000 agents, now under New York antitrust investigation. The consolidation of residential real estate into a near-monopoly directly affects housing costs for working-class families, building on years of NAR commission-fixing. The class dimensions of housing-market concentration deserve treatment beyond the business pages.

Recommended reading GoLocalProv 5 big news stories overnight, June 4, 2026

4. Albania protests against Kushner coastal development

Demonstrations against the Kushner luxury project pit ordinary Albanians against politically connected foreign capital commodifying the coastline, with police deployed against protesters. The pattern — opaque government approval of deals with US political figures, dispossession at Europe’s periphery — merits analysis beyond the “embarrassing for Trump” frame.

Recommended reading The Standard Morning Recap, June 4, 2026

5. Long COVID’s true burden — 18 million Americans, half of them invisible

A new JAMA Network Open study finds long COVID affects roughly twice the officially estimated number of Americans, with over 10 million cases invisible to standard tracking. The systematic undercounting — and the policy of “moving on” from the pandemic that produced it — has direct implications for disability, healthcare access and the working class’s right to health.

Recommended reading EurekAlert Long COVID burden double official estimates

6. The symbolic Iran war powers vote and the bankruptcy of bourgeois opposition

The 215–208 House vote was widely reported as a “rebuke,” but it is non-binding, cannot pass the Senate, and would be vetoed. A socialist analysis would examine why even this gesture required four Republican defectors — and what its emptiness reveals about the absence of any antiwar opposition within the US political establishment.
