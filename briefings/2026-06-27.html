1. US strikes Iran after drone attack on cargo ship in Strait of Hormuz

The United States launched air strikes against Iranian military targets on June 26, just nine days after the two governments signed a Memorandum of Understanding extending a pause in hostilities for 60 days. The strikes followed a June 25 attack in which Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps launched at least four drones at vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, one of which struck the Singapore-flagged cargo ship Ever Lovely off the Omani coast, damaging its bridge windows. No casualties were reported in either the drone attack or the US response.

The episode shatters the fiction that the June 17 deal represented a genuine peace. As Kevin Reed wrote on the WSWS, Vice President JD Vance’s statement that “violence will be met with violence” exposes the MOU as “political cover for continued military escalation rather than a genuine peace framework.”

Key facts: - Six US land-based aircraft struck four targets along the Strait and on Iran’s Qeshm Island, hitting missile and drone storage facilities and coastal radar sites; CENTCOM called it “a powerful response to Iran’s dangerous behavior.” - Trump called the drone attack “a foolish violation” of the ceasefire and, asked about consequences, told reporters: “You’ll find out.” - The June 17 MOU required Iran to ensure “safe, toll-free” Strait passage “using best efforts”; Tehran’s Persian Gulf Strait Authority responded that ships using “unapproved routes” would not receive safe-passage guarantees. - On June 27, Trump said he would bomb Iran again “without question” if intelligence showed dangerous uranium enrichment.

2. Supreme Court guts asylum and TPS, expands gun carry in 6-3 rulings

The US Supreme Court’s six-justice conservative majority issued a cluster of rulings on June 26 that dismantled core protections for immigrants and refugees and broadened the reach of the Second Amendment, all splitting along the same ideological lines. Two of the immigration rulings were authored by Justice Samuel Alito.

In Mullin v. Doe, the Court barred federal courts from reviewing the Secretary of Homeland Security’s Temporary Protected Status determinations — clearing the way for the Trump administration to revoke TPS from immigrants who fled war and disaster. As John Burton noted on the WSWS, the ruling threatens roughly 1.3 million people, including some 350,000 Haitians, 50,000 Syrians, and hundreds of thousands from Venezuela, Somalia, and Ethiopia. In Mullin v. Al Otro Lado, the Court held that asylum seekers do not legally “arrive in the United States” until they physically cross the border, reinstating “metering” and nullifying the right to apply for asylum at ports of entry.

Key facts: - Justice Sotomayor warned from the bench that “more people will die,” invoking the M.S. St. Louis, the ship of Jewish refugees turned away from the US in 1939. - In Wolford v. Lopez (6-3), the Court struck down Hawaii’s requirement that concealed-carry permit holders obtain property-owner permission before carrying handguns in restaurants, parking lots and other publicly accessible private spaces. Alito wrote that “the Second Amendment has the same meaning in all parts of the United States.” - Justice Jackson, dissenting on the gun case: “Today’s decision makes one thing clear: The Court’s objective is protecting guns.” - Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissented in all three immigration matters.

3. Twin earthquakes kill 920 in Venezuela as US deploys warships

A pair of powerful earthquakes — a magnitude 7.2 followed seconds later by a 7.5 — struck the Venezuelan coast on the evening of June 24, toppling buildings in Caracas and surrounding regions. By June 26 the official death toll had reached at least 920, with more than 3,300 injured and tens of thousands feared missing.

The Trump administration announced a “whole-of-government response” including the deployment of two warships, the USS Fort Lauderdale and the littoral combat ship USS Billings, transport planes and helicopters, and $150 million in aid. As Andrea Lobo wrote on the WSWS, the deployment — directed by Marine Corps Major General Kevin J. Jarrard — amounts to “a military occupation wearing the mask of disaster relief,” consolidating imperial control roughly six months after an alleged US intervention in the country.

Key facts: - UN sources cited by the WSWS estimate up to 50,000 potentially missing; 383 buildings destroyed and 6.76 million people affected overall. - Brazil’s Lula dispatched a field hospital and firefighting personnel; China pledged emergency humanitarian aid, a rescue team and medical relief. - A fresh magnitude 4.7 aftershock jolted the region as rescue operations continued.

4. Ukrainian deep-strike campaign cripples Russian logistics, throws Crimea into chaos

Ukraine’s long-range drone and missile campaign has reached an unprecedented tempo, striking Russian energy, military and space-communications infrastructure thousands of kilometers behind the front and bringing daily disruption to occupied Crimea. French analysts tallied 500 separate Ukrainian strikes between May 1 and June 18, with the pace escalating sharply in June.

Key facts: - Deep strikes in one week included the Tyumen refinery in Western Siberia (2,070 km from Ukraine, hit June 21), a Voronezh cruise-missile-components plant (June 22), and the Orenburg gas-processing plant (1,200 km away). - Both the Vladimir and Dubna Space Communications Centres — 200 km east and 100 km north of Moscow respectively — sustained critical antenna damage on June 22. - In Crimea, the Kerch oil terminal and port of Kavkaz were hit simultaneously; four S-400 complexes and two Pantsir systems were disabled; Sevastopol fuel was rationed to 20 litres per vehicle weekly and ferry services cancelled. - Zelensky said the two perimeters around Crimea and Russia’s rear “are the ones the Russians have been ordered to defend at the expense of weakening other directions.” May was Russia’s worst month for territorial gains since late 2023.

5. Gaza death toll passes 73,000 as West Bank annexation accelerates

The cumulative Palestinian death toll from Israel’s war since October 2023 has surpassed 73,000, with a further 1,031 killed and 3,309 wounded in continued Israeli violations of the October 10, 2025 ceasefire. On June 25, Israeli forces shot and killed at least two Palestinians in separate incidents in the West Bank and Gaza, including 32-year-old Mustafa Taha Mustafa al-Khatib in Salfit.

Under cover of the regional war with Iran, Israel is accelerating de facto annexation. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich announced he had “cancelled” the 1997 Hebron Agreement, stripping Palestinian municipal authority over the Old City and the Ibrahimi Mosque.

Key facts: - Settlers torched mosques in West Bank farming villages on June 17, scrawling graffiti reading “Night of the Mosques.” - The UN reports Gaza’s humanitarian mission is funded at only 24% of need; fuel shortages risk suspending more than 520 surgical procedures for lack of disinfectant. - Israel is pushing “creeping annexation” westward in Gaza without any formal announcement.

6. Volkswagen plans 100,000 job cuts and four German plant closures

Volkswagen, Europe’s largest automaker, is reportedly preparing to eliminate roughly 100,000 jobs — about 15% of its workforce and double its previous target — while closing four German plants and spinning off its core VW brand and auto-parts business into separate entities. The plan, attributed to CEO Oliver Blume, would also cut planned investment by about 15% to just over €130 billion over five years.

The targeted sites include plants in Hanover, Zwickau and Emden, plus Audi’s Neckarsulm. As Ludwig Weller and Peter Schwarz noted on the WSWS, IG Metall’s December 2024 acceptance of 35,000 job losses and 20% wage cuts is now being used by management as the precedent for far deeper reductions.

Key facts: - VW group net profit fell 28% to €1.56 billion in Q1 2026, with revenue down 2% to €75.7 billion, amid intensifying competition from Chinese manufacturers. - The company is targeting €6 billion in annual net savings by 2030 and an operating margin of 8–10% despite zero market growth. - VW’s General Works Council and IG Metall pledged to resist the cuts “with all their might” — a posture the WSWS calls hollow given the union’s record of negotiating concessions.

John Bolton pleads guilty to retaining classified national defense information

Former national security adviser John Bolton, 77, pleaded guilty on June 26 in US District Court in Greenbelt, Maryland, to one count of illegally retaining national defense information under 18 U.S.C. § 793(e), which carries a maximum 10-year penalty. According to the court filing, Bolton shared over 1,000 pages of diary-style notes about his NSA duties with family members, and an Iranian-linked hacker subsequently accessed classified material from his personal email account.

The plea agreement caps the recommended sentence at five years and requires Bolton to pay a $2.25 million fine, submit to intelligence debriefings, perform up to 100 hours of community service, and forfeit his federal retirement pay. Sentencing is set for October 28. Bolton — a prominent Trump critic who backed his 2024 opponent — was charged under the second Trump administration, even as Trump’s own classified-documents case was dismissed, underscoring the selective application of national-security law against political enemies within the ruling elite.

US measles cases hit 2,134 in 41 states

As of June 25, the CDC has recorded 2,134 confirmed measles cases across 41 states in 2026, with 93% tied to 30 active outbreaks and roughly 92% involving unvaccinated individuals or those of unknown vaccination status. Three deaths have been confirmed — two unvaccinated children in Texas and one adult in New Mexico — the first US measles deaths since 2015. The elimination status the US has held since 2000 is now under its gravest threat in 25 years, and the WHO will formally reassess in November 2026.

The crisis is inseparable from the collapse of public-health authority and the mainstreaming of anti-vaccine ideology under the current administration’s health leadership.

Roundup of other developments: - H5N1 receptor mapping: University of Pittsburgh researchers, writing in Science Advances, found that H5N1 binds to N-linked sialic acid receptors densely expressed in bovine mammary cells but “virtually absent” in cow airway tissue — explaining the necrotizing mastitis seen in dairy herds since 2024 and why infections went undiagnosed for weeks. The framework can pre-screen other species for susceptibility. - Pancreatic cancer breakthrough: The Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial, published in NEJM and presented at ASCO, showed daraxonrasib (a KRAS-ON inhibitor) extended median overall survival to 13.2 months versus 6.7 on chemotherapy in previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer (HR 0.40, p<0.0001) — a 60% reduction in death risk. Over 90% of pancreatic cancers carry KRAS mutations. - Hepatitis B: Pooled Phase 3 data on bepirovirsen (an antisense oligonucleotide) in NEJM showed a 19% functional cure rate overall and 26% in patients with lower baseline surface antigen, versus 0% on placebo — a milestone toward an elusive cure for a virus affecting 254 million people. - COVID-19: As of June 16, transmission is growing or likely growing in 2 US states, declining in 36, and flat in 11; the WHO reports 7,114,321 confirmed global deaths, with true excess-death estimates of 19–36 million. - AI policy: The White House’s June 2 executive order, “Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security,” establishes a voluntary framework for developers to give the federal government pre-release access to frontier models for security assessment — a pivot from its prior deregulatory orders. Colorado’s state AI Act takes effect June 30.

Arts and culture

The most politically charged cultural story of the day is the Trump administration’s “Great American State Fair” on the National Mall, dissected by David Walsh on the WSWS as a “grotesque” and “sparsely attended” spectacle from which most scheduled performers withdrew, leaving Trump to headline himself alongside military flyovers. Walsh situates the event within a structural assault on the arts: the administration proposed eliminating the National Endowment for the Arts, the National Endowment for the Humanities and the Institute of Museum and Library Services, while the Department of Education was cut by $8 billion below the prior year. His central data point — that the NEA’s 2026 funding of $207 million equals 0.02% of military spending, outpaced by a factor of 4,879 — captures the regime’s cultural priorities. Walsh invokes Eisenhower’s 1953 warning that military expenditure constitutes “a theft from those who hunger and are not fed,” and argues the climate of threat is inducing de facto self-censorship among federally funded arts organizations.

Roundup of other developments: - Wolfgang Tillmans wins the Roswitha Haftmann Prize. The German photographer received Europe’s most financially significant arts award — 150,000 Swiss francs (about $190,000), with no strings attached — for a four-decade career and his record of social advocacy. Tillmans’s work, from intimate portraiture to political campaigning against Brexit and the far right, represents a strand of contemporary art that has tried to keep faith with social engagement rather than retreat into the market. - Death of Clive Davis (94). The record executive and four-time Grammy winner, inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 2000, died June 22. Davis shaped the commercial careers of artists from Janis Joplin, Santana and Bruce Springsteen to Whitney Houston and Aerosmith — a figure whose life spanned the transformation of popular music into a corporate industry, with all the creative possibilities and commercial constrictions that entailed. - Death of James Burrows (85). The television director, who shaped the modern American sitcom across more than 1,000 episodes — from The Mary Tyler Moore Show to Cheers and Will & Grace — died June 19.

Taken together, the day’s cultural news poses sharply the contradiction the WSWS repeatedly identifies: a society of immense artistic and creative capacity, embodied in the careers of figures like Davis and Burrows and in artists like Tillmans, whose public cultural institutions are being deliberately starved and instrumentalized for nationalist spectacle. The “Great American State Fair” is not merely a failed event but a symptom of a ruling class that has nothing to offer culturally except militarism and the cult of the leader.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “WSWS webinar on the American Revolution: The historical significance and contemporary relevance of 1776” by Joseph Kishore — read in full on wsws.org.

The Perspective summarizes a WSWS webinar held on the eve of the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. James Oakes argued that the Declaration’s “assertion of universal human equality establishes an entirely new revolutionary standard by which every social movement from that point on is evaluated,” while Richard Carwardine characterized the Revolution as ending “a world of ascribed status.” Tom Mackaman challenged the 1619 Project’s thesis that the Revolution was a counter-revolution to preserve slavery, noting that “nobody, including its enemies, thought it was anything but a revolution.” David North anchored the contemporary dimension, warning that the anniversary unfolds “amid an escalating assault on democratic rights” and “dictatorial aspirations.” Adam Hochschild observed that the Declaration’s charges against George III “read as if they were written this morning.” North rejected “presentism,” insisting “history cannot be understood through moral denunciation,” and closed on a note of historical optimism — that “the America and the world of 2036 will look vastly different.”

The speech backs Labor’s proposed expansion of ASIO’s compulsory interrogation powers across sabotage, communal violence and border security — provisions critics say criminalize dissent through reversed burdens of proof and mandatory silence. The “Ulm 5”: How Germany is criminalising opposition to the Gaza genocide — Florian Hasek

Five activists aged 20–30 from Ireland, Britain, Spain and Germany face trial in Stuttgart-Stammheim under Section 129 of Germany’s Criminal Code — criminal-organization membership, carrying up to five years — for damaging property at an Elbit Systems office in Ulm on September 8, 2025. Elbit reportedly supplies over 80% of the drones used in the Gaza genocide and benefits from expanded German-Israeli military cooperation. The defendants have endured more than nine months of pre-trial detention with isolation of 20–23 hours daily and one to two monitored family visits per month. The presiding judge reportedly confirmed authoritarian conduct by declaring “Yes, I am!” when compared to a sergeant. Hasek draws a parallel to Britain’s June 2025 designation of Palestine Action as a terrorist organization, which produced more than 3,400 arrests, framing the Ulm trial as a qualitative escalation in the criminalization of anti-war opposition across Europe.

The California billionaire tax: A faction fight within the ruling class — Marc Wells

Wells analyzes California’s proposed one-time 5% wealth tax on roughly 200 billionaires — potentially raising $100 billion for healthcare and education — not as progressive reform but as an intra-ruling-class conflict. Between 2023 and 2025, California billionaires’ collective wealth grew 144% to $2 trillion while they paid only 0.26% annually in state income taxes. Billionaires mobilized $118 million in opposition, with Sergey Brin alone contributing $82 million. Newsom’s opposition reflects “class solidarity” with Silicon Valley; the CTA opposes the measure to protect its Proposition 98 funding stream; and the SEIU-UHW backs the tax while endorsing Xavier Becerra, who oversaw the “Medicaid Unwinding” that stripped coverage from 20 million Americans. Wells condemns Sanders, the DSA and Ro Khanna for embedding in the Democratic Party and predicts the tax will be “tied up in the courts, gutted by counter-legislation, and ultimately abandoned,” like Obama’s Buffett Rule.

The June 24 national transport strike in the Netherlands — Parwini Zora

Zora analyzes the June 24 national transport strike against proposed cuts to unemployment and disability benefits. An FNV member survey showed 98% opposed to the cuts and more than 85% ready for a protracted strike, yet the FNV leadership limited the action to a symbolic four-hour stoppage. Zora frames the action within a Europe-wide strike wave — Belgium, France, Portugal, Italy, Spain and Greece — driven by the same logic, that “every euro for wars abroad is wrung from the working class at home.” She invokes the Dutch transport workers’ 1941 February General Strike against Nazi persecution and the 1946 wildcat against colonial troop transports to Indonesia, and calls for rank-and-file committees independent of the union bureaucracy.

Hyundai autoworkers in South Korea vote to strike — Ben McGrath

Hyundai Motors workers voted 92.03% in favor of strike action, with 94.15% of the Korean Metal Workers’ Union’s 39,668 members participating. Demands include a 149,600-won monthly wage increase, performance bonuses equal to 30% of 2025 net profits, job protections, shorter hours and expanded hiring. McGrath argues the vote reflects genuine rank-and-file anger but that the KMWU historically limits stoppages to brief partial walkouts; union membership has fallen from 48,000 to 39,668 over a decade through job cuts the union enabled. He highlights Hyundai’s planned deployment of humanoid robots by 2030 and draws connections to GM Korea and US autoworkers, calling for international rank-and-file coordination.
