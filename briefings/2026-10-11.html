1. Houthi missile strike on Riyadh airport kills 12 and Trump says the US may join the Saudi war in Yemen

On Saturday, October 10, a missile hit the complex housing Terminals 3 and 4 (domestic flights) at Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport. Saudi officials say at least 12 people were killed and 309 wounded, several of them critically. It was the third attack on the airport in a week. An earlier strike on Thursday killed three Saudis, including Saudia captain Hamoud Ali Alkalthami. Saudi Arabia’s coalition command blames the Houthis. The Houthis have not claimed Saturday’s strike, though they did claim Thursday’s, and they accused Riyadh of bombing Sanaa International Airport the same day. A “projectile” also fell near Dammam’s King Fahd International Airport on Saturday evening.

The attack comes a week into a Saudi-led offensive meant to retake all Houthi-held territory, including Sanaa. The coalition says it hit 136 military targets on October 9 alone. Asked whether Washington would join, Trump said: “We may. We’re gonna look at it… we move very quickly.” He plans to call Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. The Saudi-led coalition promised a “decisive” response. Even so, the Saudi energy ministry was still promoting a major international energy conference due to open in Riyadh on Sunday.

Key facts: - Dead by nationality (General Authority of Civil Aviation): four Saudis, two Bangladeshis, and one each from the US, Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Palestine and Sudan. The victims are mostly the migrant workforce on which the Gulf economy depends. - Aviation impact: Operations were suspended until midnight, and FlightAware counted roughly 290–325 cancellations. Lufthansa, Emirates, Etihad, Air India, FlyDubai and PIA suspended Saudi flights. EASA warned of a heightened risk of civil aircraft being misidentified, and the Houthis warned airlines “for the last time” to stay out of Saudi airspace. - Strategic stakes: Houthi gains along the Red Sea coast give them surveillance of the Bab al-Mandab, the main alternative to Hormuz for Gulf exports. A Saudi diplomat says government forces retook the strait and Mayyun Island; the Houthis deny it. - Reactions: Rubio “strongly condemns” the attacks. UN envoy Hans Grundberg called them a “grave escalation.” Pakistan, which recently activated its mutual defence pact with Riyadh, called the strike “a heinous act of terrorism.” Oman alone urged restraint.

2. US jet disables blockade runner in Gulf of Oman as Iran claims control of Hormuz

On Saturday a US fighter jet hit the stern of the Panama-flagged cargo ship M/V Ocean Molica (also called Arika Sun) with a precision munition after the ship left an Iranian port. CENTCOM says the crew “ignored repeated warnings and attempted to run the ongoing naval blockade,” and that the strike disabled propulsion “without harming the crew.” The war is now in its eighth month (it began in late February). The two sides are in a counter-blockade standoff: Iran demands that ships get permission to use its preferred routes through Hormuz, and the US has blockaded Iranian ports since July 14. The IRGC says it struck the LPG carrier NV Sunshine for using an “illegal route” and that its forces “have taken control of the administration of the strait.” UKMTO separately reported an outbound tanker hit by an unknown projectile in Hormuz, which set it on fire, and on October 8 a ship off Qatar’s north coast was hit by several projectiles, causing casualties.

Diplomacy is going through Moscow and Beijing. The Kremlin says Putin passed Iran’s peace terms to Trump in a call on Friday. President Pezeshkian says Iran has revised its proposals but complained: “Every time we hold talks with the U.S., they attack again.” Trump told a rally in upstate New York that Iran must “give us everything, or they don’t exist any longer.” On Truth Social he said the US “will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the Midterm Elections.” His war policy is now openly tied to the electoral calendar.

Key facts: - CENTCOM blockade tally since July 14: 4 commercial vessels disabled, about 135 turned around, and 10 IRGC-linked tankers destroyed. - Cost of the war: A new congressional report counts at least 81 US military aircraft and drones damaged or destroyed. The USS Abraham Lincoln returned to San Diego after an unprecedented 265 days at sea, with at least eight suicide attempts aboard amid food shortages and contaminated water. The Navy moved its regional supply hub to Diego Garcia after Iran destroyed its Bahrain logistics base in February. - Sen. Chris Murphy asked why “taxpayers [are] paying billions to hit commercial ships” and called the situation “a disaster.” The Pentagon earlier refused him access to Al Udeid Air Base. - Stranded tankers: Sri Lanka says it has no plans to help 19 Iranian tankers stranded off its coast.

3. Trump calls for Zelenskyy’s replacement after striking Russian diesel deal

On Saturday Trump said: “I think it’s time for Ukraine to get a new president.” He blamed Kyiv’s drone campaign against Russian refineries for record US diesel prices and said Zelenskyy wants to “make problems for our farmers, our ranchers.” The day before, during US-hosted talks in Miami with Ukrainian, European, NATO and European Commission officials, Trump announced a deal with Putin under which Russia will supply 300,000 tons of diesel immediately, 500,000 tons in November, 1 million in December and about 3 million after that. Al Jazeera called it a “stunning reversal.” Last month Trump signed a sweeping sanctions law aimed at Moscow’s oil and gas revenues. Envoy Steve Witkoff called the Miami talks “productive,” but no agreement was announced. A Ukrainian delegation is due to brief Zelenskyy on Monday.

Within hours, Russian glide bombs killed 20 people in Zaporizhzhia, including three children. Zelenskyy said: “Russia expressed its thanks for the lifting of sanctions on diesel exports.” It was the third mass-casualty attack in four days. Nearly 80 civilians died on Wednesday and Thursday, including at least 30 from a bomb that hit buses in Kramatorsk and 22 from a missile in Pryluky. Rubio said on Thursday that the war had reached a “stalemate,” and warned that “the other way that stalemates end is through escalation,” including into neighbouring NATO states.

Key facts: - European response: EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said, “This is not the time to ease pressure on Russia and Europe won’t.” UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham promised tougher British sanctions. Republican Rep. Don Bacon called for the sanctions law to be enforced. - Kyiv’s response: Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said Ukrainians alone decide their president. Zelenskyy is arranging F-16 ammunition from Finland to intercept Shahed drones and warns of more missile strikes in the coming days. - UK–Germany: London and Berlin agreed a joint (non-treaty) security framework against “hybrid” Russian aggression such as sabotage and cyberattacks.

Washington’s dealings with Moscow over diesel, and its open demand for a change of government in Kyiv, show that the war is being managed according to the economic interests of US imperialism, now under strain from the Iran war.

4. India detains thousands in New Delhi lockdown over purge of 130 million voters

On Saturday the Modi government put the capital under effective lockdown to stop a rally at Jantar Mantar by the Gen-Z “Cockroach” movement. The movement is demanding the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar and a rollback of the “Special Intensive Revision” of voter rolls, which removed about 130 million names. Critics say the revision makes citizens prove their right to vote and falls hardest on Muslims, the poor and minorities. The Indian Express reported that Kumar overruled two fellow election commissioners who questioned the integrity of the voter database. Opposition parties say the revision shaped several recent state elections that the BJP won.

Police took movement founder Abhijeet Dipke and two other leaders off an Air India flight from Mumbai. A magistrate released them Sunday on condition of “no breach of peace for one year.” Detainees also included YouTuber Dhruv Rathee, CPI(ML) general secretary Dipankar Bhattacharya, AISA president Neha Bora, Supreme Court lawyer Prashant Bhushan and, according to TV channels, former chief ministers Mamata Banerjee, Arvind Kejriwal and Pinarayi Vijayan.

Key facts: - Detentions: About 2,500 in Delhi (The Hindu, citing police), or about 2,000 (Reuters, citing the Home Ministry), plus about 600 arrests in Mumbai (ANI). Five journalists, one of them from Reuters, were briefly held. - Lockdown measures: Mobile internet was cut in the central business district, dozens of metro stations and markets were closed, and anti-riot vehicles with tear-gas launchers guarded key routes. - BJP spokesman Sambit Patra called protesters “anarchic elements.” Congress president Kharge said Modi had made the “Mother of Democracy” a “Muzzled Democracy.” - Prior impact: The movement’s July blockades forced out Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan. State elections begin in early 2027. - International response: The UN Human Rights Office and Amnesty criticized the crackdown, and India’s UN mission called the criticism “interference.”

5. ICE agent shoots Bronx father in the neck in front of his 5-year-old son

At about 4 p.m. Thursday, a federal immigration agent shot Oscar Belgal, 28, from the Dominican Republic, in the neck in Marble Hill on the Manhattan–Bronx line, just as school was letting out. Belgal’s 5-year-old son was in the back seat. NYPD Commissioner Jessica Tisch said an agent fired seven rounds into the SUV. Masked agents pulled Belgal out and handcuffed him while he bled. DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin says Belgal “rammed two different vehicles,” called him a “thug” and an “attempted murderer,” and said body-camera footage exists but will be released “later.” ICE enforcement chief Marcos Charles said Belgal “weaponized” his vehicle. Belgal is now held at the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn with a bullet still in his body, and DHS says it will put him in removal proceedings.

Hundreds protested at the hospital that night, and police arrested four people. Officers knocked congressional candidate Darializa Avila Chevalier to the ground. A larger protest followed Friday at Foley Square. Mayor Zohran Mamdani said he spent two hours phoning Trump and Mullin to secure Belgal’s release and was rebuffed, and he repeated that ICE “must be abolished.” The NYPD’s Strategic Response Group was nonetheless deployed against demonstrators. As the WSWS reported, police were filmed “protecting and escorting ICE agents.”

Key facts: - Arrest quotas: The administration has reportedly ordered daily immigration arrests raised from 2,000 to 3,000 through the midterms (Democracy Now). - Shootings: A New York Times tally counts at least 25 people shot by immigration agents since Trump returned to office, against three ICE shooting incidents in all of FY2024. - Detained citizens: ProPublica counts 506 US citizens detained by immigration agents, including at least 70 children and teens. About a dozen citizens, mostly children, have been deported. - Masked agents elsewhere: In Harrison, New Jersey, masked agents dragged off a man while a woman holding a baby clung to him, despite a state law that mostly bans police from covering their faces.

6. Israel kills eight in Gaza on first anniversary of the “ceasefire”

Israeli attacks killed at least eight people across Gaza on Saturday, the first anniversary of the US-brokered truce. Among them was 8-year-old Shahd Abdul-Razzaq al-Najjar, killed by a quadcopter drone in western Gaza City. A bomb near the Haider Abdel Shafi roundabout killed four. On Friday more than 100 humanitarian and human rights organizations said the ceasefire “exists in name only,” citing daily killings and Israel’s continued blocking of aid, equipment and rebuilding materials.

Key facts: - Gaza Health Ministry: At least 1,481 killed and 5,254 injured since October 10, 2025. Since October 2023 the toll is 74,370 killed and 175,371 injured. - UN (September): 94% of Gaza’s population still needs shelter and basic household aid, and 65% of households live in tents or makeshift shelters. - West Bank: At least 12 settler attacks on Saturday morning alone, including beatings, pepper spray and theft, with Israeli forces helping to detain Palestinians. - Netanyahu’s memorial speech: At the October 7 memorial, bereaved father Yaakov Godo interrupted him: “You didn’t protect my son on October 7th.”

7. Justice Department opens antitrust probe of TV networks that halted Trump coverage

On Saturday DOJ spokesperson Emily Convington confirmed an investigation into whether ABC, CBS, CNN, NBC and Fox News broke federal antitrust law by suspending White House pool coverage in September. “Group boycotts among commercial competitors can violate the Sherman Act,” she said. The five-network pool stopped coverage in solidarity after the White House declared on September 19 that covering the president is a “privilege – not a right” and barred CNN, MS NOW and Politico. On September 24, US District Judge Timothy Kelly ruled the ban likely unconstitutional and ordered access restored.

Key facts: - Disputes continue: Fights over access to specific events and pool assignments have gone on since the credentials were restored. - CPJ’s Jose Zamora warned against using antitrust law to “intimidate outlets defending press access.” - Media consolidation: The probe comes days after the Trump-allied Ellison family’s Skydance took control of both CBS News and CNN (see Arts and culture).

Using antitrust law to punish a collective press protest is a new tool in the administration’s campaign to bring the media under direct state control.

Houthi siege leaves Taiz hospitals near collapse

Yemen’s internationally recognised government warned on Saturday of an “imminent health and humanitarian catastrophe” in Taiz, its third city. Medicines, IV fluids, oxygen and generator fuel are nearly gone, and dialysis, intensive care and neonatal units face shutdown. In September the Houthis captured the Red Sea port of Mocha. On October 4 they took At Turbah, cutting the last main road between Taiz city and government-held territory. Saudi-backed forces launched the counteroffensive that has now become the Saudi–Houthi front of the Iran war.

  • Population cut off: About 2.8 million people in government-held areas of the governorate, plus 200,000 recently displaced.
  • Fuel prices: A 20-litre container of petrol rose from 30,000 riyals ($19) to 90,000 ($58).
  • Severity: Local official Nabil Jamil says the siege is “more comprehensive and its impact more severe than in 2015.”

Israel’s war economy booms as workers face falling real wages

Ahead of the October 27 Knesset election, Al Jazeera reports that Israel is among the fastest-growing advanced economies. The Bank of Israel forecasts 4% growth in 2026 and 5.5% in 2027, driven by AI and defence-tech exports. Foreign direct investment hit a record $26.2 billion in 2025, up 78%. Netanyahu proposes a defence budget of 183 billion shekels ($60 billion), about 9% of GDP and 2.5 times pre-October 7 spending.

  • Cost of war: The Bank of Israel puts war costs at 350 billion shekels ($114.6 billion) as of March, excluding the Iran war.
  • Wages: Hebrew University economist Joseph Zeira says real wages and incomes have fallen and public services have deteriorated.
  • Prices: Food prices rose 8% from early 2024 to mid-2026 (Lobby 99).

Genocide and regional war have been very profitable for Israeli tech and arms capital, while workers bear the cost.

Belgian unions march against De Wever austerity budget

Police counted 30,000 marchers in Brussels on October 9, and unions claimed 50,000. They included rail workers, teachers, students, civil servants and refugee advocates, protesting Prime Minister Bart De Wever’s budget plans. The budget is due Tuesday, October 13, and a VAT rise from 21% to 22% is under discussion. Student protests have continued since October 1 in several French-speaking cities. The union leadership has again confined opposition to one-day demonstrations timed around the parliamentary calendar.

French school revolt continues with another strike day Tuesday

The high school movement against staff shortages, overcrowded classrooms and crumbling buildings is in its fourth week. The Interior Ministry counted more than 250,000 marchers in over 40 cities on October 6, about 56,000 of them in Paris. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu acknowledged that at least 215 teenagers and 85 school staff were hurt in clashes with police, “the heaviest toll in decades” for the school system. Unions have called a further strike day for Tuesday. France’s debt is 119% of GDP, and every party of government is committed to the cuts that produced the conditions the students are protesting.

Ninth journalist killed in Mexico this year

Hamurabi Huesca Manzano, owner of the news site Pinotepa Comunica, was shot dead in Oaxaca on Friday. Like many local reporters, he also drove a taxi to make a living. Washington delists Myanmar junta tycoon’s daughter amid talks with military regime

On Friday the US Treasury removed Theint Win Htet from its sanctions list. She is the daughter of Thein Win Zaw, founder of the Shwe Byain Phyu Group, which the Biden administration sanctioned in January 2024 for supplying fuel to the military. The move follows a Reuters report that the Trump administration has opened talks with Myanmar’s junta, and comes after earlier delistings of junta-linked individuals and a company in July 2025. Washington is lining up with the junta, as it is with other military regimes, as it competes with China for influence and resources in Southeast Asia.

Hurricane Isaias leaves 664,000 without power along Gulf Coast

Isaias, the first hurricane of the Atlantic season, made landfall near Destin, Florida, with sustained winds of 105 mph before weakening to a post-tropical storm over southern Alabama. More than 664,000 customers in Florida and Alabama lost power. The NHC forecast a 3–5 foot storm surge from Dauphin Island to the Aucilla River. Pensacola streets were blocked by debris, flights were grounded at Destin–Fort Walton Beach, and about 5,000 National Guard troops deployed. Some Panhandle counties suspended emergency response on Friday night, and some prisons evacuated inmates. No deaths were reported. Trump called the situation “under control.” DHS Secretary Mullin, whose department includes FEMA, approved emergency declarations for both states.

DHS proposes $70,000 fee for foreign graduates’ work permits

DHS issued a proposed rule on October 7 that would charge F-1 graduates $70,000 for 12 months of Optional Practical Training (OPT), and $30,000 more for the two-year STEM extension. In 2024 there were about 418,000 F-1 students, and more than a quarter of them received OPT authorization. On October 8 the administration also suspended Microsoft, the top PERM filer in FY2025, and several other companies from sponsoring green cards. Together with the proposed $103,265 H-1B fee, these measures make legal immigration available only to those who can pay.

US measles cases pass 4,080 as Pennsylvania records fifth death

As of October 8, the CDC counts 4,080 confirmed measles cases in 47 jurisdictions, plus 18 among international visitors. There have been 42 new outbreaks, and 96% of cases (3,897) are outbreak-associated. The 2025 full-year total was 2,289 cases, so 2026 is already about 78% higher with nearly three months left. Kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.4%, from 95.2% in 2019–20, well below the roughly 95% needed for herd protection.

Pennsylvania’s outbreak, now the largest in the country and the state’s largest in more than three decades, reached 1,136 cases on October 9, up from 890 on September 27. There have been 219 hospitalizations (19.3%). According to the Philadelphia Inquirer, five people have died. The latest was a 20-year-old Amish woman on chemotherapy for leukemia, who could not be vaccinated. The CDC lists only two measles deaths nationally. On September 4 it began requiring death certificates to list measles as the underlying cause, which is rare because measles usually kills through complications. New York Governor Kathy Hochul declared a disaster emergency after 108 cases (as of October 3), the most since 2019. The order lets pharmacists, paramedics and midwives vaccinate.

Anne Carson wins the Nobel Prize in Literature

On October 8 the Swedish Academy awarded the 2026 Nobel Prize in Literature to Canadian poet, classicist and translator Anne Carson for her “bold and inventive oeuvre,” which engages “the classical tradition to devise fresh structures for modern writing.” Carson’s work, including Autobiography of Red, Nox and her translations of Sappho and the Greek tragedians, treats ancient literature as a living resource for depicting grief, desire and the inner life. The award goes to a writer deeply engaged with the inheritance of world culture, at a time when governments are cutting humanities funding and treating classical education as a luxury. It is a serious choice.

Far-right AfD targets the Bauhaus in its centenary year

The AfD won 44% of the vote in Saxony-Anhalt’s September 6 election and is trying to form a government. It wants to cut funding for the centenary of Walter Gropius’s Bauhaus building in Dessau, which the party calls a “misguided path of modernism.” AfD cultural spokesman Hans-Thomas Tillschneider has demanded that theaters and cultural institutions sign a declaration of loyalty to “German culture” to receive state money. The state’s culture budget for institutions is about €192 million a year. A parliamentary motion would change the state slogan from moderndenken (“think modern”) to deutschdenken (“think German”). The Nazis closed the Dessau Bauhaus by cutting its funding in October 1932. Culture Minister Wolfram Weimer’s “Bauhaus Manifesto 2026,” signed by 14 institutions, does not mention the AfD.
