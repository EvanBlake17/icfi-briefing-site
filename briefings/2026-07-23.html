1. US strikes on Iran enter 11th night as war costs surge past $37.5 billion

The US air war against Iran continued for an 11th-plus consecutive night, with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth telling the Senate Appropriations Committee that the conflict has cost $37.5 billion so far. NBC News reported that the Pentagon’s internal estimate runs to $80–100 billion, and the administration is asking Congress for an additional $88 billion — $21 billion in munitions, $17.3 billion in operations, and $12.1 billion in classified programs. The war is metastasizing regionally: Iran-backed Houthi forces claimed strikes on two Saudi oil tankers in the Red Sea, while Iranian drones struck buildings at Al Azraq air base in Jordan and Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain.

As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, the Democratic Party’s posture is not opposition but a demand that Trump prosecute the war more aggressively — “Democrats to Trump on Iran: ‘Win the war!’” The bipartisan character of the war, and the near-total silence surrounding its human and fiscal costs, is the central political fact.

Key facts: - Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi: “Our defense doctrine is clear: eye for an eye… Any aggression against Iran, including our infrastructure, will compel a powerful and decisive response.” - Jordan’s military said it intercepted 8 of 10 Iranian projectiles fired at US bases. - Trump has threatened to strike “Pickaxe Mountain,” a suspected underground Iranian nuclear site. - General John Caine, chairman of the Joint Chiefs, appeared alongside Hegseth to defend the supplemental funding request at what senators called a “fiery” hearing.

2. Israel’s Gaza siege continues on 284th day of ceasefire violations

Israel’s assault on Gaza continued for the 284th consecutive day since the October 2025 “ceasefire,” which the Gaza Government Media Office describes as a continuous campaign of violations. The office has recorded 3,689 violations resulting in 1,122 Palestinians killed and 3,599 injured. On July 21, three Palestinians were killed in an Israeli airstrike on a shelter at Al-Yemen Al-Saeed Hospital in northern Gaza; three more died in a drone strike near the British Hospital in Al-Mawasi.

Israel continues to weaponize aid and movement, allowing only 35% of expected aid trucks and 36% of permitted travelers to cross Gaza’s borders, while expanding the “Yellow Line” buffer zone and building a barrier to consolidate the territory it occupies. The WSWS continues to frame the assault as an ongoing genocide bound up with the wider imperialist war drive across the region.

Key facts: - 1,122 Palestinians killed and 3,599 injured across 3,689 documented violations since October 2025. - Aid deliveries capped at roughly a third of the agreed volume, deepening famine conditions. - Israeli forces are entrenching physical partition of Gaza along the expanding “Yellow Line.”

3. Ukrainian F-16 downs a Russian jet as Zelensky touts “toxic atmosphere” around Putin

General John Caine confirmed that a Ukrainian F-16 shot down a Russian fighter jet — the first known air-to-air kill by an F-16 in Ukrainian service. The claim came as Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, in his July 22 evening address, argued that frontline pressure, long-range strikes and sanctions are creating a “very toxic atmosphere” around Vladimir Putin, and asserted Russian society is beginning to feel the strain.

The war continues to grind through civilian populations on both sides. Russian strikes on Kherson killed one person and injured 20 in the past 24 hours, and an FPV drone hit a Kharkiv gas station. Ukrainian drone strikes hit Wildberries warehouses and logistics hubs in Krasnodar and Stavropol, injuring 15. The EU is preparing a new sanctions package, and Trump has voiced support for the Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026, which would authorize secondary sanctions on buyers of Russian oil, gas and uranium.

Key facts: - First confirmed F-16 air-to-air kill in Ukrainian service, per the Joint Chiefs chairman. - Kherson: 1 killed, 20 injured in 24 hours; 15 injured in Ukrainian strikes on Russian logistics hubs. - The Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026 would target third-country buyers of Russian energy and uranium.

4. Maduro appears in a New York court as Venezuela earthquake toll passes 5,300

A motorcade believed to be carrying Nicolás Maduro arrived July 22 at a federal courthouse in New York for a pretrial hearing on drug-trafficking charges, with trial set for June 1. Maduro, 63, and his wife Cilia Flores, 69, have been jailed in Brooklyn since being captured by US forces on January 3, 2026 — an extraordinary act of extraterritorial regime change now proceeding through the machinery of the US courts.

The prosecution unfolds against the backdrop of catastrophe in Venezuela itself. More than 5,300 people have been killed and 16,700 injured in the late-June twin earthquakes, with USGS modeling suggesting the toll could exceed 10,000; 80% of buildings in La Guaira state collapsed. Acting President Delcy Rodríguez has moved to cultivate closer ties with Washington, which eased oil-sector sanctions, deployed more than 900 personnel and pledged $150 million in aid — a sequence that lays bare the geostrategic logic behind the regime-change operation.

Key facts: - Maduro and Flores held in Brooklyn since their January 3 capture; trial set for June 1. - Quake toll: 5,300+ dead, 16,700+ injured; USGS models suggest it could top 10,000. - US eased oil sanctions, deployed 900+ personnel and $150 million as Rodríguez tilts toward Washington.

5. Ebola deaths surpass 1,000 in DR Congo as UN sanctions militia leaders

The Democratic Republic of the Congo’s Ministry of Health reported 2,473 cases and 999 deaths as of July 22 from the Bundibugyo Ebola strain, while Africa CDC put confirmed deaths at 1,031 since the outbreak was declared on May 15. The strain has no approved vaccine or treatment. Officials warn the outbreak is spreading faster than it can be tracked: roughly 80% of new cases are emerging outside known transmission chains, and 737 patients are currently hospitalized or in isolation.

The public-health emergency compounds an armed conflict that has drawn UN sanctions. On July 14 the Security Council’s 1533 Sanctions Committee added AFC leader Corneille Nangaa, M23 intelligence chief John Imani Nzenze, and four other commanders (ADF, Twirwaneho, FDLR) to its list, imposing an arms embargo, travel ban and asset freeze. The convergence of an uncontrolled hemorrhagic-fever outbreak with war exposes the collapse of public-health infrastructure across a resource-rich but imperialist-plundered region.

Key facts: - Congo Health Ministry: 2,473 cases, 999 deaths; Africa CDC: 1,031 deaths. - ~80% of new cases outside known transmission chains; 737 patients hospitalized/isolated. - Six militia commanders sanctioned by the UN 1533 Committee on July 14.

6. US imposes chemical-weapons sanctions on Sudan amid famine and “genocide” warnings

New US sanctions on Sudan took effect July 20 after the government failed to meet Chemical Weapons Convention demands during a three-month grace period following alleged 2024 chlorine gas attacks. The measures include a ban on Sudanese state airlines in US airspace, restrictions on lending by international financial institutions, and export controls with a presumption of denial for security-related goods. Khartoum rejected the allegations as “baseless” and “politically motivated.”

The sanctions land amid a humanitarian catastrophe that has received scant Western attention. Nearly 400,000 displaced people in North Kordofan’s Sheikan, Bara and Gharb Bara districts face emergency-level food insecurity, and a UN report warned that atrocities in the civil war show “indicators of genocide.” The prioritization of sanctions diplomacy over famine relief underscores the subordination of humanitarian concern to great-power maneuvering in the Horn of Africa.

Key facts: - Sanctions effective July 20 after the CWC grace period lapsed; airline ban and financial restrictions imposed. - ~400,000 displaced in North Kordofan facing emergency-level food insecurity. - UN report cites “indicators of genocide” in the ongoing war.

Science, technology, and public health

  • Measles hits a 35-year high — 2,295 US cases in 2026 already exceed all of 2025, the highest since elimination in 2000; Delaware declares a new outbreak.
  • COVID’s fourth summer surge — the NB.1.8.1 “Nimbus” variant is dominant at ~43% of sequenced cases, growing in 18 states.
  • Antibiotic breakthrough — a vancomycin adjuvant restores the drug’s power against resistant Enterococcus in lab tests.
  • Illinois enacts first US AI-audit law — mandating independent third-party audits of large AI developers as Washington pushes lighter-touch preemption.

The US measles resurgence has become the year’s defining public-health failure. The country has logged 2,295 cases in 2026 (Johns Hopkins tracker), already surpassing all of 2025’s total (2,289) with more than five months to go — the highest annual count since measles was declared eliminated in 2000. The CDC’s July 16 count stood at 2,260 cases across 44 jurisdictions, led by South Carolina (670), Utah (518) and Texas (182), with 146 people (6%) hospitalized and no deaths yet in 2026. The outbreaks are driven almost entirely by unvaccinated individuals. On July 22, Delaware confirmed two additional cases — all unvaccinated adult males in Kent County — bringing its total to four and meeting the CDC’s threshold to declare a new outbreak.

Respiratory-virus surveillance shows a fourth consecutive summer COVID surge. As of July 15, infections were growing in 18 states and declining in only three, with the NB.1.8.1 “Nimbus” variant now dominant at roughly 43% of sequenced cases and wastewater levels reported high or very high in 10 states. On avian influenza, the CDC reported no unusual human flu activity; 71 total US human H5N1 cases have been identified since 2024, with outbreaks affecting 180+ million poultry and 1,000+ dairy farms. Globally, 12 human infections (three fatal, in Bangladesh, Cambodia and India) were reported between August 2025 and June 2026, and Australia’s June 30 confirmation of H5 in wild birds means the virus has now reached every continent, including Antarctica.

Research and technology roundup: - Antibiotic resistance (Nature Communications): Cold Spring Harbor and Scripps researchers paired vancomycin with a small-molecule adjuvant, pghi-4, that blocks a bacterial resistance enzyme, restoring the antibiotic’s ability to kill drug-resistant Enterococcus faecium (VREfm) in lab tests — the first adjuvant developed specifically to rescue vancomycin. - Neuroscience (European Journal of Neuroscience): A single psilocybin dose reduced reward-seeking behavior in animal models via increased activity of specific inhibitory brain cells surrounded by perineuronal nets, building on 2026 findings of measurable EEG entropy increases and brain-structure changes lasting up to a month — adding mechanistic evidence for psilocybin in treating addiction. - AI policy: Illinois’ Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act (SB 315) is now law, the first US statute requiring independent third-party audits of large AI developers, even as federal Executive Order 14409 pushes a “minimally burdensome” national framework — highlighting the widening gap between state safety mandates and federal preemption.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural intervention of the day comes from the WSWS itself, where David Walsh documents “The Fall of the American Orchestra” — an analysis of reports from the musicians’ platform Cadenza Work charting what the article calls the “structural collapse” of the American symphony orchestra. Institutions in Honolulu, Syracuse and San Antonio have folded, and only 4% of US adults attended a classical performance in 2022. The piece marshals a devastating set of figures on the class character of cultural funding: American orchestras receive just 13 cents per revenue dollar from public funding versus 87 cents from private sources, and the National Endowment for the Arts distributed only $4.82 million to orchestras in 2022. By contrast, the Berlin Philharmonic receives roughly $125 per ticket in municipal subsidy.

The article’s most striking comparison is a measure of American social inequality: Elon Musk’s wealth equals 1,088 times the combined annual budget of the twenty biggest US orchestras — enough to fund them until the year 3114. Walsh argues that a parasitic American capitalism, which showers resources on the financial oligarchy while starving cultural institutions, cannot sustain the artistic patrimony built over generations, and that only massive public funding under artist control can reverse the decline. It is a concrete demonstration of the WSWS framework: art examined not as commodity or lifestyle content but as a social good whose fate is bound to the class struggle.

Roundup: - Music: Charli XCX has set a new album, Music, Fashion, Film, for July 24 — a follow-up positioned to extend the commercial and critical momentum of her recent work, and a reminder of how thoroughly the pop industry now fuses music with fashion and film branding. Madonna released CONFESSIONS II on July 17, revisiting the dance idiom of her mid-2000s peak. - Film: The week’s theatrical calendar features a Lebanon-set drama and a mix of indie and documentary releases from distributors including Neon, GKIDS and Bleecker Street — modest counter-programming against studio franchise output, including work engaging with conflict in the Middle East. - Cultural policy: The orchestral crisis parallels wider assaults on public cultural funding under the Trump administration, of a piece with the NEA’s shrinking footprint and the broader defunding of the arts as war spending balloons past $37.5 billion.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “The specter of ‘AI communism’: Chinese open-weight models and the crisis of the American AI bubble” by Evan Blake — read in full on wsws.org. The article argues that China’s release of freely distributable, modifiable “open-weight” AI models — from firms including Moonshot AI and Alibaba — threatens to collapse the speculative American AI bubble by destroying the monopoly-pricing premise that justifies trillion-dollar infrastructure spending. It notes that tech companies have poured “$1.4 trillion into AI against roughly $700 billion in cumulative revenue,” that AI stocks now comprise 45% of S&P 500 market capitalization and supplied three-quarters of recent gains, and that “without AI, the American stock market has barely risen at all.” US officials are weighing trade blacklists and executive orders targeting open-weight models to protect American firms. The piece concludes that socialism and democratic, social ownership of AI represent the genuine alternative to capitalist control of the technology.

Trump cuts end food aid for 1.5 million children — Patrick Martin

An estimated 1.5 million children have lost food-stamp access over the past year, with projections suggesting 4 million people total have been cut from SNAP nationwide, per a study analyzing data from 19 states representing over half the US population. Texas eliminated 597,353 people (317,316 children); Arizona saw a 48% decline including 182,058 children. Only nine states met the new 6% error-rate threshold imposed under the “One Big Beautiful Bill,” which expanded work requirements to recipients aged 55–64 and threatens states failing to reach sub-6% error rates by October 2027 with penalties of up to 15% of total benefits paid. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins claimed the decline reflected improved economic conditions, but the article counters that unemployment held steady while real wages declined and food insecurity remained high in 2025. Pediatricians warned that “chronic stress will have long-term implications for the child’s health as an adult.” Martin situates the cuts within the ruling class’s transfer of social resources into war and oligarchic wealth.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output centered on structural critiques of the tech economy. “The Cloud Cartel Has Created a Global Rentier Economy” frames Amazon, Google and Microsoft cloud monopolies as rent-extraction machines entrenching US hegemony — a genuine insight into monopoly capital that nonetheless stops at redistributive regulation rather than expropriation. “The Socialist Case Against Nationalizing AI” argues AI is chiefly a mechanism of wage suppression, and that state nationalization without workers’ control would not fix it — a class-conscious critique, but one that treats “workers’ control” as a moral qualifier rather than a question of political power and the state. “Workers Need a Legal Right to Full-Time Hours” epitomizes Jacobin’s reformism: it locates the answer to precarity in legislative guarantees inside the existing labor-law framework, not in independent workplace organization or the fight against the union bureaucracy. Overall orientation: sophisticated anti-monopoly analysis channeled safely back into the legislative arena of the Democratic Party.

Left Voice

Left Voice ran André Barbieri’s critique of “Hamas’s Agreement to Transfer Gaza Administration to a Pro-Imperialist Committee,” denouncing the ceding of Gaza governance to an internationally-backed technocratic body as capitulation to imperialist management — a genuinely anti-imperialist line. Angel Arias’s piece on Bolivia denounces the state’s use of a “state of emergency” to persecute social leaders (David Mamani, David Quispe Machaca), correctly refusing to grant the government left cover. Overall orientation: the most consistently anti-imperialist of the US tendencies on Gaza, but tethered to the reformist electoral milieu it critiques.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL’s coverage this week offered the clearest instance of campist politics. “Hands off Mali! On the domestic front, “ICE Terror Can Be Stopped — and Restarted — at Any Moment” correctly insists only working-class mobilization, not electoral change, can end ICE repression — a stronger formulation, though disconnected from any perspective for building an independent party. Overall orientation: campist abroad, militant-sounding but strategically hollow at home.
