1. North Korea unveils new uranium enrichment facility; Kim pledges “exponential” buildup

Kim Jong Un personally inspected a newly disclosed uranium enrichment facility on June 3, with North Korean state media publishing photographs showing dense rows of silver centrifuge tubes consistent with a weapons-grade enrichment hall. It is the third such site Pyongyang has publicly acknowledged, after Yongbyon (2010) and Kangson (2024). Kim announced plans to bolster the country’s nuclear forces “at an exponential rate,” citing “the most ferocious enemies” — an apparent reference to the United States and South Korea.

Analysts noted the disclosure directly highlights the collapse of US-led denuclearization efforts across three decades and multiple administrations, from the Clinton-era Agreed Framework through the failed Hanoi summit of Trump’s first term and the abandonment of engagement under Biden. The timing — amid an active US war on Iran and rising tensions across the Asia-Pacific — frames the announcement as a demonstration of deterrence against the threat of US “regime change” warfare. The lesson Pyongyang has drawn from Iraq, Libya and now Iran is that only a nuclear arsenal forecloses the option of US-led invasion.

Key facts: - Kim stated North Korea has more than doubled its capacity to produce weapons-grade nuclear material over the past five years. - KCNA said the facility uses “more sophisticated technology” than previous sites but disclosed no location or output figures. - The disclosure is only the third North Korea has made public; the regime is believed to operate additional undeclared sites. - The announcement coincides with intensified US–South Korea–Japan trilateral military integration and recurring joint exercises Pyongyang treats as rehearsals for invasion. - The development confirms the dead end of “maximum pressure” sanctions, which have failed to slow — and arguably accelerated — proliferation.

2. US House passes Iran war powers resolution in symbolic rebuke of Trump

The House of Representatives voted 215–208 on June 3 to direct President Trump to end US military hostilities against Iran — the first time such a measure has cleared either chamber since the war began roughly three months ago. Four Republicans crossed party lines: Thomas Massie (KY), Brian Fitzpatrick (PA), Tom Barrett (MI) and Warren Davidson (OH). Trump responded on social media, calling it “a meaningless vote” by “4 bad Republicans and all of the Dumocrats.”

The resolution is largely symbolic. The Republican-controlled Senate has blocked all prior attempts, and Trump has twice vetoed similar measures on Yemen and Iran. As Patrick Martin notes on the WSWS, the vote is “political theater”: the same House voted 329–92 against Rep. Rashida Tlaib’s resolution to withdraw troops from Lebanon — with Democratic leadership voting against it — and passed $9 billion in Ukraine aid with broad Democratic support the same week. Many Democrats who backed the Iran resolution would vote to authorize the war if given the chance, exploiting antiwar sentiment for midterm positioning while maintaining their commitment to military intervention.

Key facts: - The war began around February 28 with joint US-Israeli airstrikes on Iran; it has killed an estimated 3,600+ people in Iran, including at least 1,701 civilians, and damaged 65 schools and 32 medical facilities. - Iran has retaliated with drone and ballistic-missile strikes on Israel, US bases and Arab Gulf states; Tehran launched fresh strikes on Kuwait this week. - Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth declared the US military “more strongly placed” to resume full combat than on day one. - Rep. Ro Khanna: “It’s the beginning of the end of the war.” Rep. Gregory Meeks: “a significant bipartisan rebuke.” - The resolution carries no enforcement mechanism and would face a certain Trump veto even if it passed the Senate.

3. Ukraine strikes St. Petersburg oil terminal as Putin economic forum opens

Ukraine launched more than 350 drones at St. Petersburg and surrounding Russian cities hours before the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum (SPIEF) opened on June 3. President Volodymyr Zelensky confirmed strikes on the St. Petersburg oil terminal — one of the largest oil transshipment complexes in northwestern Russia — and on a Russian Svetlyak-class border patrol vessel in occupied Crimea in the same operational window. Three districts of St. Petersburg were targeted; black smoke was visible from the forum venue during opening sessions. Putin is scheduled to deliver the plenary address today, June 5, under the direct shadow of the strikes; he has vowed to strengthen Russian air defenses.

The WSWS analysis by Johannes Stern argues the strikes are not independent Ukrainian operations but are “politically covered, militarily enabled, technologically supported and strategically coordinated by the NATO powers, particularly Germany.” German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius announced joint development of weapons with 1,500-kilometer ranges, with the Bundeswehr “learning in Ukraine how a high-tech war against a nuclear power is conducted.” Stern argues the war serves imperialist aims — control of Ukrainian resources and the weakening of Russia — and calls for the international unity of Russian and Ukrainian workers against both NATO imperialism and the Putin regime.

Key facts: - SPIEF drew ~20,000 representatives from more than 100 countries; the war is not mentioned anywhere in the official 2026 program. - Ukraine’s drone fleet is now 95% domestically designed, “wreaking havoc” on supply lines hundreds of kilometers inside Russia. - The St. Petersburg oil terminal strike is among the deepest and most economically significant infrastructure attacks of the war. - Putin acknowledged the campaign had “cast a cloud” over his showcase forum and threatened “savage strategic bombing” of Ukrainian cities in response.

4. UN peacekeeper killed in Lebanon as Hezbollah rejects ceasefire

Serbian Senior Sergeant Milovan Jovanovic was killed when mortars struck a UNIFIL position near Marjayoun in southeastern Lebanon on the night of June 4; peacekeepers from El Salvador and Spain were wounded. He is the seventh UNIFIL peacekeeper killed since the Lebanon war resumed in March. A UN source indicated the mortars appeared to originate from Hezbollah positions; Hezbollah denied targeting the base and reaffirmed its “unwavering commitment” to UNIFIL’s role.

The killing came as Lebanese President Joseph Aoun declared a US-brokered ceasefire renewal “the last chance” for a comprehensive truce. Hezbollah formally rejected the proposed deal, demanding Israeli forces withdraw from Lebanese territory as a precondition. The war, which began in March 2026, has killed more than 2,000 civilians and militants; Israel has moved a hospital underground and closed schools along its northern border amid Hezbollah rocket and drone barrages. The presence of UN peacekeepers in a live war zone — increasingly the targets of fire from both sides — raises the question of whose interests the mission serves as the conflict grinds on with no political resolution in sight.

5. AI cited in record 40% of US layoffs as tech giants commit $700 billion

US employers announced 97,006 job cuts in May 2026, up 16% from April and the highest May total since 2020, according to Challenger, Gray & Christmas. Artificial intelligence was cited as the reason for 40% of all announced cuts — the highest monthly share since Challenger began tracking the metric in 2023, and the third consecutive month AI ranked as the top stated reason. The technology sector alone cut 38,242 jobs, its steepest single month since early 2023.

The defining paradox: even as workers are displaced, Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta have committed a combined $700 billion in capital expenditure for 2026, largely directed at AI infrastructure. Productivity gains and the value created by automation flow to shareholders, while displaced workers receive no transition support, retraining, or income guarantee. The mechanism is a textbook illustration of how, under capitalism, a technology with immense potential to reduce socially necessary labor instead becomes an instrument for throwing workers onto the street and intensifying the exploitation of those who remain.

Key facts: - The tech sector’s 2026 running total of job cuts stands at 123,653 — up more than 65% on the same period of 2025. - For 2026 year-to-date, employers have cited AI in 87,714 planned layoffs, already surpassing all AI-related cuts in 2025 (54,836). - The figures land alongside a blowout May payrolls report (see World economy), exposing a labor market splitting between buoyant headline hiring and accelerating white-collar displacement. - The Fed and ECB are holding rates steady partly because wage growth is decelerating even as mass layoffs mount.

6. Gaza ceasefire enters second phase as Trump peace plan stalls

Six months after the October 10, 2025 Gaza ceasefire, Trump’s 20-point peace plan has “largely stalled on all its promises beyond the initial pause in fighting.” More than 700 Palestinians and four Israelis have been killed in Gaza since the ceasefire took effect, despite the formal end of large-scale hostilities. The second phase — covering postwar governance, Hamas disarmament, Israeli troop withdrawal and aid expansion — only recently began, triggered when Hamas returned the last hostage remains (Ran Gvili) in late January 2026.

The UN Security Council held an open debate on June 4 calling for consolidation of the ceasefire and a halt to escalating settler violence in the West Bank. Humanitarian aid remains severely constrained amid access disputes and continuing Israeli restrictions, despite ceasefire terms that were supposed to expand the flow of food and medicine into a territory facing engineered famine.

Key facts: - All 20 living Israeli hostages held by Hamas were released within three days of the original ceasefire. - Phase two governance and disarmament questions remain unresolved, with no agreed mechanism for Israeli withdrawal. - Settler violence in the occupied West Bank has escalated sharply, the focus of the June 4 Security Council session. - The continuing killings underscore that the “ceasefire” has not ended the dispossession and violence directed against the Palestinian population.

Sudan civil war enters fourth year as world’s largest famine deepens

Sudan’s civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) has entered its fourth year, with the UN describing the world’s gravest humanitarian catastrophe. More people are living in famine conditions in Sudan than in the rest of the world combined: 28.9 million — more than half the population — are acutely food insecure, including over 10 million facing severe or extreme hunger. Some 33.7 million people require humanitarian assistance in 2026.

Sudan is now the world’s largest displacement crisis, with 13.6 million uprooted — roughly 9.3 million internally displaced and 4.3 million in neighboring countries. The SAF shot down a long-range RSF drone over White Nile state on June 4 as RSF aerial attacks on civilian towns continue. More than 20 million people require health assistance amid outbreaks of cholera, malaria, dengue and measles, and the health system is described as near collapse. International aid funding stands at only 40% of need; US and Western funding cuts have forced the closure of many emergency food kitchens. The total death toll has exceeded 100,000. The near-total silence of Western governments — which finance wars in Ukraine, Iran and the Middle East while slashing Sudan aid — reflects the strategic and commercial calculus that determines which catastrophes command attention.

Russia loses territory as drone war intensifies

Beyond the St. Petersburg strikes, the broader Russia-Ukraine battlefield saw intense activity over the past 24 hours. Russia launched 293 drones over a 24-hour period, of which 264 were intercepted by Ukrainian air defenses. Russian forces carried out 924 strikes on 52 settlements in the Zaporizhzhia region alone, killing one person and injuring four in Zaporizhzhia city.

Despite the bombardment, Russian forces recorded a net loss of 93 square miles of Ukrainian territory in the four weeks from May 5 to June 3 — roughly double the pace of the preceding period. Ukrainian military sources say they are killing or seriously wounding an average of 35,000 Russian soldiers a month and aim to raise that to 50,000. Russian total combat losses since February 2022 are estimated at approximately 1.37 million personnel, with around 1,300 killed or wounded in the past 24 hours alone. Ukraine struck a Russian gunpowder plant and military storage facilities inside Russia proper overnight June 3–4. The mounting casualty figures on both sides underscore the catastrophic human cost of a war that the NATO powers are determined to prolong.

Portugal hit by nationwide general strike over labor reforms

Tens of thousands of workers joined a nationwide general strike in Portugal demanding wage increases and the reversal of labor reforms that would make dismissals easier. The action — part of a broad wave of European labor unrest documented in the WSWS’s EMEA strike roundup — was accompanied by a separate five-day strike of migrant-processing workers at the Agency for Integration. The Portuguese walkout joins indefinite strikes by 78,000-plus teachers in Valencia, Spain (ongoing since May 11) and multi-week university actions across Britain, reflecting mounting resistance to austerity and the erosion of job security across the continent. The strikes pose sharply the question of whether the trade union federations will channel this militancy into limited protest or whether workers will build the independent organizations needed to break the offensive of capital.

Gold above $4,500 as central banks flee the dollar

Drawing on the European Central Bank’s annual report on the euro’s international role, the WSWS’s Nick Beams reports that gold now comprises 27% of global central bank reserves, up from 20% in 2024, while US Treasury bonds fell from 25% to 22%. Gold prices rose 60% in 2025, with central banks purchasing 850 tonnes. Beams traces the shift to the February 2022 freezing of Russian central bank assets — which, as analyst Nitesh Shah notes, “weaponized” not just the dollar but all G7 currencies — combined with US debt exceeding $39 trillion and roughly $1 trillion in annual interest costs. All three major rating agencies have now downgraded US sovereign debt from top levels. ECB President Christine Lagarde: “Forces of fragmentation are becoming more pronounced.” Beams concludes the data signal “fundamental stress in the international monetary system” and the erosion of dollar dominance, though not necessarily an imminent crisis.

Trump announced June 4 that he will formally nominate Todd Blanche — his former personal defense attorney, who has served as acting attorney general — to head the Justice Department permanently, stating “We are going to make him permanent attorney general.” In two months as acting AG, Blanche indicted former FBI Director James Comey over symbolic social media activity, authorized a sweeping investigation of former officials alleged to have conspired against Trump, and moved to establish a $1.776 billion compensation fund for Trump allies and January 6 defendants. As Jacob Crosse argues on the WSWS, this transforms both agencies into instruments of personal presidential power on the eve of the midterm elections; Democrats have objected to Pulte chiefly because his appointment “complicates” reauthorization of Section 702 surveillance law — not out of opposition to authoritarianism. Crosse frames the appointments as part of the construction of an “explicit dictatorship,” with the Democratic Party prioritizing the protection of police-state surveillance powers over any defense of democratic rights.

State Department finalizes purge of 250 diplomats amid three wars

The State Department finalized the terminations of nearly 250 foreign service officers via a brief, impersonal email, completing a mass purge of the US diplomatic corps. The firings are occurring simultaneously with the active Iran war, the Lebanon conflict, the Gaza ceasefire second phase, and ongoing Russia-Ukraine diplomacy — all situations requiring specialized regional expertise. Specialists in energy security and Gulf region dynamics are among those dismissed, precisely as the US engages in energy-leverage diplomacy over Iran’s oil exports and Strait of Hormuz threats. The moves are part of the broader DOGE-driven federal workforce reduction; the department has lost hundreds of career officials since January 2025. Former diplomats warn of a generational loss of institutional knowledge about the very countries central to current US policy, leaving military force as the dominant instrument of American foreign relations — a structural shift that elevates militarism by hollowing out the capacity for diplomacy.

US measles nears 2,000 cases; elimination status at risk

As of May 28, the US has recorded 1,983 confirmed measles cases across 40 states and DC in 2026, with 30 active outbreaks; 93% of cases are outbreak-associated. The pace is on track to surpass all of 2025’s total (2,288 cases), itself the worst year in three decades. The CDC has issued a summer-travel warning, and an independent CIDRAP analysis concludes the US is “highly likely” to lose its measles elimination status — held since 2000 — when the WHO reviews it in November. The driver is collapsing immunization: MMR coverage has fallen from 95.2% (2019–20) to 92.5% (2024–25), below the threshold for herd immunity, reflecting the institutionalization of anti-vaccine politics under the current administration and the gutting of public-health infrastructure.

AI outperforms physicians on NEJM diagnostic challenge

A study in Scientific Reports (Nature Portfolio) tested GPT-4o, Claude 3.7 and Doubao on 272 complex cases from the NEJM Image Challenge (2009–2025). Claude 3.7 achieved 89.0% diagnostic accuracy, surpassing the physician majority vote by more than 40 percentage points — the clearest demonstration yet that multimodal large language models can exceed collective physician performance on complex visual clinical diagnosis. The finding intensifies debate over AI’s role in clinical decision-making even as it lands amid record AI-driven layoffs, raising the question of whether such capabilities will expand access to care or be deployed to cut medical labor costs.

Arts and culture

The death of Peabo Bryson at 75, on June 2, marks the passing of one of the defining voices of late-20th-century American popular song. The two-time Grammy winner is best remembered for the soaring Disney duets “Beauty and the Beast” (with Céline Dion) and “A Whole New World” (with Regina Belle), both of which won Academy Awards for Best Original Song. Bryson’s career, rooted in 1970s soul and R&B before crossing into the mainstream of the 1990s, embodied the trajectory of a Black American musical tradition shaped — and constrained — by the recording industry’s commercial machinery. His finest work demonstrated a craftsmanship, vocal control and emotional sincerity increasingly squeezed out of a culture industry oriented toward algorithmic disposability and the fragmentation of audiences.

The 79th Cannes Film Festival, which concluded May 23, awarded the Palme d’Or to Romanian director Cristian Mungiu for Fjord — his second after 4 Months, 3 Weeks and 2 Days (2007). Mungiu’s cinema, formed in the Romanian New Wave, is distinguished by its unsentimental attention to ordinary people crushed by bureaucratic and social systems, a body of work attentive to questions of class, moral compromise and the long shadow of Stalinism’s collapse in Eastern Europe. The jury, presided over by South Korea’s Park Chan-wook, reflected a festival increasingly marked by the contradiction between serious world cinema and the commercial and political pressures bearing down on it.

Roundup: - Sport as spectacle: The 2026 World Cup (48 teams, 104 matches, $11 billion projected FIFA revenue) opened under conditions the WSWS’s Cesar Uco documents as repressive — ICE agents deployed at every US stadium, Iran’s delegation denied visas despite qualifying, and the DRC facing a discriminatory 21-day quarantine requirement. Dynamic pricing has produced secondary-market tickets exceeding $2,500 for the opening match and one listing at $2.3 million for the final (FIFA’s official final price: $32,970). US visitor numbers fell 14.1% year-on-year in April. The tournament exemplifies the use of sporting nationalism to obscure class antagonism amid war and repression. - Performing arts and access: Across Broadway and major concert halls, dynamic pricing and soaring ticket costs continue to transform live performance into a luxury good — the same exclusionary mechanism on display in mass spectator sport, narrowing access to cultural life along class lines. - Cultural institutions under pressure: Museums and performing-arts centers continue to feel the squeeze of federal and state defunding under an administration openly hostile to independent cultural production. The Washington performing-arts center renamed for Trump and Kennedy remains emblematic of the politicization and instrumentalization of public cultural institutions.

Perspective published: “A reply to Edward Luce of the Financial Times on youth radicalization” by David North — read in full on wsws.org. (Published under today’s date at /2026/06/05/, this is the strongest candidate for today’s Perspective by authorship and analytical scope; the section page was not independently verifiable.)

David North, ICFI chairman and WSWS editorial board chairman, responds to FT columnist Edward Luce on youth radicalization. North challenges Luce’s characterization of 1960s radicalism as “drug inspired,” insisting it emerged from concrete political causes — opposition to the Vietnam War, the civil rights struggle, and “the fresh and not-forgotten horrors of the two World Wars” — and from serious engagement with Marxist theory, as “the 1917 October Revolution had not disappeared from memory” and Trotsky’s works were rediscovered as “political and literary masterpieces.” He attributes the movement’s decline not to internal exhaustion but to “the reactionary political climate of the Reagan-Thatcher years,” which drove former radicals into academia, identity politics and market liberalism. North concedes Luce correctly identifies a new wave of radicalization but argues it remains trapped within “media-vetted pseudo-leftism” — exemplified by Bernie Sanders and Zohran Mamdani — a deliberate ideological barrier constructed by ruling-class institutions (the FT included) to absorb and neutralize socialist impulses before they reach the working class. He predicts this containment will fail under the pressure of war, inequality, climate catastrophe and the assault on democratic rights, with the “rediscovery of Trotsky’s extraordinary political legacy” serving as the bridge to “Marxism as a mass socialist movement based on the working class.” The article accompanies North’s preface to a new edition of Trotsky’s My Life and a video tribute from Trotsky’s Prinkipo exile residence in Turkey.

Southern California chemical crisis was one warning too many — Marc Wells

A refrigeration valve failure at GKN Aerospace in Garden Grove — involving 7,000 gallons of methyl methacrylate monomer — threatened a catastrophic BLEVE explosion, requiring 9 million gallons of water and the evacuation of 50,000 residents. Wells traces the near-disaster to deregulation: EPA Administrator Zeldin’s 2026 rule eliminating third-party audits and worker protections after chemical releases, and Governor Newsom’s veto of California SB 674, which would have mandated real-time toxic monitoring. The plant operates near 13 schools. The article situates Garden Grove within a pattern — the Torrance Refinery’s hydrofluoric acid storage, San Pedro’s LPG facility on an active fault, and nine California chemical accidents in the first five months of 2026 — and concludes that the systemic failure flows from capitalism’s prioritization of profit over safety, calling for democratic worker control of industrial production.

American Axle and Nexteer: auto-parts strikes spread — Kevin Reed, Jerry White, Shannon Jones

A cluster of WSWS labor reporting documents an expanding auto-parts struggle in Michigan. American Axle (CEO David Dauch compensation: $111 million; $8.4 billion in profits over a decade) is using management and non-union personnel as strikebreakers and “physically harassing and assaulting” 1,000 picketers who earn a top wage of ~$22/hour after a five-year progression — down from $29/hour pre-2008. At Nexteer (1,700 workers, 86% strike authorization), the UAW’s fourth tentative agreement, TA-4, is substantively identical to three previously rejected contracts; production workers still top out at $27/hour in 2030, a 40% real-wage decline from 2005, with no protection against 300–400 anticipated automation cuts. One worker: “They’re trying to sweeten up a pile of poop by adding sugar to it.” At Bridgewater Interiors (Warren, MI; $2 billion revenue), ~1,000 UAW Local 400 workers rejected a deal with $17.50 starting wages and no cost-of-living adjustment, yet the UAW has not authorized a strike; thousands of Dana workers ($7.5 billion revenue) prepare to vote on a four-year deal with contract details withheld. One Dana worker: “No one is able to save money; we are living check to check. Florida executes Andrew Lukehart — Kate Randall

Andrew Richard Lukehart, 53, was executed June 2 — Florida’s 36th execution under DeSantis and 8th of 2026, following 19 in 2025 (more than double any prior Florida annual record, which had been 8 in 1984 and 2014). His 1996 sentencing relied on a now-unconstitutional 9-to-3 jury recommendation. Randall emphasizes his documented history of paternal and sexual abuse and his failing kidneys at the time of death, framing accelerated executions as a form of class intimidation targeting those shaped by poverty and systemic neglect — aligned with the Trump administration’s directive to pursue capital punishment “for all crimes of a severity demanding its use.” His final statement: “I’m sorry. Father, forgive them, for they know not what you do.”

Pseudo-left press review

  • Jacobin / DSA — channels Mamdani’s New York victory into Democratic Party electoralism.
  • Left Voice — promotes a “united front” that, the WSWS argues, props up the union bureaucracy and Democrats.
  • PSL / Liberation News — celebrates Mamdani while building “Liberation Centers.”
  • Socialist Worker (SWP) — frames the period through anti-fascist “unity of the left.”
  • RCP / The Communist (IMT) — recruitment-driven catastrophism around its “Revolutionary Communist International.”

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output continues to orbit the electoral victory of Zohran Mamdani in the New York mayoral race, treating it as proof of concept for a “democratic socialist” realignment of the Democratic Party. Its British coverage — “Britain’s ‘Sectarian Politics’ Narrative Is a Dangerous Con” and “The British Right Is Weaponizing Henry Nowak’s Killing” — correctly identifies reactionary maneuvers against Gaza opposition and the far right’s exploitation of a stabbing case, but routes the conclusion toward defending the existing left-Labour and Democratic milieu rather than a break with capitalist parties. A separate retrospective, “The Rise and Fall of Chris Smalls,” reduces the defeat of the Amazon Labor Union to one organizer’s “ego,” obscuring the structural role of the union apparatus and the limits of the syndicalist perspective Jacobin itself promoted. The day’s orientation: locate every radicalizing impulse inside the Democratic Party and the official trade unions — precisely the “media-vetted pseudo-leftism” North’s Perspective identifies as a containment mechanism.

Left Voice

Left Voice’s recent material elaborates its May Day call to “build a broad united front of unions, social movements, community and grassroots organizations, and the Left.” As the WSWS detailed this week, the formulation in practice subordinates workers to the union bureaucracy and Democratic-aligned forces, dressing a popular-frontist orientation in Trotskyist language. Left Voice’s platforming of DSA figures — including one who notoriously celebrated Trotsky’s assassination with an “ice-pick” tweet — exposes the gulf between its self-presentation and its actual political bloc. Despite its Morenoite roots, the tendency’s “united front” is directed not at independent working-class mobilization but at unity with the very apparatuses suppressing the auto-parts strikes documented above. Its overall orientation: a left cover for the realignment project Jacobin and the DSA are pursuing.

PSL / Liberation News

The Party for Socialism and Liberation issued a statement hailing “Mamdani’s victory and the movement for socialism,” converging with Jacobin and the DSA in presenting a Democratic primary win as a socialist advance. The PSL is simultaneously expanding its physical “Liberation Centers,” with June openings announced in Houston and Washington, DC. Liberation News pairs militant anti-imperialist rhetoric with an electoral practice that channels opposition back toward the Democratic Party orbit, alongside a top-down, substitutionist organizational model. The persistent gap between the PSL’s revolutionary phraseology and its accommodation to bourgeois electoralism defines the day’s output.

Socialist Worker (SWP, UK)

Socialist Worker’s coverage centers on countering racist street mobilizations, Labour’s rightward course, and Israeli advances into Lebanon, with branch meetings themed “Trump, Imperialism and the collapse of the world order” and “Anti-zionism is NOT anti-Semitism.” The SWP correctly opposes both racism and the Lebanon war, but its strategic conclusion — “unity of the left” and pressure on Labour and the union leaderships — remains within the framework of reformist protest politics rather than the construction of an independent revolutionary party. This reproduces the dependence on the trade union and Labour bureaucracy that has demobilized past struggles, substituting movementism for a working-class political program.

RCP / The Communist (IMT)

The Revolutionary Communist Party (British section of the IMT’s “Revolutionary Communist International,” proclaimed in 2024–26) continues its recruitment drive — “Seven weeks until the RCP’s National Congress — Forward to 1,400 members!” — wrapped in catastrophist forecasts of imminent collapse and revolution. The IMT’s perspective combines maximalist rhetoric with an organizational practice geared to membership metrics and a federated international lacking the programmatic foundations of the Fourth International. Its proclamation of a new “Communist International” outside the historical continuity of Trotskyism is, in the ICFI’s assessment, an unprincipled appropriation of revolutionary authority that obscures the IMT’s long record of opportunist adaptation.
