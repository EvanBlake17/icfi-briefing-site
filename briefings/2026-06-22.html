1. US-Iran negotiations resume in Switzerland amid fragile ceasefire

Vice President JD Vance, speaking at Emmen Military Air Base in Switzerland on June 22, declared a “good foundation” for a final Iran agreement following the first round of quadrilateral talks between the United States, Iran, Pakistan and Qatar. Iran agreed to allow IAEA nuclear inspectors back into the country, with Vance calling it “a major milestone” and saying coordination could begin “this week, maybe as soon as today.” The talks are the first high-level meetings under the 60-day memorandum of understanding signed June 17 that halted the 109-day US-Israel war on Iran (February 28–June 17).

The diplomatic optimism conceals an unstable situation. As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, the ceasefire is deteriorating: Iran briefly closed the Strait of Hormuz citing US violations and Israel’s continued operations in Lebanon, and the entire US political establishment is attacking the agreement as too soft. The bipartisan demand was for Iran’s complete capitulation, not a negotiated settlement.

Key facts: - Mediators Qatar and Pakistan reported “encouraging progress” and established a High Level Committee for “political oversight on mediation”; a deconfliction channel for the Lebanon ceasefire was also created. - Trump threatened on Truth Social “to hit Iran very hard again” — and that Iran “won’t have a country” if the strait stayed closed — even while Vance was meeting Iranian negotiators. - Israel separately killed 83 people in Lebanon on Friday; Hezbollah fired 50+ rockets in response. Israel’s PM says forces will remain in Lebanon despite the MOU. - Senators Cory Booker (“abject surrender”) and Susan Rice (“jaw-dropping, horrific surrender”) and former defense secretary Mark Esper (“strategic setback”) all denounced the deal. - Crude oil rose above $78 on the closure announcement, with analysts warning of a possible return toward the $118 wartime peak.

2. Russia launches 656 drones and 73 missiles at Ukraine; 22 killed

Russia launched 656 drones and 73 missiles in a single overnight barrage targeting Kyiv, Dnipro, Poltava, Kharkiv and Zaporizhzhia — one of the largest drone-missile salvos of the war. Ukraine’s air defense destroyed 602 drones and 40 missiles, but at least 22 people were killed and dozens wounded. The attack came after Zelensky warned Moscow was preparing a major offensive.

In retaliation, Ukraine struck the Petersburg Oil Terminal — roughly 1,100 km from Ukraine’s border — and military targets at Russia’s Kronstadt naval base. “Purely military targets at the Kronstadt base were also hit,” Zelensky confirmed. NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte made an unannounced trip to Kyiv, claiming Russia is “growing desperate” amid military and economic difficulties.

Key facts: - The barrage is among the heaviest single overnight attacks recorded in the war. - Ukraine’s deep strikes signal an escalating campaign against Russian energy and naval infrastructure far from the front. - The exchange comes as the WSWS marks the 85th anniversary of Operation Barbarossa, warning NATO is “arming the most reactionary forces in Eastern Europe against Russia.”

3. Colombia’s presidential election swings hard right

Right-wing candidate Abelardo de la Espriella won Colombia’s presidential election in preliminary results, marking a sharp political turn away from incumbent leftist President Gustavo Petro. The result is expected to reverse Petro’s “Total Peace” negotiations with guerrilla groups, harden security policy, and re-align Colombia with US foreign policy priorities — particularly toward the ELN and FARC dissidents.

Key facts: - The outcome redraws Colombia’s path on security, economy and peace processes. - Colombia has been under strain from US military operations in its offshore waters (Operation Southern Spear) and economic disruption from the global energy crisis. - The vote forms part of a regional rightward shift, echoing the contested Fujimori claim in Peru and Kast’s presidency in Chile.

4. France gripped by record June heatwave; 3 dead, 49 departments on red alert

France is in the grip of a record-breaking June heatwave, with 42.2°C recorded in Pissos (Landes) and Châteaumeillant (Cher). Paris’s overnight low of 24.2°C broke its June record set in 2017. Forty-nine departments were placed under maximum red alert and 40 more under orange alert — putting nearly 35 million people, over 90% of France’s population, under heat warnings. Météo-France called the episode of “exceptional intensity,” comparing it to the August 2003 heatwave that killed roughly 15,000 in France.

Key facts: - Three confirmed heat-related deaths in Gironde (residents aged 80–95); 13 drowning deaths over the weekend; more fatalities expected. - More than 180 monthly temperature records broken or equalled on Sunday alone. - 845 schools closed or minimally staffed; 71 SNCF Intercités routes cancelled; roads melted in Meurthe-et-Moselle; 600 households lost power in Indre-et-Loire. - The dead so far are all elderly — a marker of the class character of climate vulnerability, as the isolated elderly poor bore the brunt in 2003.

5. Al Jazeera cameraman Ahmed Wishah killed by Israeli strike in Gaza

Al Jazeera cameraman Ahmed Wishah was killed in an Israeli airstrike on a house in the Bureij refugee camp in central Gaza on June 20. Al Jazeera condemned the killing as “deliberate.” He is the 12th Al Jazeera media worker killed in Gaza since Israel’s offensive began in October 2023. His brother, Mohammed Wishah, was killed by Israeli shelling on April 8, 2026.

Key facts: - The IDF claimed without evidence that Wishah was “a Hamas terrorist who moonlighted as a cameraman”; Al Jazeera called the accusation “baseless.” - The Committee to Protect Journalists has documented a pattern of Israel “accusing journalists of being terrorists without producing credible evidence.” - At least 6 additional people were killed in Israeli strikes on Gaza on June 20, including 2 children.

6. Operation Southern Spear: at least 213 killed in US strikes on alleged drug boats

As of June 18, at least 213 people have been killed (including 9 missing, presumed dead) in at least 63 US strikes on 64 vessels in the eastern Pacific and Caribbean since September 2025, under the Trump administration’s “Operation Southern Spear.” Trump invokes a state of “armed conflict” against “narcoterrorists,” but the administration has produced no public evidence for any individual strike.

Key facts: - The first strike, in September 2025, killed all 11 aboard a Venezuelan vessel in the Caribbean. - On June 17 a strike killed 1 and left 2 survivors; on June 21 a strike killed 2 and left 6 survivors in the eastern Pacific. - Alleged targets include members of Venezuela’s Tren de Aragua and Colombia’s ELN. - Human rights groups and the governments of Colombia and Venezuela call the strikes “extrajudicial murder” under US and international law.

Chile’s Kast launches sweeping anti-immigrant “Border Shield Plan”

As Mauricio Saavedra noted on the WSWS, the center-left laid the groundwork: Michelle Bachelet’s 2021 Law 21.325 made regularization virtually impossible, and public support for a complete immigration ban now stands at 55%, up 13 points since October 2023.

World Bank cuts global growth to weakest since COVID

The World Bank cut its 2026 global growth forecast to 2.5% (down from 2.9% in 2025) — the lowest since the COVID-19 pandemic — and warned growth could fall to 1.3% under a worst-case energy-disruption scenario. Forecasts were downgraded for two-thirds of all economies. Developing economies’ growth drops to 3.6%, a post-pandemic low. Gulf economies are expected to grow at near zero in 2026 (down from 3.9%), though a reconstruction-driven rebound to ~5% is projected for 2027–28. The Bank cited higher energy prices, steeper inflation and increased borrowing costs tied to the 2026 Iran war — a conflict that cost the US DoD some $40 billion and pushed American gasoline above $4/gallon, costing households $253+ more than without the war. The Bank announced an emergency deployment of $50–60 billion through pre-arranged instruments.

US measles cases hit 2,104, nearing full-year 2025 total

As of June 18, the CDC reports 2,104 confirmed measles cases across 30 outbreaks, with 93% (1,957) outbreak-associated. The full-year 2025 total was 2,288 cases — meaning the US is on pace to surpass last year’s historically high count with six months still remaining, driven by low vaccination rates in certain communities and continued global importations.

NEJM details rare H5N5 human case with severe outcome

The New England Journal of Medicine published the first well-documented H5N5 human case report: a 75-year-old immunocompromised woman with Waldenström macroglobulinemia developed ARDS after bird exposure. Critically, her initial upper-respiratory nasal swab tested negative for influenza A — the virus was only detected in lower-respiratory specimens. The finding warns that standard upper-airway testing may miss avian flu in pneumonia patients, with implications for clinical surveillance.

Arts and culture

The art world is absorbing a heavy stretch of losses. The death of British painter David Hockney at 88, on June 11, removes one of the most vital figures to emerge from mid-20th-century pop art. Hockney’s California swimming-pool paintings, double portraits, photo-collages and opera designs spanned six decades; his restless experimentation — from Polaroid joiners to iPad drawing — reflected an artist who refused to treat any medium as beneath serious art. Whatever the commercial mythology built around him, Hockney’s best work testifies to a sustained effort to render perception, light and intimacy with fresh eyes, against the grain of art-market cynicism.

The losses extend across disciplines. Photographer Duane Michals died June 9 at 94; avant-garde guitarist James Blood Ulmer, who fused jazz, funk and blues in collaborations with Ornette Coleman, died June 3 at 86. Also among the month’s dead: documentary photographer David Plowden, chronicler of American industrial decline, and Russian dissident artist Robert Kuzovkov, shot dead in Poland — a killing that, if politically motivated, would underscore the dangers facing Russian oppositionists in exile and merits scrutiny rather than the silence it has received.

  • Oliver Tree, the 32-year-old singer-songwriter, died in a helicopter crash near Rio de Janeiro during his world tour. His viral alt-pop persona was built heavily on internet irony, but his death cuts short an artist still searching for a voice beneath the performance.
  • Anthony Head, 72, the actor known to a generation for Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Ted Lasso, died June 5. Gene Shalit, the Today show film critic whose puns and mustache made him a fixture of mass-audience criticism, died June 12 at 100 — a reminder of an era when film criticism reached tens of millions on broadcast television.
  • The Tribeca Film Festival marked its 25th anniversary (June 3–14), opening with Questlove’s documentary on Earth, Wind & Fire founder Maurice White — part of a wave of music documentaries mining the social history of popular music, even as the festival circuit remains squeezed by streaming economics and corporate consolidation.

These deaths bracket a cultural moment in which genuine artistic achievement — Hockney’s, Ulmer’s, Michals’s — is increasingly memorialized by institutions that simultaneously defund living artists and subordinate culture to the market. The WSWS framework insists these figures be understood not as “content,” but as products of definite social conditions whose finest work resisted the commodification surrounding it.

Ukrainian-Polish diplomatic crisis over Nazi collaboration — Alex Lantier

The central event is a diplomatic rupture between Ukraine and Poland triggered by Zelensky’s late-May decree honoring the Ukrainian Insurgent Army (UPA) as “Heroes of the UPA.” The UPA, military wing of the OUN, collaborated with the Nazi invasion and participated in the 1941 Babi Yar massacre and the Volhynia genocide — the murder of “at least 100,000 Polish citizens.” Polish President Karol Nawrocki revoked Zelensky’s Order of the White Eagle on June 19, declaring: “facts are not subject to negotiation.”

Lantier documents the Ukrainian ruling class closing ranks: intelligence chief Kyrylo Budanov denounced “the flywheel of hatred,” Foreign Minister Sybiha insisted UPA honors had “absolutely no anti-Polish intent,” and former presidents Kuchma, Yushchenko and Poroshenko pledged to return their Polish honors. He situates this within systematic fascist rehabilitation — streets renamed for Bandera, the Azov Battalion (insignia borrowed from the Waffen SS) “fully integrated into the armed forces,” and Zelensky’s reburial of OUN leader Andriy Melnyk. Crucially, the piece ties this to the imprisonment of Ukrainian socialist and WSWS supporter Bogdan Syrotiuk, held since April 2024 facing 15 years to life for anti-war articles published on the WSWS, whose health is deteriorating in pre-trial detention. The demand for his immediate release recurs throughout. The article concludes that workers in Ukraine, Poland and Russia share no interest with their ruling classes or NATO.

Sources WSWS Ukrainian-Polish diplomatic crisis exposes NATO war with Russia

85 years since the Nazi war of annihilation against the Soviet Union — Peter Schwarz

On the 85th anniversary of June 22, 1941, Schwarz characterizes Operation Barbarossa as a systematically planned war of extermination. Citing historian Stephen Fritz, he notes Hitler viewed this as “the ‘right’ war” combining Lebensraum conquest with the “Final Solution.” Key data: 3 million German soldiers invaded with extermination orders in hand; Einsatzgruppen killed over 500,000 Soviet Jews within six months; total Soviet dead reached 27–37 million, mostly civilians; 470,000 died in Leningrad’s 28-month blockade alone. He emphasizes Wehrmacht complicity through documented orders (the May 1941 Military Judiciary Authorization, the June 1941 Commissar Order). The decisive argument: fascism arose from capitalism’s contradictions — German industrial elites backed Hitler because they needed a totalitarian state for war mobilization. The contemporary warning is direct: Germany’s defense budget has risen from €32 billion to €53 billion as historical revisionism accelerates across Europe.
