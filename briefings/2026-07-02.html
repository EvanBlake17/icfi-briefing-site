1. Trump disclosure shows income tripling to $2.2 billion

Donald Trump’s annual financial disclosure, filed with the Office of Government Ethics in a 927-page document, shows his reported income roughly tripling in a single year — from $622 million in 2024 to about $2.2 billion in 2025, the first year of his second term. The figures were reported across the financial press, with Bloomberg, NBC News, ABC News and CNBC all confirming that cryptocurrency was the dominant driver.

The WSWS Perspective by Barry Grey frames the disclosure not as an ethics story but as evidence that the presidency has been converted into a “wealth-extraction machine.” Grey renders the sum in class terms: $2.2 billion is “equivalent to the incomes of 37,931 US autoworkers,” or roughly $251,000 per hour.

Key facts: - About $1.4 billion of the total came from crypto, including roughly $636 million from a licensing deal tied to the $TRUMP memecoin and about $580 million connected to World Liberty Financial, which issues the WLFI token and USD1 stablecoin. - The wider Trump family’s collective wealth has roughly doubled to about $10 billion since the November 2024 election; Eric Trump’s net worth rose tenfold to $400 million. - Real estate added gains — Mar-a-Lago revenue rose to $77 million, Trump National Doral to $122 million — alongside more than $14 million in Saudi and Qatari licensing deals. - Trump refused to sign the standard ethics pledge, rescinded Biden-era ethics rules, and left the Office of Government Ethics leaderless after firing its director. - Asked about the figures, Trump claimed outside managers “run my money.”

Grey notes labor’s share of national income fell to a record-low 53.8 percent in the third quarter of 2025, down from 70 percent in 1947, and concludes that “this entire class must be expropriated.”

2. US declines to renew USMCA

The Trump administration allowed the July 1 renewal deadline for the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) to pass without renewing the pact in its current form, instead triggering a yearly review process. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said he could not recommend keeping the roughly $2 trillion agreement without major changes. The deal, which Trump signed in his first term, stays in force and runs until 2036 unless a member withdraws.

Key facts: - Trump’s stated “primary” objection centers on US trade deficits with both partners. - Washington and Mexico have already begun bilateral negotiations set to continue past the deadline; the US and Canada have not yet started their own talks. - The move injects uncertainty into deeply integrated North American supply chains — particularly autos, agriculture, and energy — affecting millions of workers across the three countries.

Sources CNBC U.S. won’t renew USMCA, will review trade pact with Canada and Mexico · Washington Post Fate of $2 trillion North American trade deal in doubt · CBC U.S. declines to extend CUSMA trade deal with Canada, Mexico

3. Russian barrage kills at least 10 in Kyiv

Waves of Russian missiles and strike drones pummeled Kyiv and other Ukrainian cities overnight on July 1-2, killing at least 10 people and injuring 56 in the capital. A separate strike on the Kharkiv region killed a teenager and injured 32, with President Volodymyr Zelensky warning of further mass strikes.

Key facts: - Russian forces made a net gain of 31 square miles of Ukrainian territory in the four weeks to June 30 — a roughly tenfold increase over the 3 square miles gained in the prior four-week period, per DeepState data. - The intensified air and ground campaign comes as Washington’s attention shifts toward the Iran negotiations, and as European governments debate deeper rearmament commitments.

Sources Russia Matters The Russia-Ukraine War Report Card, July 1, 2026 · Kyiv Independent Russian strikes on Kyiv and Kharkiv

4. US-Iran talks in Doha end without breakthrough

US and Iranian negotiators concluded two days of indirect talks in Qatar on Wednesday, the first tentative diplomatic steps following tit-for-tat strikes over the prior weekend. Qatar said the meetings, mediated with Pakistani officials under the “Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding,” made “positive progress,” but major questions remain unresolved. Vice President JD Vance said the talks were “going well.”

Key facts: - The chief sticking point remains the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran insists it can charge transit tolls and the US and Gulf states refuse. - Under a memorandum signed last month, diplomats have 60 days to reach a final deal on Iran’s nuclear program, sanctions, and the strait. - Envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Qatar’s emir; the next round awaits after funeral ceremonies (July 4-9) for former supreme leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, killed in a February 28 airstrike. - At least 35 commercial vessels transited the strait in the past 24 hours, with traffic holding steady.

Sources CNN Meetings in Doha, Vance says talks ‘going well’ · Washington Times U.S., Iran conclude two days of indirect talks with major questions left unresolved

5. DSA-backed Kiros unseats DeGette in Denver

Melat Kiros, a 29-year-old democratic socialist and first-time candidate endorsed by the Democratic Socialists of America and Justice Democrats, defeated 15-term incumbent Diana DeGette in the Democratic primary for Colorado’s 1st Congressional District on June 30. Kiros campaigned against Israeli policy in Gaza and corporate influence, having already handed DeGette a first-ever defeat at the March party assembly with 67 percent of delegates.

As Patrick Martin noted on the WSWS, the result reflects genuine leftward radicalization, but the Democratic Party simultaneously ran military-intelligence figures in other Colorado races, underscoring that the party “remains indissolubly linked to Wall Street and the military-intelligence apparatus.”

Key facts: - Four Colorado state legislators were also ousted in the primary; Senator Michael Bennet’s gubernatorial bid ended. - The victory is the largest to date for the DSA-aligned current in Colorado, part of a run of leftist wins in major cities.

Sources NPR In Colorado primaries, a democratic socialist wins; Bennet’s governor bid ends · Colorado Newsline Newcomer Melat Kiros unseats longtime Rep. Diana DeGette · WSWS Colorado primary results show continuing radicalization of US workers and youth

6. Israel kills three in Gaza as ceasefire violations mount

At least three Palestinians were killed in an Israeli drone strike near al-Hilu station in Gaza City on Wednesday, a site housing a private hospital and fuel station. Israel said it killed a Hamas platoon commander, Adel Jihad Mohammad Asfour. The strike is the latest in a pattern of deadly attacks continuing under the October “ceasefire.”

Key facts: - At least 1,053 Palestinians have been killed and 3,406 wounded since the truce took effect, according to Gaza’s Government Media Office, which has documented 3,465 Israeli violations. - The Israeli military has continued expanding its zone of control, issuing forced-displacement orders and barring Palestinians from territory beyond the “Yellow Line,” placing roughly 53 percent of the enclave off-limits to residents.

International developments

  • Australian Senate committee endorses A$38 billion NDIS cuts — inquiry gave 4,000 mostly critical submissions just weeks; the bill would strip up to 300,000 people from the scheme
  • Israeli coalition advances law shielding ultra-Orthodox draft evaders — a new Basic Law would insulate Haredi men from conscription sanctions amid a manpower crisis

Australian Senate committee endorses NDIS cuts

An Australian Senate committee, chaired by Labor senators, tabled an interim report on June 23 recommending that the government’s National Disability Insurance Scheme Amendment Bill proceed. Labor’s May budget detailed a A$38 billion cut to the NDIS that would remove or block some 300,000 people from the scheme, reduce all participants’ Social and Community Participation funding by 50 percent, and cut Capacity Building funding — which pays for occupational therapy and physiotherapy — by a further 10 percent. The inquiry received more than 4,000 mostly critical submissions. The WSWS’s Max Boddy characterizes the Greens-negotiated eight-week delay (pushing the final report to August 14) as cosmetic, since both major parties back the austerity, despite testimony that disabled people “will die.”

Sources Advocacy for Inclusion NDIS Amendment (Securing the NDIS for Future Generations) Bill 2026 · WSWS Senate committee whitewashes Labor’s assault on NDIS disability support

Israeli coalition advances Haredi draft law

Netanyahu’s governing coalition advanced a Basic Law aimed at shielding ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) men who evade military conscription from sanctions, a longstanding demand of the coalition’s religious parties. The move comes amid an acute manpower shortage in the Israeli military after nearly two years of war and mounting reservist fatigue, and has deepened tensions within the coalition and with secular Israelis who bear a disproportionate share of service.

Sources Times of Israel Coalition advances Basic Law aimed at shielding Haredi draft evaders

Japanese yen sinks to 40-year low

The Japanese yen fell to about 162.8 per dollar, its weakest level since 1986, driven by the interest-rate gap between Japan and the US. As Nick Beams noted on the WSWS, the Bank of Japan raised its policy rate to 1 percent in June — a 31-year high — but this has been insufficient against US rates of 3.5-3.75 percent, and the Ministry of Finance spent $72 billion in April-May defending the currency to little effect. Beams argues the Takaichi government is trapped between raising rates to support the yen and the strain on debt at roughly 200 percent of GDP. Tokyo’s economic blueprint, released June 30, urges the BOJ to align policy with a growth plan calling for over 370 trillion yen in investment by FY2040. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama said the government stands ready to act against “excessive” moves.

Data roundup: - US indices (Wed July 1 close): Dow 52,305 (-0.03%, after a fresh intraday record); S&P 500 7,483 (-0.22%); Nasdaq 26,040 (-0.66%), dragged by a chip selloff after semiconductors surged 80%+ in H1. - Asia (July 2): Nikkei -2% (Kioxia -11.4%, Tokyo Electron -6.2%, Advantest -6.7%); KOSPI roughly -5% on AI-rally worries. - Europe (July 1): FTSE 100 -0.5%; DAX +0.18% at 25,040. - Commodities: WTI $67.95, Brent $67.74 (-1.23%) near a four-month low as Iranian exports surge past 40 million barrels and Russian shipments hit records; gold $4,068 (+0.92%), up ~22% y/y. - Crypto: Bitcoin $59,314 (-1.36%), worst month since June 2022; Ethereum $1,565 (-0.51%). - Data: ISM Manufacturing PMI 53.3 (down from 54.0); ADP private payrolls +98,000, wages +4.4% y/y. Markets await the official jobs report and comments from new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh. - Corporate: Nike beat estimates on a tariff refund though Greater China sales fell 12%.

United States

  • California Democrats strip elected schools superintendent of authority — AB 181 hands operational control to a Newsom appointee in January 2027 amid a budget crisis
  • Philadelphia hotel union calls off strike before July 4 — UNITE HERE settled at $30/hour by 2028, far below a cited $44.58 local living wage

California Democrats strip elected superintendent of authority

California’s Assembly Bill 181 — embedded in the fiscal budget passed before the July 1 start of the new year — strips the independently elected State Superintendent of Public Instruction of operational authority, transferring control to a governor-appointed Director of Education confirmed by the state Senate beginning January 2027, amid a severe state education budget deficit. The WSWS’s Marc Wells frames the measure as Governor Gavin Newsom centralizing control to push through austerity while unions suppress teacher resistance; EdSource and abc10 report the reform ends a decades-old split of authority between the governor and the elected superintendent.

Sources EdSource State superintendent will no longer manage California schools under deal Newsom cuts with Legislature · ABC10 New law will strip California Superintendent of Public Instruction of most power · WSWS California Democrats strip elected education superintendent of authority

Philadelphia hotel union calls off strike before July 4

UNITE HERE Local 274 ended a nine-day strike at the Sheraton Philadelphia Downtown — which began June 21 as FIFA World Cup crowds filled the city — just before the July 4 weekend. The ratified contract raises non-tipped wages to $30 per hour by 2028 (matching Philadelphia’s citywide hotel standard), cuts the room-attendant quota to 15 rooms, and adds pension and dependent-coverage gains — but the $30 figure remains far below the $44.58 local living wage cited by the WSWS. Nick Barrickman concludes the settlement reflects the union bureaucracy prioritizing business continuity over workers’ interests, and calls for independent rank-and-file committees.

Science, technology, and public health

  • DRC Ebola outbreak passes 400 deaths as US guts surveillance — 399 deaths across 1,333 confirmed cases; no approved vaccine for the Bundibugyo strain
  • US measles cases surpass 2,100, threatening elimination status — 41 jurisdictions affected; ~92% of cases unvaccinated or unknown

DRC Ebola outbreak passes 400 deaths

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, caused by the Bundibugyo virus and declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern in May, has reached 399 deaths across 1,333 confirmed cases as of June 29, according to the DRC’s National Institute of Public Health — an increase of 26 cases and 22 deaths over the prior report. Some 1,214 cases (including 335 deaths) are in Ituri province; 609 patients are hospitalized in isolation, 189 have recovered, and 82.7 percent of identified contacts are under follow-up.

The WSWS’s Benjamin Mateus links the worsening trajectory directly to Trump administration cuts to PEPFAR and CDC global-health programs — including a reported 1,400 percent cut to CDC support in Uganda and a drop in contact tracing from 79 to 43 percent — and notes there is no approved vaccine for the Bundibugyo strain. He characterizes the catastrophe as preventable, reflecting a policy that prioritizes mineral-extraction deals over public-health infrastructure. WHO, CDC and ECDC are all tracking the outbreak, which also affects Uganda.

Sources WHO Ebola disease caused by Bundibugyo virus, DRC & Uganda · CDC Ebola outbreak: current situation · WSWS Ebola deaths pass 400 in the DRC as US guts surveillance systems

US measles cases surpass 2,100

The CDC reported 2,134 confirmed US measles cases in 2026 as of June 25, spread across 41 jurisdictions, with 93 percent of cases outbreak-associated and about 92 percent among people who were unvaccinated or of unknown status. Three deaths have been recorded, and the country risks losing its measles elimination status when authorities review the situation in November. South Carolina confirmed 58 new infections in a recent update.

Other developments: - Neurology (The Lancet): Recent late-stage trials include the PADOVA study of prasinezumab in early Parkinson’s disease and ORATORIO-HAND extending ocrelizumab to wheelchair-using patients with progressive multiple sclerosis; risdiplam data showed deviation from the expected motor-decline course in severe SMA types 1 and 2. - Climate/health: The Lancet Countdown Europe report warns the window for “health-centred action” is narrowing as climate impacts intensify across the continent. - AI/markets: A sharp semiconductor selloff — following an 80%+ H1 rally — dragged Asian tech shares lower, reflecting mounting investor doubts about the sustainability of AI-driven capital spending.

Arts and culture

Turkish cinema lost one of its defining figures. Kadir İnanır (1949-2026), a giant of the Yeşilçam era, died June 26 at age 77 of multiple organ failure caused by advanced lung cancer, after weeks in intensive care. The WSWS obituary by Barış Demir traces his artistic evolution from handsome romantic lead — his celebrated partnership with Türkan Şoray produced some of the most popular melodramas of the 1970s — toward a socially conscious cinema portraying peasants, workers, and prisoners.

That turn, Demir argues, was shaped by İnanır’s opposition to the 1980 military coup, which crushed the Turkish workers’ movement and drove a generation of artists toward the oppressed. His performances in films depicting rural poverty and state repression gave voice to social layers the commercial industry ignored. Demir credits the genuine achievement of İnanır’s later work while criticizing the liberal illusions that led him to believe Turkey’s Kurdish question could be resolved within the framework of the capitalist state — illusions that pulled him toward bourgeois political figures late in life. İnanır’s death was widely marked across the Turkish and regional press.

Roundup: - Marxism Festival 2026 runs July 2-5 in Shoreditch, London, hosted by the British SWP, with a “culture tent,” film screenings, and live music alongside its political programming — an occasion the WSWS would situate within the broader crisis of the pseudo-left’s cultural orientation (see press review below). - Publishing/history debates: Jacobin continued a run of essays on American revolutionary history — including pieces marking the approaching 250th anniversary of 1776 and reviews of Ken Burns’s documentary “The American Revolution” — reflecting a liberal-nationalist framing of the founding rather than a class analysis of the bourgeois-democratic revolution. - The broader picture remains one of institutional austerity and the subordination of cultural production to the market — pressures the WSWS analyzes as inseparable from the wider assault on living standards documented across this briefing.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “The U.S. oligarchy’s crime boss: Trump pocketed $2.2 billion in 2025” by Barry Grey — read in full on wsws.org. (Summarized in Top Story 1 above.)

Beyond the facts of the disclosure, Grey’s argument advances several points not captured above. He details specific corruption mechanisms: three days before the inauguration, a UAE investment firm bought 49 percent of World Liberty Financial for a $23 million family profit, after which the administration approved US chip exports to the UAE; in February 2025 the SEC withdrew oversight of memecoins, directly benefiting $TRUMP; Trump pardoned Binance founder Changpeng Zhao, who then became a key partner to Trump’s crypto ventures; and a US-Kazakhstan tungsten deal involves both Trump’s sons and the family of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, financed by government loans. Grey contrasts the scale with historical benchmarks — the Teapot Dome scandal (~$8 million in today’s terms, which imprisoned Interior Secretary Albert Fall) and Spiro Agnew’s 1973 resignation over ~$250,000 in kickbacks — to argue that what was once criminal has been normalized. His programmatic conclusion: the Democratic Party offers no genuine opposition because it is “controlled by the same class of oligarchs,” and only the SEP fights for expropriation of the ruling class.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output amplified the Colorado primary result as vindication of the DSA’s electoral strategy inside the Democratic Party — precisely the perspective Patrick Martin’s WSWS analysis dissects. The magazine’s ongoing series, including “The Founders Never Meant the US to Be a Democracy” and its engagement with Ken Burns’s “American Revolution,” treats the founding through a liberal-democratic rather than class lens, obscuring the bourgeois character of 1776. Its standing call, “We Need a Left-Labor Presidential Candidate,” makes explicit the reformist program: not a break with the Democratic Party but a bid to pressure it leftward, chaining workers to a capitalist party. The piece “Public Bailouts Are What Keeps Our Economic System Afloat” flirts with MMT-inflected Keynesianism, presenting state intervention as a lever for reform rather than a mechanism of oligarchic self-enrichment. Orientation: electoralist and nationally reformist, seeking to channel radicalization back into the Democratic Party.

Sources Jacobin The Founders Never Meant the US to Be a Democracy · Jacobin We Need a Left-Labor Presidential Candidate

Left Voice

Left Voice’s coverage centered on interviews with CWA Local 7250 officials Kieran Knutson and Lori Wolf on the fight against “Operation Metro Surge” and the anti-ICE movement in Minneapolis. While correctly identifying the immigration crackdown as a central front, Left Voice draws “lessons for the labor movement” that adapt to the very union apparatus that has contained and demobilized these struggles — presenting local union officials as authentic leaders rather than subjecting the bureaucracy to political criticism. The WSWS has previously documented Left Voice’s role as “an accomplice of labor bureaucracy in Minneapolis.” Orientation: militant in rhetoric but tied in practice to the trade union officialdom, substituting activist mobilization for a fight to build rank-and-file organizations independent of the bureaucracy.

Sources Left Voice Recent stories · WSWS Left Voice: An accomplice of labor bureaucracy in Minneapolis

PSL / Liberation News

Liberation News continued to promote its analysis of “The U.S.-Iran Memorandum of Understanding and the Limits of US Imperial Power,” presenting the negotiated framework as evidence of imperialism’s retreat and, implicitly, a victory for the Iranian bourgeois-clerical regime. This inverts a Marxist analysis: the PSL’s campist framework treats the Tehran government — which represses its own working class and has no progressive content — as an anti-imperialist bulwark, sowing dangerous illusions among workers and youth. Orientation: campist and Stalinist-derived, subordinating the international working class to bourgeois-nationalist regimes it deems “anti-imperialist.”

Sources Liberation News The U.S.-Iran Memorandum and the Limits of US Imperial Power

SWP (UK)

The British Socialist Workers Party opened its four-day Marxism Festival 2026 (July 2-5) in London, its central annual recruitment and orientation event. Alongside it, Socialist Worker pushed its analysis of “the end of the two-party system,” framing the rise of Reform UK and the Greens and the fracturing of British politics as an opening to “build the left.” The SWP’s perspective remains oriented toward left-reformist realignment and united fronts with sections of the Labour and trade-union bureaucracy, rather than the construction of a revolutionary party on an internationalist program. Orientation: protest-movement and left-unity politics, adapting to the electoral fragmentation of British capitalism.

Sources Socialist Worker Marxism 2026 — a festival of socialist ideas · Socialist Worker Is it the end of the two-party system?

RCP / IMT

The Revolutionary Communist Party (formerly Socialist Appeal, IMT-aligned) continued its recruitment-driven messaging on communist.red, cataloguing “titanic events” — the US seizure of Maduro in Venezuela, the anti-ICE movement in Minneapolis, the Epstein scandal, and the war on Iran — as automatic drivers of radicalizing consciousness ahead of its national congress and a “1,400 members” growth target. The framework substitutes organizational boosterism and objectivist optimism for a concrete strategy rooted in the historical struggle against Stalinism and Pabloism. Orientation: propagandist and self-promotional, treating the deepening crisis as a recruitment opportunity divorced from the fight for the political independence of the working class.
