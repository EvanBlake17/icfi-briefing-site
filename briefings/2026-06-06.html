1. US strikes Iranian radar sites as Iran threatens the Strait of Hormuz

The US military struck Iranian coastal surveillance radar stations on June 6 after Iran launched multiple attack drones toward the Strait of Hormuz, threatening the world’s most important oil-shipping chokepoint. The exchange marks at least day 97 of open US-Iran hostilities, with negotiators describing the talks as deadlocked.

The escalation underscores the trajectory the WSWS has consistently described — a US imperialist campaign against Iran that risks igniting a broader regional and world war, with the working masses of the Gulf bearing the human cost.

Key facts: - Iranian negotiator Mohsen Rezaee said June 5 that talks were at an impasse and that the US needed to “take action.” - Asked whether an Iranian killing of US troops would be a red line, Trump said June 6 it “would be a good reason to restart the war.” - Senior US officials Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff visited Oak Ridge National Laboratory on June 5 to consult nuclear experts on potential deal frameworks. - US Indo-Pacific Command intercepted the tanker M/T Davina in the Indian Ocean on June 5, alleging it was supporting Iran in violation of the blockade; the crew’s status was not reported.

2. Lebanon ceasefire collapses as Israel expands occupation and moves to annex Gaza

At least 21 people were killed in Israeli airstrikes on southern Lebanon on June 5, according to Lebanon’s National News Agency, with at least five confirmed dead in the Nabatieh District including a paramedic. The US-brokered ceasefire — extended 45 days in mid-May — is now described by diplomats as effectively broken.

Lebanese Parliament Speaker Nabih Berri formally rejected the ceasefire June 5, citing the absence of guarantees of complete Israeli withdrawal. Hezbollah continues to refuse disarmament as a precondition. CNN reported, citing Ynetnews and Haaretz, that Israel had deployed commandos and intelligence personnel to Azerbaijan near Iran’s northern border.

On the WSWS, Andre Damon framed Lebanon and Gaza as a single coordinated US-Israeli campaign: Israeli forces have placed over 2,000 square kilometers — roughly one-fifth of Lebanon — under occupation Netanyahu has declared “permanent,” while in Gaza Netanyahu announced control of 60 percent of the territory and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich demanded “full conquest.”

Key facts: - Lebanon’s health ministry documents at least 3,516 killed and 10,674 wounded since March 2, with over one million displaced; Israel ordered forced displacement from nine additional towns June 6. - Gaza’s health ministry recorded 929 Palestinians killed and 2,811 wounded since the ceasefire technically took effect; Israeli forces shot and killed a seven-month-old Palestinian baby near Hebron. - A US House War Powers resolution opposing US involvement in Lebanon failed 324–92, with Democrats among those opposing.

Sources CNN June 5 live news: Lebanon, Israel war · Al Jazeera Iran war live updates · WSWS Israel escalates assault on Lebanon and drives to annex Gaza

3. Ukraine launches mass drone strikes deep into Russia

Overnight June 6, Ukraine launched drone strikes reaching up to 1,000 km into Russia, targeting Russian naval arsenals at the historic port of Kronstadt near St. Petersburg and a major oil transshipment terminal. Russia’s Defense Ministry said its air defenses downed 376 drones overnight, including more than 140 over Leningrad Oblast.

The attacks bracketed Putin’s flagship St. Petersburg International Economic Forum (“Putin’s Davos”): on June 3, Ukrainian drones struck the city’s oil terminal hours before the forum opened, with Russia intercepting over 350 drones in that wave. Analysts described the timing as deliberate psychological and symbolic pressure. Zelenskyy reported “positive results” and described Kronstadt as a strategic target for Russian naval capacity.

Key facts: - The June 3 St. Petersburg terminal is among the largest oil-handling facilities in northwestern Russia. - The campaign is part of an intensifying NATO-backed proxy war in which deep-strike escalation increasingly targets Russian energy and naval infrastructure.

Sources CNN Ukraine drone attack on St. Petersburg · Kyiv Independent Oil depot set ablaze as Ukraine launches drones · NPR Ukrainian drones hit St. Petersburg

A violent chip-stock rout wiped roughly $1 trillion from global markets, sending the Nasdaq to its worst single day since April 2025. A much-stronger-than-expected May jobs report (+172,000 vs. +85,000 forecast) sent Treasury yields spiking, raised Fed rate-hike odds, and crushed AI and semiconductor names. CNN called it the S&P 500 and Nasdaq’s worst day of 2026.

Key facts: - Nasdaq closed −4.18% (25,709.43); S&P 500 −2.64% (7,383.74); Dow −1.35% (50,866.78). - The sell-off spread to Asia, where South Korea’s chip-heavy KOSPI fell 5.54% — the hardest-hit major index globally. - The episode exposes how narrowly the Wall Street boom has been concentrated in a handful of AI companies, even as real wages fall for millions.

5. US Senate approves $70 billion immigration enforcement bill

The Senate voted 52–47 early June 6 to approve $70 billion in immigration enforcement funding for ICE and Customs and Border Protection through the end of Trump’s term — $38.6 billion for ICE, $26 billion for CBP, and $5 billion in discretionary DHS enforcement funds. The 18-hour session ended a months-long Democratic blockade after several Republican holdouts dropped opposition in exchange for a pledge to scrap a controversial $1.8 billion “anti-weaponization fund.”

The bill now goes to the House, which is not expected to take it up until the week of June 9. It funds enforcement for three years — through January 2029 — locking in the current deportation-and-detention framework regardless of future political shifts.

In its June 6 Perspective, the WSWS argued the Democratic Party did not merely fail to stop the expansion but actively enabled it, agreeing to separate DHS funding so that every Democratic senator could cast a symbolic “No” vote while the package passed on Republican votes and the rest of government stayed funded.

Sources CNBC Senate passes $70 billion for ICE, Border Patrol · NPR Senate passes immigration enforcement bill · WSWS How the Democrats enabled $70 billion for Trump’s immigration Gestapo

6. North Korea unveils new uranium plant as Xi prepares first Pyongyang visit in seven years

North Korean state media published photos June 3–4 of Kim Jong Un inspecting a large new centrifuge hall consistent with weapons-grade uranium enrichment, assessed by experts as likely a second facility at or near Yongbyon. Kim declared the North has more than doubled its capacity to produce weapons-grade nuclear material over five years and that the plant would drive “exponential” growth in nuclear forces.

The disclosure came days before Xi Jinping’s confirmed June 8–9 visit to Pyongyang — his first since 2019 and only his second since taking power in 2012 — marking the 65th anniversary of the China–North Korea friendship treaty. The trip follows separate summits in which Xi hosted both Trump and Putin within the past month.

Key facts: - South Korean officials reported in September 2025 that North Korea was running four uranium enrichment facilities continuously; this represents a fifth or expanded site, and the third public disclosure of an enrichment facility (after 2010 Yongbyon and 2024 Kangson). - Analysts at The Diplomat describe a deepening Russia–China–DPRK strategic alignment, reinforced by North Korean troop deployments in Ukraine.

Sources NPR North Korea unveils new plant for nuclear weapons fuel · Bloomberg Xi to visit North Korea June 8–9 · The Diplomat Xi to visit North Korea amid deepening three-way alliance

7. Iranian drones strike Kuwait airport, killing Indian migrant worker

Kuwait’s defence ministry detected 30 ballistic missiles and drones launched by Iran on June 3, severely damaging the passenger terminal of Kuwait International Airport. One person was killed — an Indian national — and at least 63 were injured, including airport workers and passengers. Kuwait’s foreign ministry said the attack also damaged unnamed diplomatic missions.

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard denied firing on the airport, claiming via state broadcaster IRIB that the damage came from a failed US Patriot interceptor. US Central Command called the claim “false” and described the attack as “deliberate, calculated and unjustified.” Kuwait Airways resumed flights after a brief suspension.

The single fatality — almost certainly a low-wage migrant worker among the estimated 3.5 million Indian nationals employed in Kuwait under kafala-style arrangements — was treated by the global news cycle as a data point, with no reporting on who he was.

Germany fails to win UN Security Council seat

Germany received only 104 votes in the UN General Assembly’s election for a non-permanent Security Council seat, far short of the 127 needed. Portugal (134) and Austria (131) won the two available European seats — the first time Germany has failed to win such a seat since reunification in 1990, despite Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul’s intensive worldwide lobbying.

On the WSWS, Peter Schwarz argued the defeat reflects global mistrust of Germany’s aggressive militarism — its backing of Israeli operations in Gaza framed as a “national interest,” support for Trump’s Iran conflict, co-leadership of Ukraine war logistics, and development of long-range weapons with Kyiv. Notably, the Greens, Left Party and AfD criticized the government for damaging Germany’s standing rather than questioning the militarization itself.

Bolivian general strike enters sixth week

Bolivia’s indefinite general strike entered its 36th day, with over 100 active road blockades strangling access to La Paz and El Alto declaring a “permanent mobilized state of emergency.” President Rodrigo Paz appointed as defense minister Ernesto Justiniano — a former “anti-drug tsar” who served under Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada during the 2003 “Gas Wars” massacre that killed at least 67 people — signaling readiness for large-scale repression.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio committed “emergency assistance and logistical support” to the Paz government, with a Trump official warning Bolivia “must not allow itself to fall prey to narco-terrorist dominance.” A new law granting “presumption of legality” for police and military during states of exception would effectively immunize state violence. Workers maintain a single demand — Paz’s resignation — and rejected negotiations; ten peasants who occupied an oil field were sentenced to three years imprisonment.

US measles cases hit 1,983, surpassing all of 2025

As of May 28, the CDC reported 1,983 confirmed measles cases across 40 jurisdictions in 2026 — already surpassing the 2,288 reported for all of 2025 with seven months remaining, and on pace for the worst year since the US declared measles eliminated in 2000. There have been 30 new outbreaks in 2026, with 93% of cases outbreak-associated; a single South Carolina cluster in Spartanburg County accounts for roughly 990 cases. The surge tracks the collapse of public-health infrastructure and falling vaccination rates amid official anti-vaccine politics.

Long COVID may affect 1 in 6 patients

A Mass General Brigham study published in JAMA Network Open (DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.14909) applied an AI algorithm to the records of roughly 460,000 patients across 58 US hospitals and found that about 16% developed Long COVID — translating to over 18 million Americans and roughly double current surveillance estimates, which rely on diagnostic codes that miss most cases. The finding reveals a major hidden public-health burden and directly challenges the accuracy of official tracking.

Science roundup

  • H5N1 bird flu: The CDC reports 71 confirmed human cases and 2 deaths in the US since the dairy-cattle outbreak began in March 2024 — 41 in dairy workers (36 in California), 24 in poultry workers. No human-to-human transmission confirmed. Globally, the WHO tracked 13 avian/swine flu human cases in Q1 2026, including a fatal pediatric H5N1 case in Bangladesh.
  • COVID-19: Infections are declining or stable in 49 of 50 states as of June 2 — the lowest national spread since spring 2021. The FDA recently approved ensitrelvir (Xocova) as post-exposure prophylaxis; taken within 72 hours of exposure, it reduced symptomatic COVID by 67% in trials, giving clinicians a tool analogous to HIV PrEP.
  • AI policy: Trump signed an executive order June 2 asking AI developers to voluntarily submit frontier models to the NSA, CISA and Treasury for security review 30 days before release — cut from a 90-day draft after industry lobbying. It is the first formal federal framework for pre-release AI oversight, however weak.
  • AI capability: Anthropic expanded Project Glasswing to 150 new organizations across 15+ countries, mostly critical-infrastructure operators; its Claude Mythos preview has identified more than 10,000 high- or critical-severity software vulnerabilities — a capability the company is deliberately restricting from general availability.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural event marked in the past day is the death of tenor saxophonist Sonny Rollins, one of the last and greatest figures of the bebop generation, reviewed at length on the WSWS by Jesse Thomas and John Andrews. Born September 7, 1930 in Harlem and dead at 95 on May 25, 2026 in Woodstock, New York from progressive pulmonary fibrosis, Rollins possessed what the authors describe as “an earthy, broad sound” that synthesized Coleman Hawkins, Lester Young and Charlie Parker into something wholly his own.

His landmark recordings — Saxophone Colossus (1956), Tenor Madness (1956) and Freedom Suite (1958) — and his improvisations stretching past ten minutes helped define post-war jazz. The WSWS obituary is distinguished from most by its attention to Rollins’s social formation. As a child he attended Camp Unity, described as “an interracial, antichauvinist, anticapitalist summer camp” run by Communist Party branches, and his aunt Mirium was a left-wing activist. Freedom Suite, recorded against the backdrop of the civil-rights struggle, stands as one of the era’s most explicit fusions of musical innovation and social protest.

Rollins’s life also illustrates the toll of capitalist society on artists: he overcame heroin addiction in the early 1950s and took two extended sabbaticals (1959–1962 and 1968–1971) to study yoga and meditation, famously practicing alone on the Williamsburg Bridge. In a final, bitter irony, the September 11, 2001 attacks near his Manhattan apartment caused the pulmonary damage that ended his performing career; his last public performance came in 2012. His death severs another link to a generation of Black American musicians who forged a major art form under conditions of segregation and exploitation.

Rollins’s passing invites reflection on the present cultural moment, in which militarism and nationalism increasingly saturate official culture, and arts funding remains under sustained political pressure. Against that backdrop, the artistic seriousness and internationalist sensibility of figures like Rollins — formed in the milieu of the 1930s–40s left — stand as a reproach to the commodified, identity-segmented cultural product of today.

Cultural roundup: - Jazz legacy: Tributes from across the music world underscore Rollins’s influence on generations of saxophonists; his catalogue on Prestige, Blue Note and Riverside remains foundational to the post-war canon. - Historical memory: The WSWS framing — situating his art in the Communist-influenced cultural institutions of mid-century America — recovers a history of working-class and anti-racist cultural organizing that bourgeois obituaries routinely omit.

Pseudo-left press review

  • Left Voice — Calls for mobilization against ICE and the Iran war but ties it to a cross-class “movement” rather than a break from the Democrats
  • Liberation News / PSL — Anti-imperialist denunciations of US-Iran war that veil political support for capitalist states in Tehran and beyond
  • Socialist Alternative — Demands on union officials and a labor party, while sowing illusions in the union apparatus
  • Socialist Worker / SWP (UK) — “Stop the war” agitation channeled into pressure on Labour and protest-as-an-end-in-itself
  • Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT) — Triumphal “revolution is on the agenda” rhetoric detached from a concrete program against the bureaucracies

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s coverage of the Senate’s $70 billion enforcement package treats it as a story of Democratic weakness and Republican overreach, urging readers toward electoral pressure and primary challenges to “move the party left.” This is the characteristic Jacobin line: the immigration crackdown is presented as a policy failure to be corrected within the Democratic Party rather than the product of a bipartisan state apparatus. The framing directly contradicts the documented mechanics — Democrats structuring the vote so the bill would pass while members cast symbolic “No” votes — and obscures the party’s active role. On the Iran war, Jacobin/DSA commentary tends toward calls for congressional restraint and “no war” resolutions, lending credibility to the same legislators (and the failed 324–92 War Powers vote) who sustain the war machine.

Assessment: Oriented throughout toward rehabilitating the Democratic Party as a vehicle for reform, channeling anti-ICE and antiwar sentiment back into electoralism.

Left Voice

Left Voice (PTS-aligned) is the most likely of the US tendencies to call openly for mass mobilization against ICE and for solidarity with the Minneapolis protests, and to denounce the US-Iran war as imperialist. Its weakness lies in dissolving the question of political leadership into a generalized “movement” and front-building with forces that remain tied to the union bureaucracy and, ultimately, the Democrats. Coverage of auto and transit struggles tends to celebrate rank-and-file militancy without drawing the conclusion the WSWS insists upon — the necessity of a political break from the apparatus through independent rank-and-file committees and a revolutionary party.

Assessment: Militant rhetoric combined with organizational adaptation to the existing bureaucratic and protest milieu.

Liberation News / PSL

Liberation News offers sharp denunciations of US strikes on Iran, the Hormuz blockade and Israeli operations in Lebanon and Gaza, foregrounding US imperialism. Its anti-imperialism, however, is of the campist variety: it treats the Iranian, Russian and other capitalist states as objective anti-imperialist allies rather than analyzing the class character of those regimes, and it subordinates the independent interests of the Iranian, Lebanese and Russian working classes to bourgeois nationalism. On North Korea, PSL coverage tends to defend the Kim regime’s nuclear program as legitimate self-defense rather than examining its reactionary nationalist basis.

Assessment: Genuine opposition to US war coexists with political adaptation to capitalist and Stalinist-nationalist regimes abroad.

Socialist Alternative

Socialist Alternative’s output around the ICE bill and the strike wave emphasizes building union power and demanding that officials “fight,” alongside the call for a new workers’ party. While it raises militant demands, it consistently routes them through the existing union structures — appealing to the UAW, SEIU and others to act rather than warning workers that these bureaucracies are the chief obstacle, as the Nexteer and Madison nurses’ experiences demonstrate. Its “workers’ party” call remains an abstraction divorced from a struggle against the bureaucracy’s political subordination to the Democrats.

Assessment: Left-reformist pressure politics aimed at reforming, not breaking from, the union apparatus.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP’s coverage of the Middle East escalation centers on “Stop the war” protest-building, with the perspective of pressuring the Labour government to change course. This reprises the Stop the War Coalition model the WSWS has long criticized: mass anger over Gaza and Iran is channeled into episodic demonstrations and parliamentary appeals rather than the political mobilization of the working class against the Labour government itself. The SWP’s enthusiasm for “the movement” substitutes activity for program.

Assessment: Protest-as-an-end-in-itself, oriented toward pressuring Labour rather than opposing it.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

The IMT’s press strikes a triumphal note — declaring that capitalism is in terminal crisis and “revolution is on the agenda” — while its concrete interventions in strikes and antiwar struggles remain general agitation for “communism” detached from the specific fight against the trade-union bureaucracies and the Labour/social-democratic parties. Its rebranding as the “Revolutionary Communist Party” emphasizes recruitment and propaganda over the construction of a tested revolutionary leadership rooted in the strategic experiences of the Fourth International.

Assessment: Maximalist rhetoric paired with an absence of a concrete strategy against the labor bureaucracy.

No direct attacks on the ICFI or WSWS were identified in the past 24 hours, though the convergence of Jacobin’s “Democratic failure” framing with the bourgeois press’s procedural narrative on the ICE bill is notable.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Whose death counts? The Indian migrant worker killed at Kuwait airport

The single fatality in Iran’s strike on Kuwait International Airport was an Indian national — almost certainly a low-wage migrant worker among the estimated 3.5 million Indians employed in Kuwait under kafala-style arrangements. The global news cycle treated his death as a geopolitical data point, with no reporting on who he was or the conditions that brought him there. A WSWS piece could restore the human and class dimension to a war whose victims are overwhelmingly workers.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Iranian drone kills Indian citizen in Kuwait

2. Seafarers as collateral: maritime labor in a war economy

Five Azerbaijani nationals working aboard vessels in Russia’s Taganrog Bay were killed June 5 in a Ukrainian drone strike, and the crew of the US-intercepted tanker M/T Davina near Hormuz remains unaccounted for. Seafarers from third-party nations face extraordinary danger as commercial routes overlap with combat zones, with no special protections and minimal coverage. This is a sharp illustration of how imperialist war turns the international working class into expendable casualties.

Recommended reading Current Events June 2026 (citing Moscow Times)

3. Cuba’s hotel workers and the weaponization of sanctions

Archipelago International’s exit from six Cuban hotels under US sanctions pressure leaves hundreds of workers in limbo and exposes how Washington’s sanctions regime functions as a direct mechanism of working-class immiseration. The story is largely absent from US media, which treats sanctions as foreign policy rather than as economic warfare against ordinary Cubans — a natural subject for the WSWS’s coverage of US imperialism in Latin America.

Recommended reading Current Events June 2026 (citing Manila Times)

4. Sri Lanka nursing home fire and the price of IMF austerity

A nursing home fire killed at least 13 people in Sri Lanka around June 5, receiving almost no international coverage. With the country still reeling from its 2022 economic collapse and IMF-mandated austerity, the disaster points to systemic neglect of the elderly poor and the gutting of social-care infrastructure across the Global South — a concrete case study for the WSWS’s analysis of debt and austerity.

Recommended reading Current Events June 2026 (citing CTV News)

5. The KOSPI’s 5.5% crash and the global semiconductor labor force

South Korea’s chip-heavy KOSPI fell 5.54% — the hardest-hit major index in Friday’s rout — concentrating the global sell-off on Samsung and SK Hynix. The volatility in AI/semiconductor stocks sits atop a vast, highly exploited electronics manufacturing workforce in South Korea, Taiwan and across Asia. A WSWS piece could connect the speculative AI bubble to the conditions of the workers who actually produce the chips.

Recommended reading CNBC Asia markets: KOSPI crushed in semiconductor rout

6. Tropical Storm Jangmi and the social cost of climate disaster in Japan

Tropical Storm Jangmi forced evacuation advisories for more than 800,000 residents and produced “record-level” early-June rainfall in the Tokyo region. As extreme weather intensifies, the burden falls hardest on working-class and elderly residents with the fewest resources to evacuate or rebuild. The WSWS could examine the inadequacy of capitalist disaster planning against the backdrop of accelerating climate change.

Recommended reading Current Events June 2026 (Tropical Storm Jangmi)
