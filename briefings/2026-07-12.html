1. US launches third round of strikes on Iran as Tehran closes Strait of Hormuz

US Central Command carried out a third round of strikes this week, reportedly hitting some 140 Iranian military targets after Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) fired on a merchant vessel transiting the Strait of Hormuz. The IRGC declared the strait closed again, said it fired a warning shot at a ship attempting an “unapproved route,” and vowed a “crushing response” to further US attacks. Mediators in Qatar and Oman are pressing to revive US-Iran talks. The fighting marks roughly the fifth month of the war opened by the February 28, 2026 US-Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei.

Writing on the WSWS, Kevin Reed noted the US has now struck roughly 170 Iranian sites and that Trump has declared the ceasefire “over,” arguing that imperialism is using “peace” negotiations as cover for continued war amid oil-price spikes and slowing global growth.

Key facts: - No large tanker (over 10,000 dwt) has transited the US-coordinated “Southern Highway” with AIS tracking on since July 7, versus a pre-war baseline of about 130 vessels per day. - The IMF is projecting 3% global growth and 4.7% inflation as the oil-shipping risk premium persists. - Mourners at Khamenei’s funeral chanted for Trump’s death and displayed a banner reading “We Will Kill Trump.”

2. Senator Lindsey Graham dies at 71 after “brief and sudden illness”

Senator Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.), 71, died Saturday night, July 11. Police-scanner audio indicated an emergency call for “cardiac arrest” at his Capitol Hill home. Graham chaired the Senate Budget Committee and had just returned from Kyiv, where he met President Volodymyr Zelensky on Friday during his tenth wartime visit to Ukraine. One of the most aggressive advocates of US militarism of his generation, Graham backed nearly every American war of the past quarter century and pushed relentlessly for escalation against both Russia and Iran.

Key facts: - Trump called Graham “one of the greatest people and Senators I have ever known.” - Netanyahu said “Israel has lost one of its greatest friends. America has lost a great patriot.” - Zelensky praised Graham’s ten wartime visits to Ukraine and his sponsorship of sanctions legislation against Russia. - His death, with a Republican governor in South Carolina, will not shift the Senate’s partisan balance but removes a central architect of the war party.

3. Russian missile and drone barrage kills at least 8 across Ukraine

At least eight people were killed and dozens wounded in overnight Russian strikes. Five died and 30 were wounded in Sumy after glide bombs struck a crowded area; two were killed in Odesa and one in Sloviansk, with additional wounded in Kyiv (11+) and Kharkiv (7). Zelensky said “civilian infrastructure was hit even before the air raid alert was issued,” and that Ukrainian defenses stopped most drones “but not the ballistic ones.”

Key facts: - Russia fired six Iskander-M/S-400 ballistic missiles, six cruise missiles, and 121 drones; 111 drones were intercepted, but all ballistic missiles penetrated defenses. - Ukrainian officials say Russia is exploiting an “acute shortage of Patriot interceptors” to intensify ballistic strikes on civilian areas. - Ukraine struck 21 fuel tankers and seven cargo and support ships in the Sea of Azov overnight — 76 vessels hit in the past week.

4. Venezuela earthquake death toll passes 4,300 with tens of thousands still missing

The death toll from June’s twin earthquakes has climbed to roughly 4,333, with 16,740 injured, according to official figures cited July 12. Nearly 50,000 people remain unaccounted for, per the International Rescue Committee. More than 750 buildings have collapsed and the national water system has failed across seven states. Rescue crews are winding down as families begin the grim task of recovering their own dead.

Key facts: - The US deployed roughly 900 search-and-rescue personnel inside Venezuela plus about 800 more staged in Puerto Rico and Curaçao. - International aid is UN-coordinated, with Brazil, Mexico, Canada, Cuba, and Switzerland participating. - Recovery is reportedly hampered by shortages of heavy machinery and medical supplies — an infrastructural deficit that years of US sanctions have deepened.

5. Trump fires remaining Election Assistance Commission members ahead of midterms

Trump has fired the last three members of the federal Election Assistance Commission (EAC), leaving the four-seat bipartisan panel with zero commissioners months before the midterm elections. Democrats Thomas Hicks and Benjamin Hovland were dismissed by email; Republican Christy McCormick was asked to resign by phone. The agency cannot take official action — including issuing voting-system guidance and certifying election equipment — until new members are confirmed.

Key facts: - The White House cited the Supreme Court’s recent 6-3 ruling upholding Trump’s authority to remove an FTC member as legal precedent for the removals. - The EAC administers federal election standards and distributes funding to states for election security and administration. - Voting-rights advocates and election officials warn of federal interference in election administration ahead of November.

Science, technology, and public health

  • Measles cases near 2,231 for 2026 — 2026 is on pace to exceed 2025’s full-year total, with 93% of cases outbreak-associated
  • H5N1 bird flu continues low-level human spillover — 12 international human cases since August 2025, three fatal, amid ongoing US dairy and poultry outbreaks
  • The Lancet publishes its July 11 issue — Volume 408 lands amid mounting concern over war-driven disruption to global health systems

Public health roundup

Measles. As of July 9, the CDC reported 2,231 confirmed measles cases in the United States for 2026 — already approaching the 2,289 recorded in all of 2025. There have been 32 new outbreaks reported this year, and 93% of confirmed cases (2,082 of 2,231) are outbreak-associated, reflecting collapsing vaccination coverage in undervaccinated communities and the erosion of public-health infrastructure. The trajectory points to 2026 becoming the worst measles year in a generation.

H5N1 avian influenza. A(H5) bird flu remains widespread in wild birds and continues to cause outbreaks in US poultry and dairy cattle, with sporadic human infections among dairy and poultry workers. From August 4, 2025 to June 10, 2026, 12 human H5N1 infections were reported by three countries outside the US, three of them fatal (in Bangladesh and Cambodia). The CDC continues to rate the public-health risk as low while monitoring people with animal exposures — but the combination of sustained mammalian spillover and gutted surveillance capacity remains a serious pandemic-preparedness concern.

Research and technology

  • The Lancet published its current issue (Vol. 408, Issue 10550) on July 11, alongside July editions of The Lancet Neurology and The Lancet Infectious Diseases. The issues appear as public-health researchers warn that active wars in Iran, Ukraine, and Gaza are disrupting disease surveillance, vaccination campaigns, and clinical-trial pipelines across affected regions.
  • AI and semiconductors drove the week’s markets, with a chip-stock rally lifting Asian indices — a reminder that the enormous capital flows into AI infrastructure are proceeding in tandem with mass layoffs across the broader economy, concentrating both computing power and wealth.
  • Climate and environment. Rising ocean temperatures and marine heatwaves around Britain and elsewhere are intensifying, part of the broader climate emergency now manifesting in the extreme-rainfall flooding seen in Missouri and repeated deadly events worldwide.

The through-line across these developments is the subordination of public health, scientific research, and pandemic preparedness to war spending and private profit. Surveillance systems for measles and H5N1 are being defunded even as both spread; scientific capacity is diverted toward military and AI applications; and the same governments pleading poverty on health care are financing record military budgets.

Arts and culture

The 60th Karlovy Vary International Film Festival closed July 11, awarding its top prize, the Grand Prix–Crystal Globe, to Fruit Gathering, the debut feature of Myanmar director Aung Phyoe. Set in contemporary Myanmar, the film follows the friendship that forms between two young women working in a textile factory; the jury described it as beginning “as a lush and meditative portrait of work and friendship before morphing, unexpectedly and organically, into a harrowing drama of obsession and queer desire.” That a film rooted in the labor and lives of women textile workers in a country ruled by a military junta took the top honor at one of Europe’s oldest festivals is significant — a rare instance of international cinema centering the working class of the global South rather than treating it as backdrop. The film takes home the Crystal Globe and $25,000 split between director and producers.

The past days also brought the deaths of several major cultural figures:

  • Bonnie Tyler, the Welsh singer whose gravel-voiced anthems “Total Eclipse of the Heart” and “Holding Out for a Hero” became global standards, died July 8 at 75. Rising from a working-class background in Swansea, Tyler embodied a era of emotive, widely accessible popular music.
  • S. Janaki, one of India’s most prolific playback singers, died July 11 at 88. Her career, spanning from 1957 across multiple Indian languages, made her voice inseparable from decades of South Asian cinema and an entire popular musical culture.
  • Peppino di Capri, the Italian singer and pianist, died July 11 at 86, closing a career that helped define postwar Italian popular song.
  • Đorđe Kadijević, the Serbian film director, screenwriter and art critic celebrated for his gothic horror work rooted in Balkan folklore, died July 10 at 93.

Also notable: Victor Willis, the original lead singer of Village People — whose “Y.M.C.A.” was appropriated as a fixture at Trump rallies — died July 1. His death is a reminder of how works created within one social context can be stripped of meaning and repurposed as political spectacle, the disco anthem of a marginalized subculture turned into a nationalist rally soundtrack.

The contrast running through the day’s cultural news is stark: on one side, a festival elevating a film about factory workers’ lives; on the other, the ongoing commodification and political instrumentalization of popular art. The WSWS framework insists that genuine artistic achievement — Tyler’s emotional directness, Janaki’s craft, Aung Phyoe’s attention to working-class life — be recognized on its own terms, against both the entertainment industry’s reduction of art to product and the right’s attempt to conscript culture into reaction.

Perspective published: None. No Perspective column dated July 12, 2026 has been published. The most recent Perspective is “The mass layoffs in Germany’s auto industry and the global war on jobs” by Tom Hall, dated July 11.

The mass layoffs in Germany’s auto industry and the global war on jobs — Tom Hall

Hall uses Volkswagen’s restructuring — as many as 100,000 jobs cut worldwide, roughly 40,000 in Germany, with annual capacity shrinking from 10 to 9 million vehicles and half the model lineup eliminated — as the entry point for a broader thesis. He argues this is not a national market adjustment but a synchronized cross-border offensive by capital, waged industry-by-industry to prevent unified resistance, citing 1.2 million US job cuts announced in the past year and a further 125,000 German auto losses projected by 2035. The IG Metall bureaucracy, he charges, is actively collaborating to implement layoffs “in an orderly fashion.” The piece ties the layoffs directly to rearmament: the same states pleading poverty for health and education are pouring record sums into war budgets, proving “there is no money” is a political choice. Its conclusion is programmatic — independent, internationally coordinated rank-and-file committees, linking German and American autoworkers rather than pitting them against one another.

As US strikes intensify, Trump says the Iran ceasefire is “over” — Kevin Reed

Beyond the strike figures cited above, the WSWS article stresses the economic dimension: oil-price spikes and slowing global growth (IMF projecting 3% growth, 4.7% inflation) as the backdrop to renewed war, and the use of “peace” negotiations as cover for continued aggression. Its distinctive conclusion is the call for independent international working-class mobilization against war — in Iran, the US, and globally — rejecting reliance on any wing of the bourgeois political establishment to halt the drive to war.

28 killed in shoe factory fire in China — Peter Symonds

A fire at Huiteng Footwear in Jinjiang — a city producing roughly 20% of the world’s sports shoes — killed 28 of 237 workers present. Symonds places it within a national pattern: nearly 20,000 workplace accidents and over 18,000 deaths in China in 2025. The article argues profit-driven cost-cutting and lax enforcement, not “accident,” explain the disaster, indicting both private capital and the CCP regime’s subordination of worker safety to export competitiveness.

Inmates face torture after prison riots in Sri Lanka — Wasantha Rupasinghe

Following the July 5-6 Negombo Prison riots that killed over 30 people, more than 1,000 transferred inmates reportedly face torture, with autopsies showing gunshot and blunt-force deaths. The prison system holds 42,000 inmates in facilities built for 11,762, with 75% held on remand awaiting trial. The piece frames the violence as an expression of capitalist state repression requiring a revolutionary socialist response rather than appeals to state institutions.

ICE killer remains free as witnesses to Houston shooting are held in immigrant prison — Jacob Crosse

Three construction-worker witnesses to ICE’s fatal shooting of Lorenzo Salgado Araujo are themselves detained in a Conroe, Texas ICE facility, and all independently contradict ICE’s claim that the victim tried to run over an agent. The article demands the witnesses’ release, prosecution of the agent, and abolition of ICE, criticizing Democratic officials for calling merely for “investigations.”

German government declares war on the working class with radical austerity reforms — Marianne Arens, Peter Schwarz

Germany’s July 2 reform package doubles defense spending to €184 billion while cutting social protections, offering ordinary families only ~€50/month in tax relief and threatening 300,000 metalworking and electrical jobs. A ZDF poll found 81% consider the new sick-leave rules unfair. The authors present the guns-versus-butter tradeoff as a conscious ruling-class offensive and warn of the danger of a fascist resurgence absent independent working-class mobilization.

Berlin’s CDU-SPD government turns German capital into a centre of war production — Stefan Steinberg

Berlin’s coalition is converting the city into a military-industrial hub — roughly 130 defense firms (26,000 jobs, €8 billion turnover) plus 430 dual-use firms, a new “defence technology ecosystem,” and Rheinmetall converting a former auto plant for weapons production. Steinberg draws the historical parallel to Rheinmetall’s Nazi-era role, stressing that militarization is proceeding without democratic debate and with union complicity, opposed only by the SEP.

Canada announces its largest ever military procurement to purchase 12 German-made attack submarines — Roger Jordan

Canada will spend $100 billion on 12 German-made 212CD submarines from Thyssenkrupp, part of a pivot toward European/NATO integration amid tensions with Washington, alongside expanded troop deployments to Latvia. Jordan frames this as Canadian imperialism repositioning itself — not defending working-class interests — and calls for an international anti-war movement.

Left Voice

The PSL’s activism draws real anti-war sentiment into the streets, but its politics remain those of a protest lobby oriented, in practice, toward the left wing of the Democratic Party and campist alignments abroad rather than a struggle for workers’ power.
