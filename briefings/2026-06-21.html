1. Israel pounds Lebanon as Vance heads to Switzerland for Iran talks

Israeli airstrikes killed at least 83 people and wounded 141 across Lebanon on Friday, June 20, the majority in the south, even as Washington prepared to convene the first session of US-Iran talks in Burgenstock, Switzerland. More than 100 Israeli airstrikes have struck southern Lebanon since midnight Saturday. The fighting persists despite the Trump-Iran memorandum of understanding signed June 17, which calls for hostilities to end “on all fronts” — but which neither Israel nor Hezbollah signed.

Vice President JD Vance is leading the US delegation, joined by Jared Kushner and envoy Steve Witkoff; Iran is represented by Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf. An emergency session on Lebanon has been added as the first agenda item. Vance told reporters his priorities are structuring the talks, advancing the nuclear file, and securing a Lebanon ceasefire.

Key facts:

  • Since March 2, at least 4,057 people have been killed and 12,121 wounded in Lebanon, according to figures cited across wire reporting
  • Netanyahu and Defense Minister Katz declared Israeli troops will remain in southern Lebanon “as long as necessary”; on June 21 Netanyahu ordered the IDF to hold fire everywhere except Ali al-Taher Hill, where clashes with Hezbollah continue
  • Five Israeli soldiers have been killed since the US-Iran deal was signed June 17
  • Hezbollah’s Ali Fayyad: “A ceasefire while the enemy continues its targeting and assassinations is meaningless”

The working-class and poor communities of southern Lebanon, devastated since 2023, are again bearing the human cost while diplomats negotiate in a Swiss luxury resort. The exclusion of Lebanon and Hezbollah from the memorandum is not an oversight but the architecture of an imperialist settlement imposed over the heads of the region’s population.

2. Iran declares Strait of Hormuz closed; US says traffic is flowing

Iran’s military command and the IRGC Navy formally announced the Strait of Hormuz is closed to shipping, citing Israel’s violations of the Lebanon ceasefire. US Central Command flatly contradicted the claim, reporting that 55 ships carrying over 17 million barrels of oil and cargo transited the strait, with traffic described as increased rather than halted.

The closure announcement is widely interpreted as diplomatic leverage. Tehran reportedly told Hezbollah it will not formally reopen the strait until Israel publicly commits to ending military operations in Lebanon. The dispute is the central backdrop to the Switzerland talks and threatens to unravel the June 17 memorandum.

Key facts:

  • Roughly one-fifth of global oil supply normally transits Hormuz; any genuine closure would be a major shock to world energy markets
  • Oil prices eased on the week after the US-Iran deal signaled a potential reopening, with WTI near $76.51 and Brent near $80.59
  • The contradictory claims underscore that the “peace” deal rests on no stable basis — both sides are maneuvering for advantage while the population bears the risk of renewed war

3. Ukraine launches largest-ever drone attack on Moscow

Ukraine launched nearly 200 drones against Moscow — the largest drone raid on the Russian capital since the 2022 invasion began. The primary target was Moscow’s main southeastern oil refinery, struck for the second time in a single week; thick black smoke hung over the city for hours. Russia pledged “massive group strikes” in retaliation.

Key facts:

  • Moscow Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said air defenses destroyed nearly 200 drones, but several broke through
  • A drone crashed into a megamall in southeast Moscow, setting it ablaze; another struck a high-rise apartment building; 17 people were injured
  • All four of Moscow’s airports suspended operations for most of the day
  • The refinery strike is part of a sustained Ukrainian campaign against Russian energy infrastructure

Western media frame the assault as a military “record.” The deeper reality is that workers and civilians on both sides are being killed and displaced to serve oligarchic and NATO interests. Russia’s promised retaliation will again fall on Ukrainian working-class neighborhoods.

4. Colombia votes in polarized presidential runoff

Colombians vote today, June 21, in a presidential runoff between far-right candidate Abelardo de la Espriella, who won the first round with 43.7%, and left-wing candidate Iván Cepeda (40.9%), running to continue President Gustavo Petro’s program. The winner takes office August 7.

Key facts:

  • A June 13 poll showed de la Espriella leading 50.9% to 43.1%, with roughly 5.9% undecided or planning to nullify
  • If Cepeda wins, he would become Colombia’s first consecutive left-wing president since the country’s 1810 independence
  • First-round turnout was 58.17%, the highest since 1998, with the lowest rate of blank/spoiled ballots in over 20 years
  • Cepeda has walked back earlier calls for a constituent assembly to rewrite the constitution, pivoting toward centrist voters

Cepeda’s abandonment of the constituent assembly — the one structural reform that might have challenged Colombia’s oligarchic constitutional order — exemplifies the dead end of left electoralism. The choice between a far-right hardliner and a social democrat who has pre-emptively capitulated before even winning office confirms that the Colombian working class cannot defend its interests through the capitalist state.

5. UK Labour crisis deepens as Burnham wins by-election

Andy Burnham, the Greater Manchester mayor, won the Makerfield by-election with 54.8% of the vote in a seat vacated specifically to give him a path into Parliament. He is now the frontrunner to replace Keir Starmer, whose government is unraveling.

Key facts:

  • Over 95 Labour MPs have called on Starmer to resign
  • The crisis sharpened in June when Defence Secretary John Healey and two others resigned over defence-spending disputes
  • Cabinet ministers including Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood, Energy Secretary Ed Miliband and Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander have reportedly urged Starmer to set a departure timetable
  • Starmer has refused to resign and says he will contest any formal leadership challenge; polls now compare his unpopularity to Liz Truss
  • Other potential successors include David Lammy, Angela Rayner and Wes Streeting

The disintegration is the latest chapter in the destruction of social democracy. Labour abandoned the working class for business-friendly austerity and rearmament, rendering itself indistinguishable from the Tories. Burnham’s “soft left” repositioning offers no break from this trajectory — it is a maneuver to refurbish a discredited apparatus.

6. France hits record-threatening heat; 35+ departments on red alert

The Île-de-France region, including Paris, entered red heatwave alert on Sunday, June 21, with at least 35 of France’s 96 departments now under red alert. Météo-France warns Monday, June 22 could become the hottest day ever recorded in France in any month, with peaks near 42°C in some areas; Sunday forecasts reached 39–41°C.

Key facts:

  • Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu announced a temporary ban on public alcohol consumption at Fête de la Musique events in affected regions; Paris parks were ordered open 24 hours
  • Communes including Brive-la-Gaillarde and Nanterre cancelled music-festival events entirely
  • The French alerts are part of a Europe-wide heatwave

The government’s response — opening parks overnight, restricting festival alcohol — is cosmetic. Workers laboring outdoors, in warehouses and in un-air-conditioned housing face the gravest danger. Years of austerity have hollowed out the public-health infrastructure that might mitigate mass heat casualties, as the 2003 French heatwave that killed some 15,000 people demonstrated.

Science, technology, and public health

  • Ebola surges in central Africa as funding collapses — the Bundibugyo outbreak passes 850 confirmed cases and ~200 deaths across DRC and Uganda; less than 10% of pledged funds delivered
  • H5N1 reaches Australia, the last continent — a dead brown skua near Esperance tests positive, marking the country’s first detection of the global panzootic strain
  • Nature publishes “transcriptomic clocks” — gene-expression models across 11,000+ transcriptomes estimate age and lifespan-modifying effects at the level of biological pathways

The Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak is now the largest Ebola epidemic on record at this stage. The DRC Ministry of Health reported 837 confirmed cases and 196 deaths as of June 15, with Uganda reporting 19 cases and two deaths; the WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on May 17. Ituri is the epicenter, with the WSWS reporting an overall case fatality rate near 23% and warning the true tally has surpassed 894 cases. There is no licensed vaccine for the Bundibugyo virus; treatment is supportive only. WHO, CDC and ECDC situation reports confirm severe undercounting of contacts. Critically, of an estimated 17,000–35,000 contacts, only about 4,000 have been traced — a collapse of surveillance capacity tied directly to the dismantling of USAID disease-monitoring networks in July 2025.

H5N1 avian influenza has reached Australia, the last continent free of the panzootic strain. The CSIRO confirmed June 20 that a brown skua — a migratory seabird found sick June 14 and later dead at Cape Le Grand National Park near Esperance, Western Australia — tested positive for H5 high-pathogenicity avian influenza. A sick giant petrel from the same region returned a suspect-positive result. No poultry detections have occurred yet. The virus has expanded its host range dramatically since 2021, infecting marine mammals, dairy cattle and causing sporadic human cases; the WHO continues to monitor for human-to-human transmission.

Other developments:

  • Aging biology: Nature (Vol. 654, June 4) published work by Alexander Tyshkovskiy and Vadim Gladyshev on “transcriptomic clocks” — models built from over 11,000 transcriptomes across 25+ tissues and four mammals that estimate chronological age, expected mortality and lifespan-modifying interventions, grouping aging into functional gene modules
  • Public-health policy: The Ebola and H5N1 crises both underscore how austerity and the gutting of global surveillance infrastructure convert containable outbreaks into mass-casualty events — a preventable consequence of the subordination of public health to private profit

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural news of the cycle is the death of American singer-songwriter and producer Oliver Tree (Oliver Tree Nickell) at 32, one of six people killed in a mid-air collision of two helicopters over Rio de Janeiro on June 14, confirmed in the days since. Tree built a substantial following through a knowing, ironic synthesis of pop, alternative rock and internet-era performance art — a persona that both satirized and depended on the viral mechanics of the streaming economy. His death at the height of a commercialized music ecosystem, in a tourist-helicopter disaster, is a reminder of how the culture industry packages even its critics. The crash, which killed six, also points to the lax safety regimes surrounding luxury tourism for the wealthy.

The cycle also brought the death of Dee Palmer, 88, the English composer and arranger long associated with Jethro Tull, whose orchestral arrangements helped define the band’s progressive-rock sound, and of James Bradley, 72, the American author of Flags of Our Fathers — the account of the Iwo Jima flag-raising and his own father’s role in it that interrogated the manufacture of patriotic myth from the realities of war. Bradley’s work, later adapted by Clint Eastwood, belongs to a tradition of confronting how the state instrumentalizes soldiers’ sacrifice for nationalist purposes.

In festival programming:

  • Frameline50, the San Francisco International LGBTQ+ Film Festival (June 17–27), marked its milestone 50th edition, selecting the Cannes-premiered Teenage Sex & Death at Camp Miasma as its closing-night film
  • The Tribeca Festival (June 3–14, New York) continued its expansion beyond film into TV premieres, live podcasts, anniversary screenings and musical performances — a trajectory reflecting the consolidation of “festival” branding into a multi-platform content marketplace

These developments unfold against a backdrop of intensifying political pressure on the arts: defunding of public cultural institutions, the targeting of pro-Palestinian artists, and the broader subordination of cultural production to the streaming oligopolies. The WSWS framework insists on distinguishing genuine artistic achievement — work that engages honestly with war, class and historical memory, as Bradley’s did — from the algorithmic content churned out to maximize engagement. The task is to recover an art that speaks to the social experience of the broad mass of working people, against both the commodification of culture and the identitarian fragmentation increasingly promoted in its place.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: No Perspective was published on June 21, 2026. Multiple independent checks — the WSWS homepage, RSS feed, monthly and recent archives, and a site search — confirm the most recent articles are dated June 20. The five articles below are from June 20.

Ebola surges in central Africa as funding collapses — Benjamin Mateus

Beyond the case data noted above, Mateus’s analysis provides the political economy of the catastrophe. He documents that despite $910 million pledged globally, less than $90 million — under 10% — has been delivered; the US contributed a “quantifiable pittance” of $23 million. He contrasts this with Elon Musk’s $180 billion wealth gain from the SpaceX IPO (7,826 times the entire US Ebola response) and global military spending of $2.887 trillion in 2025. Nine treatment centers are operating above 90% capacity. The article traces the outbreak’s severity to chronic conditions in Ituri: nearly one million displaced and over half of children under five chronically malnourished amid armed conflict. Mateus also documents community resistance — residents in Rwampara burned an Ebola treatment center, with locals explaining that international intervention is “not for us, it’s for you,” a sentiment Mateus situates within the colonial history and ongoing extraction that have stripped the region of its wealth. His political conclusion: the outbreak is a preventable product of capitalism’s monopolization of resources by the financial oligarchy.

Australia: Victorian educators reject AEU-Labor sellout — Sue Phillips

Victorian public school teachers, Education Support staff and principals rejected the Australian Education Union’s proposed four-year enterprise agreement by 57.7% to 42.3% — the first rejection of an AEU-endorsed deal in over 40 years, following a March 24 statewide strike of 35,000+ educators. Phillips details that the deal contained real pay cuts with no caps on class sizes or workload, and sharply criticizes the voting mechanism: a delegate-based system, not one-member-one-vote, in which groups of 20 members cast a single delegate vote — an “anti-democratic mechanism.” AEU president Justin Mullaly’s leadership censored critical social-media comments and intimidated questioners, and deliberately offered ES staff an inferior package to divide the workforce. The article calls for independent rank-and-file school committees organized through the International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees, demanding real wage increases, smaller class sizes and expanded planning time.

Australian government backpedals on cosmetic tax changes — Mike Head

The Albanese Labor government reversed minimal capital gains tax reforms from its May 12 budget, caving to a corporate offensive. The CGT discount eligibility threshold was expanded from $2 million to $10 million in annual turnover, allowing ~2.7 million small businesses to access a 50% discount and full exemptions after 15 years. Head cites Treasury data showing just 0.2% of tax filers earned ~60% of all net capital gains income in 2023–24. He situates the reversal in the budget’s central contradiction: marginal tax tweaks paired with $63.8 billion in NDIS cuts and $114 billion in overall spending cuts, while workers’ promised relief amounts to roughly $24 per week. The rapid capitulation, Head argues, exposes Labor’s fundamental commitment to capitalist interests.

Australia: Protester arrested in Sydney as Labor government starts demolition of public housing — Zac Hambides, Martin Scott

NSW Labor deployed about 60 police to dismantle a protest encampment at Waterloo South, arresting a 22-year-old. The camp had operated since May 24, blocking demolition of a public housing estate. The government plans to demolish 750 homes over six to nine months, displacing ~2,000 residents, including elderly and disabled people. The redeveloper is Stockland, a private firm with $18 billion in real estate assets that posted a $292 million statutory profit in January. NSW audit data show 56,000+ households on social-housing waitlists, 45% waiting over five years; the replacement plan proposes ~3,300 homes over 30 years, only a minimum 30% allocated to social housing. The authors call for working-class mobilization independent of Labor and the unions.

Free Ercan Akpolat, the Turkish mayor of the historic Prinkipo island where Trotsky lived! — Ulaş Sevinç

The WSWS demands the release of Ercan Akpolat, mayor of Büyükada (Prinkipo), detained with over 40 municipal officials on charges — bribery, extortion, document forgery, unauthorized construction approvals — that the WSWS calls a political frame-up. The article highlights Büyükada as Leon Trotsky’s place of exile from 1929–1933, where he wrote major works and first called for the Fourth International; Akpolat’s administration had since 2023 held annual commemorations and planned to restore Trotsky’s house as “an international library and museum house.” The arrests fit a systematic pattern: over 20 CHP mayors arrested since March 2025, removing officials representing 8.8 million voters. The WSWS argues this repression cannot be fought through appeals to bourgeois-democratic institutions but requires independent working-class mobilization against the Erdoğan regime.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output centers on the aftermath of the New York City Democratic primary. “The Morning After” frames Democratic primary victories as the horizon of socialist ambition, offering no critique of the Democratic Party as a capitalist institution. “NYC Socialists Are Trying to Expand Their Electoral Wins” promotes a DSA slate running in Democratic primaries as an advance for socialism, with a “socialists vs. pro-Israel oligarchs” framing that tacitly accepts the Democratic Party as the terrain of socialist politics — a classic endorsement of a bourgeois party with no independent class program. “Claire Valdez on Taking the Mamdani Coalition to Congress” completes the thread: channeling the Mamdani electoral coalition into Congressional Democratic races. The consistent line is the subordination of socialist politics to the Democratic Party’s electoral apparatus — the mechanism by which every insurgent movement in US history has been absorbed and neutralized.

Left Voice

Left Voice offers the cycle’s most consistently anti-capitalist coverage. “Rodrigo Paz Declares State of Emergency in Bolivia to Crush Protests” and its companion piece document the right-wing government’s crackdown on a 50-plus-day uprising and call for escalating international solidarity with Bolivian workers and the poor — internationalist framing in line with their FT-CI tendency, without subordination to bourgeois parties. “Die Linke Leadership Is Attempting to Silence Pro-Palestinian Voices” exposes the German reformist party’s accommodation to pro-Zionist pressure. Left Voice’s strength is its refusal of electoralist liquidation; its persistent weakness is a tendency toward movementist spontaneity and a reluctance to draw the full programmatic conclusions of the Trotskyist tradition regarding the construction of a revolutionary party.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL’s Iran coverage is openly campist. “Why Trump Failed in His Criminal War Against Iran” correctly opposes the US-Israeli assault but presents the Iranian theocratic state as essentially victorious, with no call for independent working-class action — politics reduced to anti-imperialist pressure campaigns. “The U.S.-Iran Memorandum of Understanding and the Limits of US Imperial Power” frames the war’s end as a geopolitical triumph for Iran and “multipolarity,” treating the interests of the bourgeois-clerical regime as aligned with the working class. “From Abolition to Liberation: Juneteenth” centers anti-racism but offers no critique of how reliance on Democratic politicians channels Black struggle into the two-party system — consistent with the PSL’s recent celebration of Mamdani’s primary win.

Socialist Alternative

“Trump Indicts Anti-ICE Activists — Defend the Minnesota 15!” is straightforward labor-defense and civil-liberties work, calling for union action against the federal prosecutions covered in our US section. “Trump’s Surrender — Will the Iran War Deal Hold?” frames the war’s end as a defeat for Trump and Netanyahu and calls for an independent anti-war movement, addressing the working class in both countries rather than celebrating the Iranian state — a more defensible posture than the PSL’s. The tension remains SA’s “dirty break” proximity to the Mamdani/DSA orbit, which pulls in the opposite direction from the political independence it formally advocates.

SWP UK

“Calls for Resistance at International Conference Against War in London” reports on the Stop the War Coalition’s international conference with delegates from 23 countries — anti-war in orientation but framed through cross-class broad-frontism that historically subordinates socialist politics to the lowest common denominator. “Win, Lose or Draw — Starmer Faces Defeat” criticizes Starmer’s Labour from the left without calling for a political break from it, a soft entryism that keeps the SWP orbiting the Labour left. “Anti-Racists Humiliate Tommy Robinson in Oxford” documents Stand Up To Racism counter-mobilization — the SWP’s strongest ground, though critics note it is often used to launder relationships with reformist parties.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

“Iran: Repression Provokes Backlash and Calls for a Revolutionary General Strike” correctly raises the demand for a revolutionary general strike against the Iranian regime’s repression of its own working class — a line notably superior to the PSL’s campism and consistent with Marxist internationalism. “Palestine: Britain’s Movement in Solidarity” and the RCP’s anti-Reform-UK coverage, however, are subordinated to the organization’s entry work inside the Labour Party under the banner of “fighting for a socialist Labour” — entryism into a bourgeois party that in practice means tailoring communist politics to Labour’s factional rhythms.
