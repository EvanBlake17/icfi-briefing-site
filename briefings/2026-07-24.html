1. Trump prepares “massive attack” on Iran as forces mass in Middle East

The United States is assembling a major military buildup across the Middle East and Europe for what officials describe as a “vast expansion” of the five-month war against Iran, now in its second bombing phase after a June truce collapsed. President Trump told Axios: “I am considering a massive attack. Bigger than ever before… I am close to making a decision. We are all set for it.”

According to reporting cited across the Wall Street Journal, Financial Times and Axios, special-operations forces have redeployed to the region, fighter squadrons are staged across the theater, and B-1 bombers based in the US and UK are on high alert for “major combat operations” — one B-1 has already flown a strike mission in the renewed campaign. More than 150 medics have arrived at Landstuhl Regional Medical Center in Germany, the main receiving hospital for wounded US troops, which the WSWS Perspective by Andre Damon calls “a clear indication that the Trump administration is preparing for a major increase in American casualties.”

Key facts: - A July 15 Situation Room meeting reportedly discussed seizing “Kharg Island and other territory along the Strait of Hormuz using U.S. troops,” alongside strikes on nuclear facilities at “Pickaxe Mountain.” - Retired Lt. Gen. Keith Kellogg told Fox News a ground invasion may be needed: “You’re going to need ground troops to do something.” Retired Adm. Mark Montgomery told the Financial Times that forcibly reopening the Strait could require 150–200 US strikes a night for weeks. - Trump threatened on Truth Social to destroy “ONE BRIDGE OR POWER PLANT, including those located next to, or in, the Capital City of Tehran” for any Iranian strike on shipping. Secretary of State Marco Rubio vowed disproportionate retaliation — “a head for an eye.” - The renewed bombing has killed more than 3,600 people by HRANA’s count (1,701 civilians); US/Israeli estimates run to 6,000-plus dead. Iranian officials report 35+ killed and 300+ wounded in the past week as strikes push north to Tehran.

As Damon writes, Trump’s public threat against Tehran’s civilian infrastructure amounts to “a public declaration of intent to commit war crimes under the Geneva Conventions,” and Rubio’s “a head for an eye” formula echoes the reprisal practices of the Hitler dictatorship. The Perspective frames the escalation as the product of a failing war: a January covert effort to foment regime change collapsed, leading to the February 28 assassination of Iranian leaders, which itself failed and triggered the ongoing bombing campaign.

Damon draws a direct parallel to Vietnam, quoting the 1965 Pentagon Papers memo by Assistant Secretary of Defense John McNaughton stating that 70 percent of the US purpose in escalating was “to avoid a humiliating US defeat.” Washington fears, the article argues, that a withdrawal from Iran would damage American credibility relative to China and accelerate the decline of the dollar — noting federal debt has passed 100 percent of GDP and that a survey of central banks found, for the first time, more planning to cut dollar reserves than increase them. The Perspective stresses the bipartisan character of the war: 149 House Democrats backed the $839 billion appropriations bill that funded January’s initial assault, and Democratic criticism — Senator Gary Peters branding Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth a “failure” and demanding a “strategy… to actually win this war” — is complaint about execution, not opposition. Some Democratic factions instead fret that the Iran war is diverting resources from the “more important” confrontation with Russia. Damon links the war to domestic austerity — cuts to food aid for 1.5 million children and threats to Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid — and to the cost-of-living crisis, concluding that the ruling class’s real fear is the emergence of popular opposition, since the war is being escalated with no public explanation despite consistent majority opposition in polling.

2. Oil tops $100 as Houthi strikes on Saudi tankers trigger global market rout

Brent crude closed above $100 a barrel for the first time since May after Houthi forces struck two Saudi oil tankers, the Encelia and Layla, in the Red Sea, setting the Encelia ablaze. The Yemen-based movement said it fired missiles and drones to enforce a newly declared blockade of Saudi ports; five Saudi tankers reversed course. Brent finished up $6.62 (7%) at $100.69, its highest close since May 22 and up roughly 40 percent on the month. WTI rose about 6.4% toward $92.

The oil shock detonated a synchronized global equity selloff and became the dominant cross-asset driver of the day. In Asia the damage was severe: South Korea’s KOSPI plunged 5.72% (down 406 points), tripping a sidecar circuit-breaker, with Samsung Electronics off 7.6% and SK Hynix down 8.3%. Japan’s Nikkei fell 2.69% on the combination of AI-spending fears and Middle East risk. The attacks widen a maritime front that now spans the Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz, raising the specter of a sustained disruption to Gulf oil supply.

Key facts: - US indices sold off hard Thursday: Dow -0.97% (51,711.65), S&P 500 -1.21% (7,408.30), Nasdaq -2.15% (25,137.69). - Europe followed: FTSE 100 -0.81%, DAX -0.7%; Shanghai Composite -1.4%, Hang Seng -1.0%. - ANZ’s Daniel Hynes called the Red Sea attack “a serious escalation,” warning supply tightness “is only going to worsen.” - The Houthi blockade of Saudi ports marks a qualitative expansion of the war beyond the direct US–Iran exchanges.

3. Trump imposes new tariffs on 60 trading partners

The administration imposed new Section 301 tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on some 60 trading partners, effective 12:01am ET Friday, immediately backfilling the temporary 10% worldwide tariff that expired at midnight. The new duties cover 99.4% of US trade and are justified as retaliation for partners’ alleged failure to block imports made with forced labor — the product of a March investigation by US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer under Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act.

The “forced labor” rationale provides a durable legal vehicle after the Supreme Court struck down Trump’s broader IEEPA “Liberation Day” tariffs in February. By reaching for a statute that survives judicial challenge, the administration has entrenched economic nationalism as permanent policy — a trade-war offensive that will raise consumer prices and intensify frictions with allies and rivals alike.

Key facts: - 10% duties hit Argentina, Bangladesh, Britain, Cambodia, Canada, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, India, Indonesia, Jordan, Malaysia, Mexico, Pakistan, Sri Lanka and Trinidad and Tobago. - The EU, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea and Switzerland face 10%–12.5%. - The duties took effect at 12:01am ET Friday as the temporary blanket tariff expired at the same moment. - US futures pointed higher Friday (Dow +0.4%, S&P +0.2%) even as the tariffs took effect, reflecting a bounce off Thursday’s rout.

4. Trump ties Saudi nuclear deal to Israel normalization

One day after his administration announced the signing of a civilian nuclear-energy agreement with Saudi Arabia, Trump added conditions that threw the pact into doubt, declaring it “totally subject to Saudi Arabia joining the very respected and successful Abraham Accords” and stating the deal bars uranium enrichment. Riyadh has long insisted it will not normalize relations with Israel absent a credible pathway to a Palestinian state — a position hardened by the near-total destruction of Gaza and the regional war on Iran.

The reversal, delivered via social-media posts, laid bare the transactional imperialist bargaining over the Middle East: Washington is using the nuclear deal as leverage to complete the Abraham Accords architecture and consolidate an anti-Iran bloc, while binding the Gulf’s largest US arms client more tightly to the war effort. Analysts note that both the enrichment ban and the normalization demand may prove unacceptable to Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, casting doubt on whether the agreement survives in its announced form.

Key facts: - The prior day’s announcement made no mention of the normalization precondition. - The move ties the Saudi monarchy directly to the Iran war and the destruction of Gaza, complicating its domestic and regional standing. - Saudi Arabia has repeatedly said normalization is contingent on a Palestinian state.

5. Zelensky replaces Ukraine’s top commander amid drone-war escalation

President Volodymyr Zelensky dismissed Commander-in-Chief Oleksandr Syrskyi, 60, and appointed 43-year-old Gen. Mykhailo Drapatyi to “reset” the defense against Russia. The reshuffle followed six days of protests in Kyiv — the largest wartime demonstrations in the capital — sparked by the removal of Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov, who had clashed with Syrskyi, and reflects pressure from pro-war veterans for a younger, more aggressive command.

As Clara Weiss writes on the WSWS, the change signals a Western-backed shift toward intensified long-range drone warfare — now running 300–350 strikes per day using AI-assisted “Hornet” drones linked to Eric Schmidt’s company — prioritizing infrastructure strikes deep inside Russia over other strategy. The escalation is producing rising civilian deaths on both sides, with Ukrainian civilian deaths up 37% year-over-year.

Key facts: - Syrskyi had led the armed forces since February 2024, succeeding Valerii Zaluzhnyi. - Drapatyi previously commanded ground forces and is associated with the drive to expand deep-strike operations. - The protests tested Zelensky’s authority under conditions of a grinding war of attrition and Western pressure.

6. India’s “Cockroach” youth movement defies police crackdown

Tens of thousands of young people, mobilized by the newly formed Cockroach Janata Party, defied a government march ban to converge on parliament in New Delhi on July 20, demanding the resignation of Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan over repeated leaks of the National Eligibility Entrance Test. Police fired tear gas and baton-charged the crowd; Delhi Police reported nearly 180 injured, including 118 security personnel and 60 protesters. Protesters have refused to leave a sit-in at Jantar Mantar.

What began two months ago as a satirical online campaign has grown into a nationwide challenge to Modi’s government, rooted in mass youth unemployment and inequality. As Kranti Kumara and Keith Jones write on the WSWS, the deployment of paramilitary forces under the National Security Act reflects ruling-class fear that student unrest could ignite broader working-class opposition; they warn against reliance on establishment “left” parties and call for independent working-class mobilization.

Key facts: - The exam scandal — repeated NEET paper leaks — has forced millions of students to retake grueling tests and delayed university admissions. - Common Dreams labeled the July 20 crackdown a “Black Day for Democracy.” - Movement leaders say they will continue the protest but suspend street marches over supporters’ safety concerns.

Japan’s defense minister calls to break the nuclear “taboo”

Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi said Japan “cannot avoid” a debate on acquiring nuclear weapons, a rare statement from a sitting cabinet member: “While this is a difficult issue for Japan to discuss, one topic we cannot avoid is nuclear weapons.” As Ben McGrath reports on the WSWS, Tokyo holds roughly 8,000 kg of weapons-grade plutonium — enough for about 1,600 warheads — and plans to revise its “three non-nuclear principles” (not to possess, manufacture, or allow the introduction of nuclear weapons) by year-end despite 75% public opposition. Koizumi cited France’s arsenal expansion and Finland’s lifting of a ban on NATO nuclear deployments as models. Beijing condemned the remarks. The WSWS frames the statement as serving Japanese imperialism’s regional ambitions and warns it threatens to destabilize the Indo-Pacific and raise the risk of nuclear war.

Iran war spills across the region

Beyond the direct US–Iran exchanges, the war has drawn in multiple states. Iranian strikes have hit targets in Israel, Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, while the Houthi blockade of Saudi ports has widened the maritime front in the Red Sea. Casualty trackers now place the cumulative death toll across all combatants at roughly 9,000–18,000 as of July 24, with tens of thousands wounded, the majority in Iran and Lebanon. The US has reinstated a naval blockade on Iranian oil exports, a measure with far-reaching implications for global energy markets and freedom of navigation.

House subpoenas People’s Forum and BreakThrough News over Singham funding

The House Ways and Means Committee subpoenaed three left-wing nonprofits — the People’s Forum, BreakThrough News, and Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research — over nearly $40 million that allegedly flowed from tech multimillionaire Neville Roy Singham, who relocated to Shanghai after selling his company in 2017. In letters dated July 21, the committee — which claims the groups failed to produce “a single responsive document” to earlier requests — said it is probing whether tax-exempt-organization laws adequately guard against “foreign influence” and whether Congress needs to amend those laws. Fox News and other outlets have tied the network to the Party for Socialism and Liberation (PSL).

As Patrick Martin writes on the WSWS, this is an “anticommunist witch-hunt” that — despite the WSWS’s sharp political differences with the PSL — sets a dangerous precedent for suppressing left-wing organizations by attributing domestic social unrest to foreign money rather than the class conflict inside the United States. The framing that US opposition movements are the product of foreign manipulation is a hallmark of authoritarian repression and is aimed ultimately at the entire working class.

Appeals court hands Trump a win on mandatory detention as ICE arrests hit record

A federal appeals court covering Louisiana, Mississippi and Texas stayed lower-court rulings that had freed three Texas immigrants, handing the administration a rare victory for its mandatory-detention policy — a July 2025 memo that threatens millions with imprisonment without bond if they crossed the border illegally, regardless of how long ago or whether they have applied for asylum. Separately, a California federal judge ordered ICE to remedy likely unconstitutional conditions at the Adelanto detention center. ICE arrested more people in June than in any month in its history, and July is on pace to break that record.

The rulings mark another escalation of the assault on due process and democratic rights. Four separate court challenges are testing the mandatory-detention regime, but the Fifth Circuit’s stay allows the government to continue holding immigrants without bond hearings while appeals proceed — a mechanism designed to make detention itself the punishment.

US measles hits 35-year high

US measles cases have surpassed the entire 2025 total with five months of the year remaining, reaching the highest annual count since 1991. The Johns Hopkins tracker reported 2,295 cases as of July 23; the CDC’s last official update (July 18) stood at 2,260 across 44 jurisdictions, with 93% (2,108 cases) linked to 34 outbreaks. South Carolina leads with over 600 cases in a Spartanburg-centered outbreak, followed by Utah (518) and Texas (182). The resurgence reflects collapsing vaccination rates amid the anti-science climate promoted at the highest levels of the federal government, and marks the effective loss of the elimination status the United States achieved in 2000.

White House accuses China’s Moonshot AI of distilling Anthropic’s model

OSTP Director Michael Kratsios publicly alleged on July 22–23 that China’s Moonshot AI covertly “distilled” Anthropic’s Fable model to build its 2.8-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 — released July 16 as the largest open-weight model to date — and separately obtained Nvidia GB300 chips in Thailand in defiance of export bans. Treasury has threatened sanctions. It is the first time a senior US official has named a specific Chinese lab for allegedly copying a specific named American frontier model, sharply escalating the US–China AI conflict and folding the technology rivalry into the broader economic-nationalist offensive.

Arts and culture

The most politically significant cultural event of the day was the capitulation to censorship on German public television. As Peter Schwarz details on the WSWS, ZDF management cancelled a planned anti-fascist song by pianist Igor Levit and rapper Danger Dan on the 100th edition of the satirical program Die Anstalt, denouncing the piece as “instructions for left-wing, violence-prone resistance.” The presenters offered only weak protest and ultimately endorsed state bans on far-right parties — a stance Schwarz argues is self-defeating, since the state apparatus that would enforce such bans is itself riddled with fascist sympathies. The episode is a revealing barometer of how the German cultural establishment, under conditions of rearmament and the rise of the AfD, polices even mild dissent while cloaking capitulation in appeals to the “democratic” state.

The incident belongs to a broader pattern across the imperialist countries in which cultural institutions, dependent on state funding and corporate sponsorship, retreat from artists who engage seriously with fascism, war, and social crisis. It stands in contrast to the genuine tradition of politically committed art — from Weimar cabaret to the anti-war culture of the 1960s — that the WSWS defends against both censorship and the anti-democratic instrumentalization of “anti-extremism.” Schwarz’s conclusion is that only the international mobilization of the working class on a socialist program can address the social roots from which fascism grows; the reliance on the capitalist state to ban far-right parties disarms that struggle.

Roundup: - Film: Danish writer-director Nicolas Winding Refn’s In Her Private Hell, with Sophie Thatcher as an actress arriving in a futuristic metropolis to shoot a science-fiction film, forgoes coherent narrative for stylized spectacle — the latest example of a formalism that substitutes visual surface for social content, a recurring tendency in contemporary “art” cinema. (Shepherd Express) - Obituary: Actress Kaylee Hottle, 18, known for the Deaf character Jia in the recent Godzilla films, died in a traffic collision. A Deaf performer, her casting had been a rare instance of authentic representation in a major franchise, and her death is a genuine loss. (Deaths in 2026) - Letters: The July 23 issue of The New York Review of Books surveys questions of historical memory and empire — a reminder of the persistence of serious critical writing even as the commercial publishing and film industries drift toward franchise product and AI-generated content. (NYRB)

Third Dallas-area postal worker dies in three years amid extreme heat — Tom Hall

A 2024 USPS inspector-general report documented 1,332 heat-related accidents over two years, yet management has refused route relief or air-conditioned vehicles, instead telling workers to “recognize symptoms” themselves. The article demands binding, enforceable heat-safety obligations on management rather than the current policy of shifting responsibility onto individual workers.

  • Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT): Absorbed in organizational self-promotion toward its national congress, abstracting party-building from the concrete crises of the day.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output remains dominated by the promotion of New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani as proof that “democratic socialism” can be built through the Democratic Party. Recent pieces such as “Zohran Mamdani’s 100 Days of 21st-Century Sewer Socialism” and “Zohran Mamdani and the Contradiction of Democratic Socialism” celebrate municipal administration of capitalism while conceding, almost inadvertently, that “running New York means managing capitalism.” On the war, the WSWS has documented that Jacobin and the DSA “sow complacency to demobilize opposition to war against Iran,” proposing to subordinate anti-war opposition to the “interests of imperialism” via pressure on Democratic legislators. The class content is that of the affluent upper-middle-class layer the DSA represents — a social stratum whose overriding fear is the emergence of an independent movement of the working class outside and against the Democratic Party.

Left Voice

Left Voice offers more left-sounding analysis — e.g., “How the War in Iran Is Shaking the World Economy and Global Order” and “Israel May Be the Weak Point in Trump’s Imperialist Aggression Against Iran” — and has criticized Mamdani from the left, asking “Why Is Zohran Mamdani Endorsing Strikebreaker Kathy Hochul?” and noting that “Mamdani Halts NYPD Expansion, but NYC’s Repressive Machine Remains Intact.” Yet the Morenoite PTS tradition consistently dissolves its formal criticisms into orientation toward the DSA milieu and identity-based framings (its “Bread & Roses” current), refusing the fight for a genuinely independent revolutionary party of the ICFI’s program. Its criticism of Mamdani functions as advice to the reformists — how they might govern more radically — rather than a political break with the Democratic Party as an instrument of the capitalist class.

PSL / Liberation News

The PSL is now itself the object of the House Ways and Means subpoena of the People’s Forum, BreakThrough News and Tricontinental (see United States). The organization’s campist politics — uncritical defense of China and the Iranian regime dressed as “anti-imperialism,” and its reliance on Singham-linked funding — leave it politically exposed to the “foreign influence” framing the state is deploying. As the WSWS stresses, defending the PSL against state repression is a matter of principle precisely while rejecting its nationalist, Stalinist program, which subordinates the working class to bourgeois nationalist regimes abroad and to sections of the Democratic Party at home, and offers no independent perspective.

Socialist Alternative (ISA)

Socialist Alternative’s “Middle East War: Capitalism Offers No Way Out” acknowledges over 10,000 dead and 50,000 wounded but frames the catastrophe in the abstract language of “capitalism” and generic “working-class resistance,” avoiding a concrete accounting of the role of the imperialist labor and social-democratic parties. The WSWS has separately documented the “disinterest of Australian pseudo-left Socialist Alternative in the war on Iran” — a passivity that reflects the ISA’s orientation to protest politics and its accommodation to the union and parliamentary apparatus, and its refusal to draw the revolutionary conclusions its own descriptions of the crisis imply.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP reports on July 23 protests outside the Elbit Systems arms factory in Filton, channeling genuine anti-war anger into single-issue direct action against individual weapons manufacturers. This tactic substitutes moral protest for a political struggle against the Starmer Labour government that is prosecuting British participation in the war and driving rearmament — a hallmark of the SWP’s decades-long refusal to fight for the political independence of the working class from Labour and the trade-union bureaucracy.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

The RCP’s press (communist.red) is preoccupied with organizational milestones — “Seven weeks until the RCP’s National Congress — Forward to 1,400 members!” and Central Committee reports. This party-building triumphalism proceeds largely abstracted from the concrete war and class struggles of the moment, reflecting the IMT’s characteristic combination of maximalist rhetoric with an ossified propaganda-society method that evades the strategic and programmatic questions raised by the ICFI — above all the historical struggle against Stalinism and Pabloite revisionism from which the IMT descends.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Trump ties Saudi nuclear deal to Israel normalization and bars enrichment

Trump’s abrupt addition of Abraham Accords normalization and an enrichment ban as conditions on the just-signed Saudi civilian-nuclear pact directly links the Gulf’s largest US arms client to the Iran war and the destruction of Gaza. The maneuver exposes the imperialist bargaining over the Middle East and warrants analysis of the class and geopolitical interests driving the drive to consolidate an anti-Iran bloc.

Recommended reading Washington Post Trump adds condition to nuclear deal with Saudi Arabia

2. Asian semiconductor rout wipes out billions as KOSPI trips circuit-breaker

The 5.72% KOSPI crash, with Samsung down 7.6% and SK Hynix 8.3%, and the parallel Nikkei plunge point to the vulnerability of the AI-driven bubble and the exposure of hundreds of thousands of electronics and semiconductor workers across South Korea, Japan and Taiwan to mass layoffs. This deserves coverage connecting the financial turmoil to the global working class and the war-driven disruption of supply chains.

Recommended reading Seoul Economic Daily KOSPI closes down 406.27 points

3. Appeals court revives Trump’s mandatory-detention regime as ICE breaks arrest records

The Fifth Circuit’s stay of orders freeing three Texas immigrants, alongside record ICE arrests and the Adelanto conditions ruling, marks a significant escalation of the assault on democratic rights and due process. This is a core WSWS campaign area and merits a detailed report on the legal architecture of mass detention and the collusion of both parties in building it.

Recommended reading Stateline Trump administration claims court victory in mandatory detention

4. Tariffs on 60 economies weaponize “forced labor” pretext to cover 99.4% of US trade

The new Section 301 tariffs, imposed under a “forced labor” rationale after the Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs, represent a durable escalation of economic nationalism and trade war. The WSWS should analyze how the “human rights” framing masks an offensive against the international working class and the interests of American finance capital behind it.

Recommended reading NBC News U.S. sets new tariffs at 10% to 12.5% on 60 trade partners

5. Randomized trial deflates hopes for a repurposed gonorrhea vaccine

The NEJM finding that 4CMenB does not reduce gonorrhea incidence redirects a major public-health strategy amid rising antibiotic-resistant infections. A WSWS science report could situate this within the broader crisis of infectious-disease control under conditions of austerity and the deprioritization of public health.

Recommended reading NEJM Current issue, July 23, 2026

6. Kaylee Hottle, 18, Deaf actress of the Godzilla films, killed in traffic collision

The death of the young Deaf performer, whose casting brought authentic representation to a major franchise, is a cultural loss warranting a brief obituary. It also raises the question of the treatment of disabled artists in a commercial film industry increasingly hostile to genuine human content.
