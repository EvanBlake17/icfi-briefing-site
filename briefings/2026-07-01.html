1. Supreme Court upholds birthright citizenship, rejecting Trump order

The US Supreme Court on June 30 struck down President Trump’s first-day executive order seeking to end birthright citizenship, ruling 6-3 in Trump v. Barbara that the 14th Amendment guarantees citizenship to virtually all children born on US soil. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the majority opinion, grounding the decision in the Citizenship Clause and the 1898 precedent United States v. Wong Kim Ark, and declaring that “the Framers of the Fourteenth Amendment extended that promise to ‘every free-born person in this land.’ We keep that promise today.”

Notably, only Justice Amy Coney Barrett joined Roberts from the court’s conservative wing; the three liberal justices supplied the remaining votes. Justices Thomas, Alito and Gorsuch dissented. Kavanaugh’s concurrence left the door open to future congressional restriction — a warning the WSWS’s John Burton emphasized, noting the ruling is a narrow and fragile defense of a democratic right the administration remains committed to destroying by other means.

Key facts: - The order, issued in January 2025, targeted children of undocumented parents and of parents on temporary visas. - The children were held to be “subject to the jurisdiction” of the US and thus citizens at birth. - Roberts assembled his majority with only one conservative colleague, underscoring how isolated the defense of the 14th Amendment has become on the court. - The ruling is one of several end-of-term decisions; ahead of it, Trump publicly attacked his own Supreme Court appointees, and the White House signaled it will seek congressional and administrative routes to curtail birthright citizenship despite the defeat.

2. Supreme Court strikes campaign finance limits, upholds transgender athlete bans and expands presidential power

In the same end-of-term flurry, the court issued a series of decisions that further entrench oligarchic power. On June 30, in NRSC v. FEC, the court struck down 6-3 the 1974 caps on coordinated spending by political parties, overturning a 2001 precedent and extending the logic of Citizens United. Kavanaugh wrote the majority; Kagan warned in dissent that “with no limits on coordinated expenditures, the party can serve as the candidate’s checking account.” CNN and CNBC noted the ruling delivers a major boost to Republicans ahead of the midterms.

The same day, the court upheld 6-3 state bans on transgender athletes in girls’ and women’s sports, reviewing the West Virginia (B.P.J.) and Idaho cases. Kavanaugh’s majority opinion held that schools may base eligibility on biological sex under Title IX and the Equal Protection Clause, validating bans now in 27 states. Days earlier, in Trump v. Slaughter, the court overturned the 1935 Humphrey’s Executor precedent, allowing the president to fire officials of independent agencies at will while shielding the Federal Reserve through a separate 5-4 ruling. Sotomayor warned the majority had granted power “unknown even to the English Crown.”

WSWS writers Ed Hightower and John Burton situated the rulings as components of a coordinated drive to concentrate executive power, protect finance capital and scapegoat vulnerable minorities amid deepening social crisis.

3. Venezuela earthquake toll passes 1,700 as US deportees killed in collapsed hotel

Twin once-in-a-century earthquakes that struck Venezuela’s coast on June 24 have killed more than 1,700 people, with thousands still missing, the UN reported. Among the dead are Venezuelans deported from the United States hours before the quakes struck. On June 24, 146 Venezuelans were flown from Texas to Caracas and taken to the Hotel Santuario in La Guaira, a coastal city devastated by the tremors. The hotel collapsed; families searching hospitals, shelters and morgues have been unable to locate many of their relatives.

Key facts: - The WSWS’s Andrea Lobo reported that 135 of 147 deportees died when their detention hotel was destroyed roughly six hours after arrival. - The Venezuelan agency responsible for transporting deportees declined to say how many survived. - The WSWS links the scale of devastation to years of US sanctions that gutted Venezuela’s construction and emergency-response infrastructure. - UN agencies are scaling up relief as aftershocks continue to strike the Caracas region; the world body reported the toll passing 1,700 with thousands still missing. - The disaster underscores the human cost of the US “maximum pressure” sanctions regime, which the WSWS argues left Venezuela’s infrastructure and emergency services unable to withstand the quakes.

4. More than 1,000 Palestinians killed since Gaza “ceasefire” announced

Israeli operations have killed more than 1,000 Palestinians in Gaza since the ceasefire announced by Trump in October 2025, according to Gaza’s Health Ministry, pushing the total death toll of the war past 73,000. The enclave has endured near-daily air strikes, shelling and gunfire along the dividing line separating Israeli- and Palestinian-controlled zones. Gaza’s Government Media Office documented at least 3,465 Israeli ceasefire violations between October 10, 2025 and June 29, 2026.

The WSWS’s Kevin Reed reported that children were among those killed in a designated “safe area,” and that Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich has confirmed new settlement plans in northern Gaza. Reed frames the “ceasefire” as political cover for the continued prosecution of genocide.

Key facts: - UN human rights chief Volker Türk has repeatedly warned that Palestinians across Gaza remain unsafe six months after the ceasefire announcement. - The 73,000 figure is a documented undercount; thousands remain buried under rubble. - Al Jazeera and PBS corroborate the ministry’s casualty reporting. - Reed argues the deal announced by Trump functions as diplomatic cover, indicting Washington as complicit in the continuing killing rather than a broker of peace.

5. NATO heads toward Ankara summit as Ukraine war escalates toward direct NATO-Russia conflict

NATO’s 32 members will meet July 7-8 in Ankara, where allies will receive their “first report card” since committing to spend 5% of GDP on defense by 2035 (3.5% core plus 1.5% supporting). Secretary General Mark Rutte said the alliance will announce tens of billions of dollars in new defense contracts. In 2025 European allies and Canada raised military spending by 20% year-on-year; for the first time, Norway surpassed the US in per-capita defense spending. Trump and Zelensky, the latter as an invited guest, are expected to attend.

The WSWS Perspective by Johannes Stern warns the summit will formalize an “extraordinarily dangerous new stage” of the war, in which the line between proxy war and direct NATO-Russia conflict “is being systematically erased.” Ukraine’s long-range strikes on Russian energy and military-industrial sites depend on NATO intelligence, satellite surveillance and targeting data — “the logic of provocation… the logic that leads to world war.”

Key facts: - Rutte’s “NATO 3.0” concept envisions Europe assuming primary war-fighting responsibility “backed by American power.” - Finland’s parliament has repealed its nuclear-weapons ban; Macron has proposed extending the French nuclear umbrella across Europe. - Germany plans to permanently station 5,000 troops near Russia and Belarus by 2027; Stern highlights Defence Minister Boris Pistorius’s June 22 visit to Lithuania — the 85th anniversary of Operation Barbarossa — as a revival of Germany’s historic “Drang nach Osten.” - Citing Mark Galeotti in the Times of London (“If Putin fears he is losing Crimea, there’s no telling what he’ll do”), Stern warns Western pressure calculated to break Moscow “could at the same time trigger a wider war,” listing triggers from a strike on a NATO logistics hub to a Baltic or Black Sea naval clash.

6. Record US heat dome threatens 162 million as three firefighters die in Colorado

A record-setting heat dome is spreading across the central and eastern US, placing around 162 million people across 35 states under heat alerts as the country enters the July 4 holiday week. CNN reported that temperatures in New York City and Washington could be the hottest in over a decade. More than 50 large fires are burning nationally, covering over 460,000 acres, with the National Interagency Fire Center warning of “extremely critical fire weather” rare for late June.

Three firefighters were killed in a burnover while battling the Knowles Fire in western Colorado: Emily Barker, 38; Nick Hutcherson, 27; and Sydney Watson, 27. Two others were injured. The WSWS’s Jacob Crosse tied the deaths to the erosion of worker safety, citing USPS heat-injury data and a 35% Cal/OSHA inspector vacancy rate as evidence that profit is prioritized over life.

7. Poland strips Zelensky of top honor as Ukraine Recovery Conference opens without him

Polish President Karol Nawrocki revoked Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky’s Order of the White Eagle — Poland’s highest state honor — on June 19, after Zelensky awarded a Ukrainian special-operations unit the honorary title “Heroes of the UPA.” The Ukrainian Insurgent Army is associated in Poland with the Volhynia massacre, in which Ukrainian nationalists killed tens of thousands of Poles during World War II. Zelensky returned the decoration and skipped the “Ukraine Recovery Conference” in Gdansk, sending Prime Minister Yulia Svyrydenko in his place. The conference nonetheless produced $11.7 billion in agreements.

The WSWS’s Jason Melanovski frames the rift as a clash between two oligarchic nationalist regimes, noting Ukraine’s glorification of the OUN/UPA — blamed for killing roughly 100,000 ethnic Poles in 1943-45 — and Poland’s own reactionary nationalism.

Six people — four women and two men, all child-welfare workers — were killed in a shooting at a youth welfare center in Stade, Lower Saxony, on June 29. The arrested suspect is a 45-year-old man, a German-born Turkish citizen with a prior record for making threats and no firearm license. He had arrived for a scheduled appointment over a custody dispute concerning his three-month-old daughter; the child and her mother were present but unharmed. The WSWS’s Peter Schwarz situated the attack alongside prior German, Austrian and US mass shootings as a symptom of social breakdown under austerity and militarization, warning against far-right efforts to scapegoat migrants.

US-Iran framework reopens Strait of Hormuz and extends ceasefire

The US and Iran signed a 14-point memorandum of understanding in mid-June to extend a 60-day ceasefire, including in Lebanon, and reopen the Strait of Hormuz to commercial shipping. The framework — described by mediators as a “road map” rather than a final deal — provides for talks on down-blending Iran’s highly enriched uranium, the return of IAEA inspectors, sanctions relief, and the possible release of up to $25 billion in frozen Iranian assets contingent on compliance. Vice President JD Vance was involved in the negotiations. The deal has fueled a fierce debate in Washington over US concessions, with Trump hitting back at critics.

Europe’s record heatwave kills more than 1,300

A record-breaking heatwave gripped much of Europe in late June, shattering temperature records in at least a dozen countries. France recorded its hottest day ever on June 24, with a national average of 30.0°C and 43.8°C locally; Spain set new June station records above 40°C. More than 1,300 excess deaths have been linked to the heat since June 21 — roughly 1,000 in France and 327 in Spain, the majority among people over 65. Scientists attribute the intensity to fossil-fuel-driven climate change and an “omega block” trapping North African air over the continent. In an analysis, the WSWS’s Thomas Scripps cited up to $600 billion in projected costs by 2030 against $916 billion in fossil-fuel profits captured in 2022, concluding that capitalism cannot solve the climate crisis.

BMA narrowly pushes through UK resident doctors’ pay deal

Resident (junior) doctors in England voted by almost 53% on a 57% turnout to accept the Starmer government’s pay offer, ending the dispute after the BMA cancelled a nationwide strike at the eleventh hour on June 13. Nearly 33,000 doctors voted. The deal delivers an average 6.6% uplift fully phased in by April 2027 (a 3.5% DDRB award backdated to April 2026 plus a further ~3.1%). The WSWS’s Tony Robson argued the deal fails to reverse a 21% real-terms pay cut since 2008 and leaves intact the NHS crisis — a 7.2 million-strong waiting list and continued outsourcing to private providers — characterizing the BMA leadership’s move as a sellout on behalf of the Labour government.

Police break picket lines in six-week Ontario community services strike

About 4,500 Ontario community and social services workers, represented by OPSEU/SEFPO across 27 locals, entered their sixth week on strike in June, facing police mobilization and scab labor. The workers — the lowest-paid public-sector workers in the province, now earning less in real terms than in 2018 — staff autism programs, palliative care, psychiatric centers, shelters and crisis units. They are demanding wages owed after Bill 124’s unconstitutional wage suppression and an end to underfunding that has left 70,000 autistic children on waiting lists amid a $1.5 billion ministry shortfall. On June 23, police were deployed at a London hotel where management billeted replacement workers. The WSWS’s Carl Bronski framed the confrontation as class-war austerity backed by the state.

BIS warns AI spending boom threatens a “bust”

The Bank for International Settlements, in its flagship annual report released June 28, warned that the AI investment frenzy could collapse into a “prolonged investment collapse” endangering global financial stability. The WSWS’s Nick Beams argued the report exposes capitalism’s inability to rationally direct AI’s productive potential, citing the 10.5% KOSPI plunge and $400 billion SpaceX value loss post-IPO as tremors of the coming reckoning.

Teamsters and DOJ move to end 37-year federal oversight of union

The International Brotherhood of Teamsters and the US Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of New York jointly filed a motion on June 17 to end the 37-year consent-decree monitorship imposed in 1989 to eliminate organized-crime influence. If approved by Judge Loretta Preska, the independent monitor position would be eliminated within three years. General President Sean O’Brien said the union can “police their own.” The WSWS’s Alex Findijs noted the filing came shortly after the uncontested re-election of O’Brien and Fred Zuckerman, and amid mass UPS layoffs — arguing the end of oversight strengthens an unaccountable apparatus against the rank and file.

Texas mandates Bible stories for more than 5 million public school students

The Texas State Board of Education voted 9-5 on June 26 to require Bible verses and stories — including Adam and Eve, the Beatitudes and the Parable of the Prodigal Son — in the K-12 English and literature curriculum, affecting more than 5 million students, with rollout beginning in 2030. The board simultaneously approved a social-studies rewrite emphasizing Texas and US history while deemphasizing global history and cultures, including references to Islamic contributions to mathematics and astronomy. The WSWS (Andy Hartmann, Jacob Crosse) tied the vote to SB 10’s Ten Commandments mandate and to over 22,000 book challenges since 2021, describing a Christian-nationalist offensive serving ruling-class interests.

US measles cases pass 2,100; elimination status effectively lost

The CDC has confirmed 2,134 measles cases across 41 jurisdictions as of June 25, with 93% of cases in unvaccinated or unknown-status individuals and 93% tied to 30 active outbreaks. The US is on pace to exceed 2025’s 2,288-case record. Public health experts, writing in a CIDRAP op-ed, now argue the US has in substance lost its measles elimination status — following the formal loss of that status for the Americas region in November 2025. The collapse tracks with sustained anti-vaccine policy and the gutting of public-health infrastructure.

Large JAMA study finds no link between prenatal Tylenol and autism/ADHD

A University of Hong Kong sibling-comparison study of more than 700,000 mother-child pairs (2001-2023), published June 29 in JAMA Internal Medicine, found no association between prenatal acetaminophen (Tylenol) exposure and either autism or ADHD, regardless of trimester, dose or frequency. It is one of the largest such studies to date and reinforces prior Swedish and Japanese research refuting the claim — which gained political traction in the US in 2025.

Arts and culture

The WSWS marked a significant career retrospective this week with Kevin Reed’s survey of Peter Gabriel, “From progressive rock to world music and beyond.” The piece traces Gabriel’s trajectory from his years fronting Genesis, through the commercial peak of So (1986), his founding of the WOMAD festival and Real World label, and up to his forthcoming eleventh studio album, o/i, due throughout 2026. Following the model of 2023’s I/O, each track receives both “Bright-Side” and “Dark-Side” mixes released in step with the lunar cycle; the first single, “Been Undone,” appeared on the January “wolf moon.” Reed foregrounds Gabriel’s political engagement — the 1980 anti-apartheid anthem “Biko,” his human-rights activism, and his recent public condemnation of “Israel’s genocidal violence in Gaza.”

Gabriel’s career offers a case study in the WSWS’s approach to culture: the impulse toward internationalism and social conscience in his best work (the collaborations across African, Middle Eastern and South Asian traditions on WOMAD stages) stands as a rebuke to the nationalism and provincialism now being cultivated across official culture. That an artist of Gabriel’s stature has publicly named the Gaza genocide — at a moment when many cultural figures maintain silence for fear of blacklisting — is itself notable.

Roundup: - Cultural censorship in Texas: The State Board of Education’s mandate of Bible stories and its simultaneous purge of global-history content (covered above) belongs equally to the cultural sphere — part of a broader assault that includes more than 22,000 book challenges in Texas since 2021. This is the systematic narrowing of what may be taught, read and imagined. - Historiography: The WSWS published a letter from historian Steven Mintz praising a recent WSWS webinar’s characterization of the American Revolution as a bourgeois revolution, contrasting the Marxist method favorably with academic historiography’s turn toward discourse and identity. The exchange points to a live struggle over historical memory — whether the revolutionary content of the past is preserved or dissolved into fragmented “narratives.” - Music-industry note: Gabriel’s lunar-cycle release strategy — bypassing conventional album-cycle marketing — reflects both the creative possibilities and the commercial pressures of a streaming economy in which even established artists must engineer novelty to hold attention.

The through-line across these items is the contest over culture as a terrain of political struggle: the ruling class works to impose religious and nationalist orthodoxy on public education while independent artists who engage honestly with war and oppression face marginalization.

This week in history: the 1929-1930 miners’ lockout

John Harris and Peter Symonds recount the 15-month lockout of roughly 10,000 New South Wales coal miners beginning in 1929, a defining experience of the Australian working class. Mine owners, backed by the Bruce-Page conservative government, sought to slash wages and impose the “dilution” of labor. When miners resisted, they were locked out. On December 16, 1929, police opened fire on miners at the Rothbury colliery, killing 29-year-old Norman Brown and wounding many others. The Scullin federal Labor government, elected weeks earlier, betrayed the miners — refusing to defend them and ultimately presiding over a settlement on the owners’ terms. The article also critically examines the Communist Party of Australia, whose sectarian “Third Period” line under Comintern direction — denouncing all reformists as “social fascists” — disoriented militant workers at a decisive moment. The lockout’s lessons concern the treachery of Labor and the necessity of an independent revolutionary leadership.

The anti-Marxist content lies precisely in the framing of a capitalist state administration as an instrument of working-class power. By presenting the management of New York’s austerity budget as “socialism,” Jacobin obscures the class character of the state and channels the radicalization of workers and youth back into the Democratic Party — the very mechanism the WSWS’s Kishore identifies.
