1. Federal agents search Ilhan Omar’s home 22 days before the midterms

Early Monday, October 12, agents of Homeland Security Investigations (HSI), the criminal investigative arm of ICE, searched the Washington, DC, home of Rep. Ilhan Omar (D-Minnesota) under a warrant obtained by the Justice Department. AP sources describe it as a criminal investigation, and reports say it concerns immigration paperwork filed by her late father when Omar was a child. Omar, 44, came to the US as a child refugee from Somalia and has been a citizen since 2000. She has not been charged. The raid broke the Justice Department’s practice of avoiding overt action against candidates within 60 days of an election. Omar called it political targeting and said she would not “be silenced.” Supporters rallied for her in Minneapolis Monday night.

The search followed days of incitement from the White House. On October 8 Trump posted that Minnesota was being “taken over by Somali Immigrants,” and on October 10 he attacked Omar and Somalis at a Tennessee rally. As Patrick Martin wrote in today’s WSWS Perspective, the raid is “a new step in Trump’s drive toward dictatorship,” and its voter-fraud pretexts lay the groundwork for interfering in the November 3 vote itself.

Key facts: - Blanche knew: CBS News reports Attorney General Todd Blanche was personally aware of the search in advance. Trump said he “had nothing to do with” it and called Omar “a disaster.” - Deportation threat: DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin repeated the far-right smear that Omar married her brother and said she could be deported “if we can prove it.” “Border czar” Tom Homan said no one is above the law, “regardless if they’re a congressman or not.” - Democratic response: House Democratic leaders Hakeem Jeffries, Katherine Clark and Pete Aguilar issued a joint statement calling the raid “deeply disturbing.” - The pattern: MS NOW counts Omar among a growing list of Democrats under investigation by the administration, alongside New York Attorney General Letitia James, Fed Governor Lisa Cook and Rep. LaMonica McIver.

2. Riyadh airport stays shut until October 17 as Houthi strikes kill 20 in a week

Saudi Arabia’s General Authority of Civil Aviation extended the closure of King Khalid International Airport through October 17. Houthi missile and drone attacks on Saudi airports have now killed at least 20 people in a week, among them a US citizen. The Saturday strike on Riyadh’s Terminal 3 is now put at 13 dead and more than 300 wounded. A second strike on the airport Sunday killed one person, a Pakistani national, and injured 11. On Monday the Houthis claimed another ballistic missile attack on the capital’s airport. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a missile over Riyadh, and CNN reported three missiles intercepted. Saudi authorities are investigating why air defenses failed to stop Saturday’s strike, and Hindustan Times reports the kingdom is running short of interceptor missiles.

Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman said “our response must be equally serious.” Houthi official Mohammed Al Bukhaiti warned of a “swift and painful response” if the US joins the Saudi campaign, and the movement says “the worst is yet to come.” Inside Yemen the war is intensifying on several fronts.

Key facts: - Dammam hit: The US embassy urged “extreme caution” at all major Saudi airports, citing an October 11 attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam. It also said American citizens had been detained for photographing the aftermath. - Business as usual: Delta postponed its Atlanta–Riyadh nonstop from this month to March 2027. Riyadh says its energy and investment conferences will go ahead regardless. - Yemen: Military sources report that fighting in Taiz killed more than 60 people in 24 hours. Houthi-run health officials say airstrikes on Sanaa attributed to Saudi Arabia killed one person and wounded 12. - Oil: Brent topped $105 Monday, a two-week high. It then eased in Asian trading (see World economy).

3. Russia hits Ukrainian energy sites despite Trump’s energy ceasefire as EU imposes record sanctions

On October 11 Trump posted on Truth Social that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an “energy ceasefire,” “effective immediately.” He gave no terms, and Moscow did not confirm. Zelensky told reporters it was “news to me.” Russian strikes hit a Kharkiv power plant within hours, and the BBC reported blackouts across Ukraine on Monday. Zelensky said Russia attacked six energy facilities Monday and that Moscow does not appear willing to accept the arrangement. He offered to halt Ukraine’s drone campaign against Russian refineries only if Russia stopped attacking the power grid: “We have one refinery.” Trump insisted the ceasefire still holds and lashed out at critics of his deal to resume buying Russian diesel. A bipartisan group of six senators has urged him to reverse it.

The European Union responded the same day by escalating. EU foreign ministers adopted the largest single sanctions package in the bloc’s history: 1,569 listings, covering 826 Russian companies and 743 executives in missile, drone, aircraft, semiconductor, radar and ammunition supply chains. Major shipyards, including submarine builders, are among them. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas publicly urged Trump to impose new sanctions on Russia rather than strike diesel deals with Moscow. As Johannes Stern wrote on the WSWS, the gap between Washington and Brussels reflects competing imperialist interests in Ukraine, not any division between peace and war.

Key facts: - Scale: The EU says the newly listed firms generate at least €20 billion a year and employ more than 250,000 people. EU sanctions lists now cover almost 4,600 individuals and entities. - Kallas: “This is not the time to reduce pressure on Moscow, but to increase it.” German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul called it an “unprecedented sanctions strike against Putin’s war economy.” - NYT framing: The New York Times headline reads “Trump Gives Putin a Hand in Russia’s Economic War With Ukraine.” It reflects the section of the US establishment that opposes the diesel deal.

4. Iran rejects US reply on Hormuz as Revolutionary Guard claims strikes on three supertankers

Iran’s top security official said Sunday that the US response to Tehran’s mid-September proposal “did not sufficiently address” its seven conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz and returning to negotiations. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei said Monday that Iran is still reviewing the reply and will answer through intermediaries, with Qatar and Pakistan among them. The dispute is over sequencing. Washington insists the nuclear programme be addressed first, while Tehran wants talks to begin with reopening the Strait and restoring security there. The Revolutionary Guard (IRGC) claimed it struck three supertankers in the Strait over two days.

The war has moved further onto British soil. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said an incident at RAF Fairford, the base used by US heavy bombers, was “highly likely to have been coordinated in Iran.” Seven men were arrested and later released on bail, and Iran denies any involvement.

Key facts: - War timeline: The war began with US and Israeli strikes on February 28. A June interim ceasefire collapsed and on-off strikes resumed. Before the war, about a fifth of global oil and LNG trade moved through the Strait. - Coordinated pressure: The foreign ministers of the US, UK, Australia, Canada and 23 other countries issued a joint statement condemning Iranian “hostile activities” abroad. The UK sanctioned two Iranian nationals. - On Yemen: Baghaei offered Iranian help to settle the Saudi–Houthi conflict: “The issue of Yemen should have been resolved a long time ago with the (UN) roadmap.” - Repression inside Iran: Authorities arrested lawyer Amirsalar Davoudi on Sunday.

5. Delhi police file cases against opposition marchers and detain youth protest leaders

On Monday, October 12, opposition INDIA bloc parties defied a security lockdown and marched to the Supreme Court in New Delhi. They demand the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar over the Election Commission’s revision of the voter rolls. Ahead of the march, authorities closed six metro stations and restricted traffic in the capital. By Tuesday, Delhi Police had registered four First Information Reports (FIRs), formal criminal complaints, against the protest. They cite BNSS Section 163, the successor to the colonial-era Section 144 power to ban public gatherings. Also on Tuesday, police detained leaders of the youth-based “Cockroach Janta Party” after they slipped back into the barricaded protest ground at Jantar Mantar.

The Supreme Court bench led by Chief Justice Surya Kant issued notices to the government, the Election Commission and Kumar on October 5, and a further hearing is expected this week. The Commission says the revision targets fraudulently registered voters and “foreign illegal immigrants.” Critics say it disproportionately strikes Muslims and the poor from the rolls. The WSWS’s Kranti Kumara described the response as “massive repression to crush protests,” a turn by the Modi government toward open police-state methods against mass opposition.

Key facts: - Criminal cases: Two of the four FIRs were filed at Tilak Marg police station, according to India TV. - Youth movement targeted: CNA reports the Cockroach leaders were detained after “entering [a] barricaded Delhi protest site.” Its founder was among the more than 2,000 detained earlier in the crackdown. - Opposition line: Opposition parties argue the revision benefits Modi’s BJP (Bharatiya Janata Party). They have confined their campaign to the courts and to demands for an official’s resignation.

6. Federal judge orders Trump to cancel taxpayer-funded campaign ads

US District Judge Jennifer L. Rochon in New York issued a temporary restraining order Sunday night, October 11, effective 6:45 p.m. ET for two weeks. It requires the administration to cancel all remaining TV spots in a five-ad campaign promoting Trump and bars officials from entering into, renewing or expanding any agreement for them. The plaintiffs are a coalition led by Common Cause, represented by Democracy Forward. They argue the ads illegally divert appropriated funds into political publicity three weeks before the midterms. The DNC is suing separately in Washington, where Judge Loren AliKhan has set a hearing for Thursday. The Washington Post reports the administration blew past an earlier deadline to stop running the ads.

The spending figures show what amounts to an executive-branch campaign operation paid for with public money: - Total: $21,705,138 by Monday morning, per AdImpact. It may be an undercount, because the ads were classified as public service announcements. - By ad: “Final Battle” cost $13 million, “Maduro” $3.1 million, “Celebrating The Enduring Spirit” $3.1 million, “The Country He Loves” $1.4 million and “To The Great Proud People of Iran” $1.1 million. - Funding: DHS allotted $20 million for a “National Media Campaign” and awarded it to the Maryland firm LMD Agency on September 20. A day earlier, the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) granted $20 million to Customs and Border Protection for “OBBB Commemorative Events.” - Administration defense: DHS official Holly Mehringer told the court the money came from funds for “commemorating efforts and events related to border security.” The Justice Department argued an order was unnecessary because the campaign would end the next day. Trump has said his political committee will pay for the ads, but a White House official said there are no plans to reimburse the money already spent.

7. NATO opens Steadfast Noon nuclear drill as its commander unveils a theory of victory over Russia

NATO’s annual nuclear-strike exercise, Steadfast Noon, began Tuesday, October 13, over Italy with 17 participating countries. The alliance says no live weapons are used. The drill rehearses the delivery of US B61 nuclear bombs stored in Europe by the dual-capable aircraft of allied air forces. Germany has sent three Tornado fighter-bombers, and Sweden is deploying JAS 39 Gripens. AP reports that senior alliance officials confirmed the exercise would go ahead despite Russia’s late-September nuclear warning. Moscow had threatened to use its full arsenal if NATO blockaded the Kaliningrad exclave.

On Monday, at the Warsaw Security Forum, NATO’s Supreme Allied Commander Europe, US General Alexus Grynkewich, presented what the WSWS reports is a three-stage “Theory of Victory” against Russia: “block,” “blunt” and “break.” The exercise comes with the US–Russian New START treaty expired since February, leaving no binding limits on either side’s strategic arsenal for the first time in half a century.

Key facts: - Opposition: Italian peace group PeaceLink has published the airspace notice for the drill and is circulating a call against it. - Arms race: France tested its M51.3 submarine-launched ballistic missile on October 6, days after the Russian warning.

Latin America

Escondida supervisors set to strike as Centinela walkout enters its second week

At BHP’s Escondida mine in Chile, the world’s largest copper mine, Union No. 2 of Supervisors and Staff (1,020 members) rejected the company’s final offer by 94.8 percent. Mediation ended without agreement, and the union has announced a strike for Wednesday, October 14.   • Deaths: Escondida halted operations in late September after a fatal accident. Chilean mine deaths this year stand at 11, after 20 in 2025.

Ebola epidemic in Congo outpaces West Africa as Kenya traces contacts

The Bundibugyo-strain Ebola epidemic in the Democratic Republic of Congo, declared May 15, reached 9,000 confirmed cases and 4,339 confirmed deaths by October 11, its 149th day. That is a confirmed case-fatality ratio of 48.2%. Seven provinces covering about 679,000 square kilometres are affected, an area larger than Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone combined. North Kivu now accounts for nearly 40% of new confirmed cases, up from 24% at the end of August. Al Jazeera reports the virus is still spreading in the east.

This week in history: October 12–18

25 years ago (2001): Protests against the US invasion of Afghanistan. From October 13, a week after the US bombing began, protests spread across Europe and Australia. Berlin drew 20,000–30,000, London 20,000–50,000 at a march organized by the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, Stuttgart 15,000 and Bern about 5,000, while 30,000 joined Italy’s Perugia–Assisi march. Sydney’s 3,000 was the largest in Australia. The WSWS noted at the time that the organizers were pro-capitalist and that union leaderships refused to call strikes, so organized workers were largely absent. Opposition to the “war on terror” was thereby confined to protest from the outset.

50 years ago (1976): Canada’s National Day of Protest. On October 14, 1976, more than 1.2 million workers struck for 24 hours against Pierre Trudeau’s Anti-Inflation Act of 1975. The act’s Anti-Inflation Board capped wage increases at 8, 6 and 4 percent over three years, while price controls went unenforced. Auto plants, steel mills, mines, ports and public services shut down, with Quebec’s three federations joining English Canadian workers. It was the largest stoppage in Canadian history and the first general strike since Winnipeg in 1919. The Canadian Labour Congress had authorized a general strike in May but reduced it to a single day of protest, and the controls stayed in force until 1978.

75 years ago (1951): Assassination of Liaquat Ali Khan. Pakistan’s first prime minister, a close lieutenant of Jinnah, was shot twice in the chest on October 16, 1951, while addressing about 100,000 people at Company Bagh in Rawalpindi. Police killed the assassin, the Afghan national Sayyid Akbar, on the spot, and his motives and any accomplices were never established. Khan had tied Pakistan to Washington in the Cold War. His successor, Khwaja Nazimuddin, presided over communal incitement and a crackdown on Bengali protesters. The killing came four years after a Partition that left up to two million dead and displaced 12 to 14 million.

100 years ago (1926): The Joint Opposition’s October 16 statement. Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Pyatakov, Sokolnikov and Evdokimov signed a statement renouncing factional activity, which Pravda published the next day. The Opposition had taken its platform directly to factory cells in Moscow and Leningrad and had been met by Stalinist “fighting detachments” and the OGPU. Under threat of expulsion under the 1921 ban on factions, it made a tactical retreat so it could remain in the party and continue the fight for its program. Trotsky wrote that the Stalinists broke the truce the day after it was made, but the Opposition gained time to prepare for the struggle over the 15th Party Conference.
