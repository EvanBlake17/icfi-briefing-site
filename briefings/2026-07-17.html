1. US weighs ground troops in Iran as war enters a sixth night

The US-Iran war entered its sixth consecutive night of strikes, with the White House now openly discussing a ground campaign. According to the Wall Street Journal, administration officials have met to weigh sending ground forces into Iran and seizing Iranian islands near the Strait of Hormuz. Overnight US strikes killed 8 people and wounded 20 in southern and western Iran, hitting six bridges in Hormozgan province, airport infrastructure, and Chabahar port.

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards claimed they “destroyed several US refueling aircraft and fighter jets” in retaliatory strikes on US assets in Jordan and at Bahrain’s Sakhir air base. Qatar intercepted a missile Friday morning — a child was injured by falling shrapnel — while Bahrain said it destroyed “hostile Iranian aerial attacks” it branded “cowardly attacks targeting civilians.” Ceasefire talks have collapsed, and a regional expert warned the two sides risk a “forever war.”

As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, the pivot from air strikes to a potential invasion reflects the failure of the bombing campaign, quoting Trump’s own framing: “Sometimes you need a ground campaign, but we have other people that will do the ground campaign for us.” A US official described the strikes as “shaping operations” to “set the stage, if needed.”

The war has now killed and wounded civilians across multiple Gulf states, drawing Qatar, Bahrain and Jordan directly into the line of fire and raising the danger of a general Middle East conflagration. The collapse of ceasefire diplomacy — with neither Washington nor Tehran signaling any willingness to negotiate — has removed the last off-ramp. The ICFI has warned that the Iran war is not an isolated adventure but one front of an emerging Third World War that also encompasses Ukraine, the Asia-Pacific, and the drive against China.

Key facts: - Only 3 commodity vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz yesterday, the lowest since May, after Iran vowed to halt oil and gas exports through the strait while attacks continue - Washington revoked its sanctions waiver on Iranian oil effective 12:01 a.m. EDT July 17 - US strikes have killed at least 35 people this month; Iran’s Foreign Ministry called a hospital strike “a cowardly war crime against the most innocent” - Current US regional forces number roughly 50,000, though few are ground troops

2. More than 500 Rohingya feared dead off Myanmar’s coast

More than 500 Rohingya refugees are feared dead after two boats capsized off Myanmar’s coast, in what would be one of the deadliest single episodes on record along one of the world’s most lethal maritime routes. Two boats carrying roughly 250 and 280 people departed Rakhine State in late June; the second is believed to have sunk off the Ayeyarwady coast on July 8.

UNHCR and IOM said they are “gravely concerned by the potentially devastating loss of life” and called for “stronger regional and international efforts… to prevent further loss of life.” The catastrophe follows the deadliest year on record in 2025, when over 6,500 Rohingya fled by sea and nearly 900 were reported dead or missing.

Key facts: - Roughly 1.2 million stateless Rohingya remain in overcrowded Bangladesh refugee camps, where aid cuts have forced reduced rations - The disaster reflects the persecution of the Rohingya by Myanmar’s military junta combined with the collapse of international refugee funding - Departures spike during monsoon season despite the extreme danger, as camp conditions deteriorate - The route across the Andaman Sea and Bay of Bengal has become, in the UN’s words, “one of the world’s deadliest,” a direct product of the closure of legal avenues of escape

3. Israel controls over 60 percent of Gaza amid daily ceasefire violations

A senior Israeli officer has confirmed that the IDF now controls more than 60 percent of Gaza’s territory — exceeding what was permitted under the October 2025 ceasefire deal. The IDF destroyed four Hamas weapon depots in central Gaza overnight. Analysts say Israel “has conditioned the international community to accept daily breaches and killings in Gaza as the new normal,” operating as though the ceasefire imposes no restraint.

Since January 2026, the OHCHR has recorded at least 12 attacks on Gaza police, killing 35 personnel, including officers directing traffic or overseeing markets — killings analysts describe as a deliberate effort to destroy Gaza’s civil administration and any capacity for postwar governance.

Key facts: - The territorial expansion continues even as the war on Iran dominates headlines, shielding Israeli operations from scrutiny - Targeting of civil officials points to a strategy of engineered chaos to “derail Gaza’s future” - The killings occur under a nominal ceasefire, underscoring the fiction of the diplomatic framing - The expansion in Gaza runs parallel to Israel’s vow of an “indefinite” presence in Lebanon and Syria (see International developments), pointing to a regional project of permanent occupation backed by Washington

4. Catastrophic flash flooding returns to Texas Hill Country

Catastrophic flash flooding struck Texas Hill Country again, killing at least two people and forcing more than 200 rescues almost exactly one year after the July 2025 floods that killed more than 130, including dozens of girls and counselors at Camp Mystic. Parts of the region received a year’s worth of rain in three days.

Governor Greg Abbott confirmed two deaths: a man near Comfort and a woman near Uvalde. One victim, John Mark Steward, 65, was swept from his mobile home near Kerrville after calling a neighbor at 3:06 a.m. to report the rising water. Rescuers used boats and helicopters to save stranded drivers and residents trapped in their homes.

Key facts: - The disaster arrives amid ongoing scrutiny of warning-system and infrastructure failures exposed by the 2025 catastrophe - The recurrence in the same region within a year underscores the intersection of climate-driven extreme rainfall and inadequate emergency preparedness - Federal cuts to weather and emergency-response agencies loom over the response

5. Ukraine strikes Russia’s shadow fleet as a minister’s firing sparks protests

Ukraine’s Unmanned Systems Forces struck 11 vessels of Russia’s “shadow fleet” overnight — five tankers, one gas carrier, three cargo ships, and two tugboats — in the Black Sea and Sea of Azov. Ukrainian National Guard divers destroyed a Russian Su-24M bomber at Saky airfield in occupied Crimea, and air defenses downed three ballistic missiles and 129 drones. A Russian missile strike on Odesa killed two and injured at least eight, including children; guided bombs on Zaporizhzhia killed three more.

Domestically, President Zelensky’s dismissal of Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov — credited with driving Ukraine’s drone-innovation program — triggered nationwide protests on July 16, the largest since the imposition of martial law, reflecting deep war-weariness and anger at the Zelensky government.

Key facts: - Total Russian combat losses since February 2022 are now estimated at ~1,424,620 personnel, including 1,340 in the past day, per Ukrainian figures - The shadow-fleet strikes escalate the economic war over Russia’s sanctions-evading oil exports - The protests expose fractures within the Ukrainian regime as the NATO-Russia proxy war grinds on

6. Hong Kong raids two bookstores in a third crackdown this year

Hong Kong police raided two independent bookstores — “Have A Nice Stay,” founded by former journalists, and the longstanding Greenfield Book Store — arresting five people on July 15. Authorities allege the material “stirred up hatred” against the Hong Kong government, judiciary, and law enforcement. Customs officials referred the case after finding allegedly seditious books in an overseas shipment.

It is the third round of arrests targeting independent bookstores in four months. In March, police arrested staff of the Book Punch store over the sale of a biography of jailed media proprietor Jimmy Lai. The pattern signals an intensifying campaign against independent media and publishing under Hong Kong’s national security law.

Key facts: - The specific book titles deemed “seditious” have not been publicly disclosed - The targets — a former-journalists’ shop and a dissident biography — point to a broad assault on independent publishing infrastructure - The crackdown proceeds under the sweeping powers of Beijing’s national security regime

International developments

Lebanon and Israel agree partial-withdrawal framework as Israel vows indefinite presence

Lebanon and Israel concluded two days of US-brokered talks in Rome, agreeing on the structure and guidelines for an Israeli withdrawal from two “pilot zones” in southern Lebanon. Lebanese President Joseph Aoun demanded immediate withdrawal from designated areas before further talks; Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Saar said Israel is “ready to move forward implementing these two pilot zones.”

Hours later, however, Israel’s defense minister told the Pentagon that Israeli troops will remain “indefinitely” in the broader “security zones” spanning Lebanon, Syria, and Gaza — exposing the pilot-zone framework as cover for permanent occupation. The deal stems from a June 26 Washington agreement calling for an end to Israel’s war on Lebanon and the disarmament of Hezbollah.

Sources Al Jazeera Lebanon, Israel hold talks in Rome · Times of Israel Sixth round of Israel-Lebanon talks begins in Rome

Sudan’s war intensifies around El Obeid as drone strikes drive famine

Fighting intensified around El Obeid as Sudan’s civil war approaches its fourth year. Between January and May 2026, the OHCHR documented over 1,000 civilians killed in drone strikes — about 80 percent of all conflict-related civilian deaths recorded this year. G7 foreign ministers and the EU’s Kaja Kallas urged the warring parties to cease hostilities around the city, fearing a repeat of the El Fasher massacre when the RSF took that city in October.

Nearly 19.5 million people face acute food insecurity, while the WFP has cut assistance from 5 million to 3.5 million people due to a $646 million funding gap driven by donor cuts. The RSF and its allies announced a parallel government based in Darfur this month, deepening the country’s fragmentation.

Trump alleges election ‘vulnerabilities’ to push the SAVE America Act

In a 25-minute primetime East Room address, President Trump said he was declassifying documents revealing “shocking vulnerabilities” in US election infrastructure — while presenting no evidence of a single fraudulent vote in any election. Trump claimed DHS found roughly 250,000 noncitizens registered to vote across California, New Jersey, Nevada, and Pennsylvania. Nevada’s secretary of state’s office “flatly rejected” the claim.

Trump pressed Congress to pass the SAVE America Act, which would impose proof-of-citizenship and voter-ID requirements for federal elections. The bill has passed the House but faces Senate resistance. Analysts noted that nothing in the new material suggests past counts, including 2020, were actually affected — casting the speech as a pretext for restricting ballot access as part of the administration’s broader authoritarian consolidation.

Wildfire smoke makes Detroit, Chicago and Minneapolis the world’s most polluted cities

Thick smoke from wildfires in Canada and northeastern Minnesota swept across the Great Lakes and Northeast, and by late Thursday morning Detroit, Minneapolis, and Chicago were the three most polluted major cities in the world, according to IQAir. All three registered “code purple” — “very unhealthy” — air on the EPA index, with all of Michigan and much of Minnesota under hazardous air-quality alerts. Officials urged residents to remain indoors.

The fine particulate matter in wildfire smoke lodges deep in the lungs and enters the bloodstream, raising the risk of heart attacks, strokes, and chronic respiratory disease. The International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees issued a call to “shut down plants and workplaces amidst spreading toxic air,” noting that workers — especially those in outdoor and industrial jobs — are compelled to keep working through hazardous conditions while corporate operations continue uninterrupted.

US measles nears a 34-year record as outbreaks spread

The US measles outbreak added 61 new cases, bringing 2026 to 2,231 confirmed cases — just 58 short of the entire 2025 total, itself a 34-year high. The relentless spread reflects collapsing vaccination coverage amid the anti-science campaign driven by the Trump administration’s public-health apparatus. Public-health experts warn that once the 2025 total is surpassed — likely within days — the United States will record its worst measles year since elimination was declared in 2000, a reversal of a signal achievement of twentieth-century public health.

The WSWS reported on two simultaneous disease outbreaks in New York City — Legionnaires’ disease and cyclosporiasis — which Philip Guelpa analyzed as products of the systematic prioritization of profit over public health, from deferred maintenance of cooling systems to inadequate food-safety inspection.

Roundup of other developments: - H5N1 bird flu: The USDA confirmed another infected dairy herd in Utah — the state’s 23rd — bringing the national total to 1,161 herds across 19 states. Arts and culture

The most significant cultural news of the period remains the loss of two towering figures of postwar visual art in recent weeks — British painter David Hockney, who died June 11 at 88, and American photographer Duane Michals, who died June 9 at 94. Hockney, one of the most popular artists of his generation, moved fluidly between painting, photography, stage design, and iPad drawing; his California pool paintings and Yorkshire landscapes united technical brilliance with an unfashionable insistence on observation and joy in the visible world. Michals broke with the documentary tradition of American photography by staging sequenced, text-annotated images that probed mortality, memory, and desire — an intensely literary body of work that expanded the expressive range of the medium. Their deaths, alongside those of photographers William Coupon and Hiromitsu Morimoto and performance artist Charles Dennis, mark the passing of a generation shaped by the social and artistic ferment of the mid-twentieth century.

Roundup: - Exhibitions: The Metropolitan Museum of Art opened “Raphael: Sublime Poetry,” a major survey of one of the central figures of the Italian High Renaissance. Such retrospectives, drawing large audiences, testify to an enduring popular hunger for serious art against the commodified churn of the culture industry. - Film and television: Netflix’s adaptation of Laura Ingalls Wilder’s Little House books revisits the mythology of American frontier settlement — material that demands a critical reckoning with the westward expansion, dispossession, and class struggle it romanticizes, rather than nostalgic reproduction. - Technology and childhood: A wave of AI-driven toys, dolls, and robots “built to befriend children” is drawing warnings from child-development experts that such products risk crowding out genuine human relationships — a commercial encroachment of surveillance-capitalist technology into the most intimate spheres of childhood. - Music and popular culture: Jacobin’s essay on 1970s British punk (see pseudo-left review) is a reminder of how a genuine cultural revolt against the “no future” of postwar decay was expressed through DIY practice — and later commodified.

These developments unfold against a backdrop of war, censorship (see the Hong Kong bookstore raids above), and austerity pressures on public arts funding, which continue to narrow the space for serious artistic work independent of the market and the state.

Across all six tendencies, the common thread this period is the subordination of the working class to one or another wing of the bourgeois political establishment — the Democratic Party in the United States, the Labour Party in Britain, or bourgeois-nationalist regimes internationally. None advances the central lesson of the war crisis: that the fight against imperialist war requires a break with the pseudo-left’s national-reformist framework and the construction of a unified international movement of the working class on the program of the Fourth International.
