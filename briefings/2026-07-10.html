1. US intensifies strikes on Iran as ceasefire collapses after Khamenei’s burial

The US war against Iran resumed at full intensity over the past 48 hours. US Central Command struck roughly 90 Iranian targets over two nights — air defenses, drone and missile storage, naval assets and coastal logistics — after Iran attacked three commercial tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. President Trump declared the 60-day ceasefire “over” while claiming Tehran “wants to make a deal so badly.” The strikes coincided with the burial of assassinated Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei — killed February 28, the war’s opening day — at the Imam Reza shrine in Mashhad, where his eldest son Mostafa led the funeral prayer.

The WSWS characterizes the campaign as a US-driven war of regime destabilization, with Andre Damon writing that Washington bombed Iran “for a third day amid Khamenei’s funeral,” a supreme leader the WSWS notes was “assassinated by US and Israeli forces.” The targeting of Iran’s economic lifelines — Washington has moved to revoke Iran’s oil-export license — points to a strategy of strangulation, not merely deterrence.

Key facts: - Iran’s Health Ministry reports at least 14 killed and 78 wounded across five provinces from the strikes, including nine armed forces members and casualties at an airport in Iranshahr. - Tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — roughly 20% of world oil supply — collapsed from 21 vessels to 6 in a single day; Iran says the strait reopens only under “Iranian arrangements.” - Vice President Vance: “If they shoot at ships, we’re going to knock the hell out of them.” - US strikes hit the Bushehr aerospace site, Chabahar port, Golestan transport links and IRGC-linked infrastructure; Iran retaliated against US-allied Gulf Arab states.

Sources CBS News Iran-US war: Trump says ceasefire over, Strait of Hormuz attacks · NPR US strikes Iran again, Tehran fires back at Gulf Arab states · Al Jazeera US strikes Iran despite promised pause for Khamenei funeral · WSWS Trump bombs Iran for a third day amid Khamenei’s funeral

2. Israeli strikes keep killing Palestinians in Gaza under cover of “ceasefire”

Israeli forces continued to kill Palestinians across Gaza despite the nominal ceasefire in effect since October 2025. Gaza’s Civil Defence and Health Ministry reported between 6 and 12 killed and 17 to 20 wounded in the past 24 hours, including a drone strike on Nuseirat and a strike on a home’s courtyard in Khan Younis. A humanitarian relief vehicle was hit Wednesday, killing driver Ahmad Nasser Saleem; World Central Kitchen said “humanitarian aid deliveries should never be a target” and demanded “a full accounting by Israel.”

The killings proceed within Netanyahu’s declared step-by-step plan to encircle Hamas and expand territorial control. The pattern confirms the WSWS assessment that the “ceasefire” is a framework for continued ethnic cleansing rather than a halt to the genocide.

Key facts: - Since the October 2025 ceasefire began, Israeli strikes have killed at least 1,092 Palestinians and wounded over 3,507, per Gaza’s Health Ministry. - Israel’s military now controls close to 70% of Gaza, up from roughly half at the ceasefire’s start.

Sources Al Jazeera Israeli attacks on Gaza kill 10 in 24 hours despite ceasefire · NPR Israel expands military control of Gaza under ceasefire · Middle East Monitor 12 Palestinians killed in Israeli strikes amid ceasefire violations

3. NATO’s Ankara summit pledges €70 billion for Ukraine, drives rearmament

NATO leaders concluded their 2026 summit in Ankara with a pledge of €70 billion in military equipment, assistance and training for Ukraine this year, and a commitment to sustain at least that level in 2027. Trump claimed “tremendous unity” and reported progress toward the alliance’s target of 5% of GDP defense spending by 2035. Allies reaffirmed Article 5 — “an attack on one is an attack on all.”

The summit measures the acceleration of the drive to war economy across the imperialist bloc. As the WSWS has emphasized, the rearmament is financed by austerity against the working class, with hundreds of billions redirected from social spending to weapons procurement.

Key facts: - European allies and Canada have raised core defense investment by more than $139 billion since the 2025 Hague summit’s 5%-of-GDP pledge. - Over $50 billion in new procurement was announced, including precision-strike and air/missile defense systems.

Sources NATO Overview — 2026 NATO Summit in Ankara · Al Jazeera Five key takeaways from the NATO summit in Ankara

4. DR Congo Ebola deaths pass 600 in fastest-growing outbreak on record

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has passed 600 confirmed deaths among 1,759 confirmed cases as of July 8, with 51 new cases and 20 deaths recorded in a single prior 24-hour period. The Africa CDC calls it the continent’s fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record. It is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine.

The catastrophe is compounded by the collapse of the response itself: frontline health workers in Ituri province have walked off the job over unpaid wages, unpaid since the May 15 outbreak declaration. Dr. Biensi Kano: “Since the Ebola virus disease outbreak was declared, we’ve been demanding payment for our work.” This is a textbook case of austerity and underfunded public-health infrastructure undermining an emergency response.

Key facts: - The outbreak began in Nia-Nia village, Ituri province, spreading through mining towns including Mongbwalu and Bunia; suspected cases now reported in Tshopo and Haut-Uele provinces.

Sources Al Jazeera Confirmed Ebola deaths in DR Congo hit 600 · NPR Ebola outbreak in Congo

5. Fire at Chinese shoe factory kills 28

A blaze gutted the Fujian Huiteng shoe factory in Jinjiang — a major sportswear manufacturing hub — killing 28 workers, in one of China’s deadliest factory fires in recent years. Xi Jinping demanded “an all-out search and rescue effort” and ordered officials to “strictly hold those responsible accountable.” Xinhua reported the search concluded Friday as an investigation into the cause continues. The disaster renews scrutiny of industrial and worker-safety regulation in China’s export manufacturing base, where profit pressure routinely overrides worker protections.

Sources Bloomberg Fire at a shoe factory kills 28 in one of China’s deadliest blazes · UPI Shoe factory fire kills 28 in China

Russia and Ukraine escalate drone and missile war

The NATO-Russia proxy war intensified as both sides traded long-range strikes. Russian air defenses reportedly intercepted 376 Ukrainian drones overnight across Belgorod, Bryansk, Kaluga, the Moscow region and Crimea. Ukrainian drones struck Russia’s Taganrog port and aviation college and set the Ilsky oil refinery ablaze; Crimea’s Yevpatoria lost power after fresh strikes. A daytime Russian drone strike on Kyiv this week killed 3 and injured 10, including two medics, following an earlier overnight bombardment that killed at least 22 and injured roughly 90.

Russian officials describe the intensified strikes on Kyiv as retaliation for Ukraine’s long-range attacks. Notably, Ukraine’s Patriot interceptor supply is strained by competing demand from the Middle East war — a concrete illustration of how the simultaneous US wars against Iran and Russia are stretching imperialist military resources.

Sources NPR Russian missile and drone attack on Kyiv · Kyiv Post Ukraine strikes Russian refineries and ports

Sudan’s RSF siege of El Obeid threatens 500,000 civilians

Half a million people are trapped in El Obeid, capital of North Kordofan, as the RSF paramilitary attempts to seize the city after already capturing El Fasher. The UN human rights office documented 15 drone strikes on El Obeid and surrounding areas in three weeks, killing at least 45 civilians. The city has had no electricity for nearly a month; food prices have surged as much as 300% amid fuel shortages. More than 11,000 people, including over 5,500 children, have fled in the past two weeks. The Sudanese catastrophe — a war fueled by rival regional powers backing the SAF and RSF — remains one of the world’s gravest humanitarian disasters, largely ignored by the Western press.

Sources Al Jazeera El Obeid under siege by RSF: Sudan’s next El Fasher? · UN News Civilians trapped as fighting escalates in North Kordofan

Venezuela earthquake death toll hits 3,899 as reconstruction tops $37 billion

The official death toll from the June 24 twin earthquakes has risen to 3,899, with 16,740 injured and 17,907 displaced — the deadliest natural disaster in Venezuela in over a century. Reconstruction is estimated at more than $37 billion (roughly $24 billion in housing, school and commercial damage, $13 billion in infrastructure). Interim President Delcy Rodríguez has requested $4.85 billion from the IMF and launched “Misión Venezuela Renace,” offering mortgage subsidies of up to 80% and monthly assistance. The disaster deepens the political crisis following claims that former President Nicolás Maduro is now jailed in Brooklyn after being “captured” by the United States — a stunning escalation of US intervention in Latin America whose implications for the region’s sovereignty are far-reaching.

Preliminary hearing opens in Charlie Kirk assassination case

A preliminary hearing began in Provo, Utah, with prosecutors presenting evidence to justify a full trial of the man accused of killing conservative activist Charlie Kirk. A former Utah Valley University police officer testified to finding an apparent “sniper pad” — elbow, knee and foot impressions — on a rooftop with a clear line of sight to where Kirk was shot. Prosecutors say the suspect texted a roommate that he targeted Kirk because he “had enough of his hatred,” and are seeking a “victim targeting enhancement” arguing the killing was motivated by Kirk’s political expression. The Trump administration and the right have used Kirk’s killing to justify a sweeping crackdown on political dissent, making the courtroom framing politically charged.

Sources PBS NewsHour Prosecutors argue accused Kirk killer should stand trial

ICE detention population hits record 73,000

Immigrant detention has surged 84% since January 2025 to an all-time high of roughly 73,000 people, with over 70% having no criminal record, following the Trump administration’s Secure America Act funding boost. Mass detention continues alongside ongoing anti-ICE protests in cities from Los Angeles to Chicago to Minneapolis. The scale of the detention apparatus — and the fact that the overwhelming majority held have committed no crime — confirms the WSWS characterization of the campaign as a police-state assault on the working class using immigrants as the initial target.

Philadelphia utility workers stage first strike in PECO history

Roughly 1,500 IBEW Local 614 workers walked off the job at midnight July 4 after contract talks collapsed, marking the first strike in the utility’s history. The dispute centers on wages and working conditions amid a summer heat wave, with both sides accusing each other of bad-faith bargaining. The walkout at a major electricity provider during dangerous heat highlights both the militancy building among utility workers and the risks of privatized, understaffed critical infrastructure.

Understaffing, patient safety and wages are the common demands, reflecting deepening crisis conditions in US healthcare labor. The concentration of walkouts points to a broadening rebellion in the health sector that intersects with the ongoing degradation of public health amid measles, COVID and other threats.

US measles cases near 2026 record with half the year remaining

The CDC reported 2,170 confirmed US measles cases as of July 2 — just 119 short of the full-year 2025 total of 2,289 — with 93% of cases tied to 31 active outbreaks across 41 jurisdictions. Children and teens aged 5–19 account for 51% of cases. With five months of peak transmission season still ahead, 2026 is on track to set a new post-elimination-era record, a direct consequence of collapsing vaccination rates and the anti-science campaign now entrenched in the federal public-health apparatus.

Sources Global Biodefense / CIDRAP US 2026 measles cases nearly match full-year 2025 toll

H5N1 bird flu spreads across species and continents

From August 2025 to June 10, 2026, three countries outside the US reported 12 new human H5N1 infections, three of them fatal. The virus has now been confirmed on every continent including Antarctica, with over 180 million poultry birds and 1,000-plus US dairy farms affected to date. The WHO still rates public-health risk as low with no sustained human-to-human transmission, but the virus’s expanding mammalian host range — including cattle — keeps pandemic-preparedness concern elevated.

Sources CDC H5N1 global summary

Oral GLP-1 pill orforglipron beats oral semaglutide

In the 52-week ACHIEVE-1 trial of 1,698 adults with type 2 diabetes, published in the New England Journal of Medicine, orforglipron lowered HbA1c by 1.71–1.91 percentage points from a baseline of 8.3%, versus 1.47 points for oral semaglutide. In the companion ATTAIN-1 obesity trial (3,100+ adults), the top dose produced 11% average weight loss versus 2% for placebo over 72 weeks. As the first once-daily, small-molecule (non-peptide) oral GLP-1 drug to outperform oral semaglutide, orforglipron could simplify manufacturing and expand access — though under the profit system such advances remain gated by price.

Sources NEJM / ScienceDaily Orforglipron outperforms oral semaglutide

FTC and Illinois move to regulate AI

  • Variants watch: XFG.1.1 accounts for 32% of US sequenced COVID cases, followed by XFG (13%) and XFG.14.1 (8%); a heavily mutated offshoot, BA.3.2 (“Cicana”), has been detected in dozens of states and is under CDC monitoring, its immune-evasive profile sustaining transmission despite widespread prior immunity.
  • FTC on AI: The FTC voted 2-0 on July 1 to propose that AI companies steering model outputs toward undisclosed ideological objectives may violate Section 5’s deception provisions; comments are open through July 31. It is the first federal move to treat hidden AI output-steering as consumer-protection deception.
  • Illinois AI law: Governor JB Pritzker signed the Artificial Intelligence Safety Measures Act on July 6, requiring independent third-party safety audits by experts with no financial conflicts — a stricter bar than prior state laws. As of July 1, states have enacted 109 AI-related laws, a fragmenting patchwork in the absence of federal standards.

Arts and culture

The Karlovy Vary International Film Festival, one of Europe’s oldest and most significant, runs July 3–11 in Czechia, closing this weekend. The festival remains a venue where serious international cinema — including work from Eastern Europe, the Balkans and the Global South — reaches audiences outside the commercial Hollywood circuit, at a moment when war and militarism increasingly shape both funding and content across the film world.

Looming over the summer release calendar is Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey, opening July 17 in the United States. Adapted from Homer’s epic and featuring Matt Damon, Tom Holland, Zendaya, Robert Pattinson and Anne Hathaway, the film represents another large-scale attempt to return ancient epic to mass audiences. Homer’s poem — a meditation on war, homecoming, exile and the human cost of a decade-long conflict — carries obvious resonance in an era of open imperialist war; whether Nolan’s spectacle engages that content critically or reduces it to visual grandeur will be the test.

In the visual arts, two major exhibitions center women artists of the twentieth century: - Alice Neel, “Beautifully Imperfect,” opens July 16 at the Serralves Foundation in Porto. Neel, a lifelong radical who painted the poor, the working class and the marginalized of New York with unflinching humanism, spent decades ignored by the art establishment for her figurative commitment and her politics. Her rehabilitation is welcome, though institutions often sand away the class content of her work. - Ana Mendieta receives her largest-ever UK exhibition at Tate Modern, featuring pieces never before shown in Britain. Mendieta’s “earth-body” works, made after her family’s exile from Cuba, grapple with displacement, violence and the body — themes museums tend to fold into identitarian framing at the expense of their broader social meaning.

Cultural output on the left underscores the enduring appeal of the anti-war and working-class tradition. The IMT-aligned communist.red this week published a review praising the 1943 film The Silent Village — a re-enactment of the Nazi massacre at Lidice staged by Welsh miners — as “filled with working-class spirit from top to bottom,” alongside a review of Elem Klimov’s harrowing 1985 anti-war masterpiece Come and See. That these tendencies reach for genuinely powerful anti-war art is notable; the question, as always, is whether the political conclusions drawn match the works’ unsparing honesty.

The through-line across these developments is the pull of war, exile and class on serious culture — and the countervailing pressure of institutions to defang that content. Genuine artistic achievement, from Neel’s portraits to Klimov’s cinema, retains its power precisely because it refuses to look away.

Jacobin / DSA

The critique is real but disarming: by framing the disaster as a failure of bad pundits and flawed policy, Jacobin obscures the systemic drive of US imperialism and leaves the door open to a “smarter,” more competent foreign policy — precisely the Democratic Party frame. “The Case for Nationalizing Artificial Intelligence” argues that because “the public built AI,” public ownership should replace billionaire control. Its coverage adapts to the milieu of the DSA left and the anti-war NGOs even while criticizing them rhetorically. Overall orientation: revolutionary phraseology combined with movementist practice that leaves the working class without an independent political strategy.
