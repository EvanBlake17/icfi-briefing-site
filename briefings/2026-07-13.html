1. US launches fourth wave of strikes on Iran as Strait of Hormuz crisis deepens

US Central Command carried out a fourth wave of strikes on Iran over the weekend, hitting dozens of additional targets and, for the first time, deploying one-way attack drones at sea. Over three preceding nights CENTCOM struck roughly 300 sites — missile and drone infrastructure, naval assets, ammunition depots and coastal radar — including some 140 targets in the Saturday-night round alone. Iran retaliated with drone and missile attacks against US-linked sites in Bahrain, Kuwait and Jordan.

The escalation has driven the Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly 20% of global oil supply passes — to the center of a widening war. Iranian authorities declared passage through the waterway “not possible,” while CENTCOM insisted, “Iran does not control the strait. Traffic is flowing.” Trump declared the fragile ceasefire “over” but said talks would continue; Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi traveled to Muscat to negotiate an Omani-brokered two-route shipping-lane proposal.

As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, Trump has openly threatened to “decimate and destroy” Iran, marking a qualitative expansion of a war of aggression that the ICFI has warned threatens to become a regional and world conflagration.

Key facts: - Iran reported 17 killed and 115 injured from earlier US strikes; a naval officer was killed at Jask port, and three people were hurt in Qatar by falling missile-interception debris. - Iran’s parliament speaker: “The era of one-sided deals is OVER.” - Brent crude rose 4.14% to $79.16 a barrel Monday; WTI rose 2.97% to $74.38. - The IEA has described the disruption as potentially the largest supply shock in oil-market history.

2. Israeli forces kill Palestinians in Gaza as “ceasefire” talks stall in Cairo

Israeli forces killed at least five to six Palestinians on Sunday, including nine-year-old Tala Abu Matar, shot in a tent encampment in Al-Bureij. Four more were killed in a drone strike on a metal foundry in Gaza City’s Sabra neighborhood. Gaza’s Health Ministry reports 1,098 Palestinians killed and 3,535 wounded by Israeli fire since the October “ceasefire” took effect — even though large-scale ground fighting has largely stopped.

Nine months into the US-brokered agreement, Israeli forces now control roughly 70–80% of Gaza, far beyond the agreed “Yellow Line,” in what amounts to slow-motion annexation. A Hamas delegation met in Cairo on the stalled second phase — disarmament and Israeli withdrawal — with no breakthrough. Total Palestinian deaths since October 2023 now exceed 73,118.

Key facts: - The Gaza Government Media Office and NPR both cite Israeli control approaching 70% of the territory. - Daily killings continue despite the formal cessation of hostilities, exposing the “ceasefire” as a mechanism for continued dispossession.

Sources Al Jazeera Israeli forces kill five Palestinians in latest attacks on Gaza · NPR 9 months into a ceasefire, Israel now controls nearly 70% of Gaza

3. US Congressman Ro Khanna detained by armed Israeli settlers and soldiers in West Bank

California Democrat Ro Khanna said he and other Americans were detained for more than an hour by masked, armed Israeli settlers near Khirbet Zanuta, a Palestinian hamlet whose residents were forcibly displaced by settler raids. “Israeli settlers, brandishing American made M4s, detained me & other Americans on my trip to Palestine,” Khanna wrote, adding that when the Israeli army arrived, soldiers “sided with the settlers and continued our detention.” The group was released after police intervened, following appeals to the US embassy in Jerusalem.

The incident — a sitting member of the US Congress detained at gunpoint by settlers armed with US weapons — has drawn heavy press coverage and a wave of pro-Israel attacks on Khanna. On the WSWS, Kevin Reed situated the episode within the daily violence Palestinians endure across the occupied West Bank, where the same settler-military apparatus operates with impunity and US backing.

4. DOJ subpoenas four New York Times reporters over Air Force One story

Four New York Times reporters — Julian Barnes, Eric Lipton, Tyler Pager and Eric Schmitt — were subpoenaed Friday evening, with federal agents delivering papers at some reporters’ homes. The subpoenas relate to a story reporting that the Secret Service urged Trump to use an older Air Force One rather than the Qatar-gifted 747 at the NATO summit because the gifted plane lacked “advanced antimissile capabilities.” Issued by SDNY US Attorney Jay Clayton — Trump’s nominee for national intelligence director — the subpoenas demand testimony before a Manhattan grand jury Wednesday.

“The appearance of Federal law enforcement agents on the doorstep of news reporters should shock the conscience of any American who believes in the Constitution,” said the Times’ deputy general counsel. The move fits an intensifying pattern of state intimidation against the press.

Sources NPR Justice Department subpoenas New York Times reporters over Air Force One reporting · PBS News New York Times reporters subpoenaed after Air Force One stories

5. Senator Lindsey Graham dies at 71 after 10th wartime trip to Ukraine

Senator Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.) died Saturday evening of an aortic dissection caused by arteriosclerotic cardiovascular disease, per the DC Medical Examiner. He had just returned from his 10th wartime visit to Ukraine. In the Senate since 2003 and chair of the Senate Budget Committee, Graham was seeking a fifth term in November.

Graham was among the most aggressive militarists in Congress — a champion of the Iraq war, the arming of Ukraine and unconditional support for Israel. Trump called him “one of the greatest people and Senators I have ever known”; Netanyahu said “Israel has lost one of its greatest friends”; Zelensky cited his repeated visits. As Patrick Martin noted on the WSWS, the bipartisan mourning across the political and media establishment is itself an expression of the reactionary consensus Graham embodied. South Carolina Gov. Henry McMaster will appoint an interim successor; a special election is set for November 3, 2026.

Sources CBS News Senator Lindsey Graham dies after brief and sudden illness · Washington Post Lindsey Graham, longtime South Carolina senator, dies at 71 · WSWS Trump, Democrats, media mourn right-wing warmonger Lindsey Graham

6. Bangkok pub fire kills at least 27

A fire broke out around midnight at the Rong Beer Na Ladprao pub in Bangkok’s Chatuchak district, killing at least 27 people and hospitalizing 63, with 22 in critical condition. A musician performing at the venue reported seeing smoke from a circuit breaker near the stage before an explosion filled the space with thick smoke. Firefighters brought the blaze under control early Monday. The disaster — a crowded entertainment venue with apparent electrical faults and inadequate fire safety — follows a grim pattern of profit-driven negligence in venues catering to workers and youth across Southeast Asia.

NATO’s Ankara summit pledges €70 billion for Ukraine

NATO allies pledged €70 billion (~$80 billion) in military aid to Ukraine for both 2026 and 2027, and Trump told Zelensky the US would license Ukraine to domestically produce Patriot missiles. Ukraine was again left without a concrete NATO membership timeline. Most significant structurally, Canadian PM Mark Carney announced a new Defense, Security and Resilience Bank aiming to mobilize $134 billion for allied defense financing by 2027 — a permanent institutional apparatus for the war economy that dwarfs the “aid” framing.

Russian strikes cut power to 70,000 as front-line evacuations continue

Russian strikes left nearly 70,000 customers without power in Chernihiv region, while drones struck a high-rise and a home-improvement store in Odesa. Eight civilians were injured across Kherson; Russian forces launched 69 attacks Monday, concentrated on the Sloviansk and Pokrovsk sectors. Ukrainian authorities evacuated 419 people, including 39 children, from front-line Donetsk. Underscoring deepening Moscow-Beijing ties, Russian and Chinese navies completed the “Joint Sea 2026” exercise near Qingdao on July 11.

Sudan’s El Obeid under siege as RSF drone strikes escalate

Roughly 500,000 civilians, including some 100,000 displaced people, are trapped in El Obeid, North Kordofan, as the Rapid Support Forces close off surrounding routes. The UN reports 15 drone strikes in three weeks killed at least 45 civilians, hitting hospitals, schools, power stations and fuel depots. Save the Children says over 11,000 people, including 5,500-plus children, have fled in the past two weeks. On July 9, the European Parliament voted 476–28 to call for designating the RSF a terrorist organization and sanctioning commanders — a largely symbolic measure. The UN warns of a repeat of the El-Fasher massacre, yet the crisis receives a fraction of the coverage afforded Gaza or Ukraine.

Typhoon Bavi slams eastern China; 2.2 million evacuated

Typhoon Bavi made landfall in Yuhuan City, Zhejiang, around 11:20pm Saturday and again near Yueqing/Wenzhou, forcing the evacuation of more than 2.2 million people. Over 1,300 trees toppled in Yueqing; no official deaths were reported as of Sunday. China’s National Development and Reform Commission allocated 100 million yuan (~$14 million) for emergency recovery. Heavy rain is forecast through July 14 across Fujian, Zhejiang, Jiangxi, Anhui, Jiangsu and Shandong.

Former Emir of Qatar Sheikh Hamad bin Khalifa Al Thani dies at 74

Sheikh Hamad, who ruled Qatar from 1995 to 2013, died Sunday, per the Amiri Diwan. Under his rule Qatar’s GDP rose more than 24-fold, driven by LNG exports; he introduced Qatar’s first permanent constitution (2004) and founded Al Jazeera. Qatar declared four days of mourning, with tributes from Egypt’s el-Sisi, Pakistan’s Zardari and Turkey’s Erdogan.

SCOTUS greenlights termination of Temporary Protected Status for Haitians and Syrians

The Supreme Court cleared the way for the Trump administration to terminate Temporary Protected Status for Haitians and Syrians, exposing hundreds of thousands of people to loss of legal work authorization and deportation to countries wracked by violence and collapse. The ruling is part of a sweeping assault on immigrant rights and the broader construction of a deportation apparatus. It arrives as the administration escalates raids and detentions nationwide, with the courts increasingly functioning as an instrument of the executive’s anti-immigrant offensive.

ICE enforcement escalation fuels protests across US cities

The “Secure America Act,” signed June 10, injected $350 million into ICE operations targeting sanctuary jurisdictions, driving a surge of raids and detentions and provoking protests in cities including Houston and Kansas City. The escalation follows a series of killings of civilians by immigration agents. In Houston, hundreds protested the ICE killing of worker Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, who was shot through the side of his van while driving to work — a case the WSWS characterizes as state-sponsored extrajudicial murder (see WSWS coverage below). A Brookings analysis warns that ICE operations are “disrupting societal norms and democratic ideals,” reflecting the machinery of authoritarian rule being erected around immigration enforcement.

Science, technology, and public health

  • US measles cases climb to 2,231 for 2026 — 93% of confirmed cases are outbreak-associated amid 32 distinct outbreaks.
  • H5N1 bird flu remains widespread in poultry and dairy herds — 12 human infections reported outside the US from August 2025 to June 2026.
  • COVID-19 growing in 11 states — Uneven summer trends as surveillance infrastructure erodes.

Measles. The CDC reports 2,231 confirmed US measles cases for 2026 as of July 9, with 32 outbreaks and 93% of cases (2,082 of 2,231) outbreak-associated. The continuing spread reflects the collapse of vaccination coverage and public-health infrastructure, compounded by an anti-vaccine political leadership that has actively undermined immunization programs. Measles, declared eliminated in the US in 2000, is now endemic again in pockets of the country.

Bird flu (H5N1). A(H5) influenza remains widespread in wild birds globally and continues to cause outbreaks in US poultry and dairy cattle, with sporadic human infections among dairy and poultry workers. The CDC characterizes the immediate public-health risk as low but is monitoring closely. Internationally, 12 human H5N1 infections were reported by three countries outside the US between August 2025 and June 2026. The persistence of the virus in mammalian hosts — particularly dairy herds — keeps alive the risk of a mutation enabling efficient human-to-human transmission, a danger public-health authorities remain unequipped to contain.

COVID-19. As of July 8, infections were growing or likely growing in 11 states, declining in 18, and flat in 21 — an uneven summer pattern unfolding as wastewater and clinical surveillance systems continue to be dismantled. The end of systematic tracking makes it increasingly difficult to detect new variants or anticipate surges.

Peer-reviewed research. Recent issues of The Lancet and The Lancet Public Health (July 2026) feature studies on adverse childhood experiences among homeless youth, opioid-agonist treatment and mortality in French primary care, and COVID-19 mitigation among formerly incarcerated people — research foregrounding how poverty, incarceration and social dislocation drive health outcomes, in line with the social-determinants framework.

Public health in the neocolonial world. WSWS coverage of a dengue epidemic in Sri Lanka — tens of thousands infected amid the collapse of the public health system under IMF-dictated austerity — is a stark reminder that the same processes of privatization and cuts driving disease resurgence in the imperialist centers operate with even greater lethality in the oppressed nations.

Arts and culture

The death of Sam Neill at 78 dominates the cultural news of the past 24 hours. The New Zealand actor, who died “suddenly and unexpectedly” in Sydney, built a five-decade career spanning independent art cinema, international blockbusters and television. Best known globally as Dr. Alan Grant in Jurassic Park, Neill’s finest work lay in more textured roles — from Jane Campion’s The Piano to the menacing performances in Possession and In the Mouth of Madness — that revealed a subtle, intelligent screen presence. His passing marks the loss of an actor whose career bridged the New Zealand and Australian new waves with the Hollywood studio system, and who repeatedly chose challenging material over easy stardom.

Elsewhere, the machinery of state intervention in culture is advancing on multiple fronts:

  • Trump’s assault on the Smithsonian. The administration is moving to impose what the WSWS terms a “fascistic version of US history” on the Smithsonian museums, purging exhibitions on slavery, racism and class struggle in favor of a triumphalist national narrative (see WSWS coverage below). The effort to rewrite historical memory from above is a hallmark of authoritarian regimes and a direct attack on the scientific study of history.
  • Booker Prize. The 2026 Booker Prize longlist — the “Booker Dozen” — will be announced Tuesday, July 28, focusing attention on the year’s literary fiction and the ongoing pressures of consolidation and commercialization in publishing.
  • Film. The expanded Fraenkel Film Festival opened in early July, with Christian Marclay programming Brian De Palma’s Carrie. Documentarian Ross McElwee’s new film Remake — a somber meditation generated by the death of his son — arrives in theaters this month, alongside a 40th-anniversary re-release of his landmark Sherman’s March. Rustin Thompson’s documentary The Last Picture Shows, traveling 10,825 miles to record 123 small-town movie theaters, chronicles the slow extinction of communal cinema-going under the pressures of streaming monopolies and economic decline.
  • Visual arts. The Galway International Arts Festival 2026 features Sean Henry’s lifelike figurative sculptures — part of a broader resurgence of representational public art engaging directly with the human figure.

These developments unfold against a backdrop of tightening political control over cultural institutions, from museum defundings to the intimidation of the press. The WSWS framework insists that art be understood in its social and historical context: works like The Last Picture Shows and McElwee’s autobiographical documentaries speak to real processes of social atomization and loss, while the state’s drive to rewrite history at the Smithsonian is an ideological weapon of a ruling class preparing for war and repression.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s “Only International Solidarity Can Free Palestine” argues for Palestinian liberation through solidarity campaigns modeled on the anti-apartheid movement — a moderating, protest-politics framing that evades the question of revolutionary leadership and the role of the working class across the region. “We Can’t Rely on Green Capital for Decarbonization” offers a mild social-democratic critique of Biden-era green subsidies, calling for public investment but stopping well short of a struggle against the profit system. “Lower the Voting Age” frames an 18-to-16 franchise expansion as a boon to progressive organizing — a reform operating entirely within the bourgeois-electoral framework. Overall: a liberal-left orientation that channels social anger into pressure on the existing order rather than its overthrow.

Left Voice

The Morenoite Left Voice remains the sharpest of the US tendencies. “Why Aren’t the Largest U.S. Labor Unions Calling a General Strike Against ICE?” is a pointed critique of the union bureaucracy’s refusal to mobilize, consistent with a formal “class independence” line. “Mamdani’s First Budget Is a Masterclass in Class Collaboration” attacks the DSA-backed New York mayor from the left for collaborating with capital — notable for refusing to grant a “progressive” Democrat a pass. Its Iran war analysis frames Trump as trapped in an unwinnable war, arguing US imperial decline from a revolutionary-internationalist standpoint. Yet Left Voice’s activism-driven, movementist practice and its orientation to spontaneous mobilizations remain at odds with the ICFI’s insistence on the primacy of building a revolutionary party.

Liberation News / PSL

Liberation News illustrates the PSL’s campism most clearly. Its “Solidarity Statement with the Communist Party of Turkey (TKP) on the anti-NATO protests” and sympathetic coverage of the Alliance of Sahel States — “‘One space, one people, one destiny’: Two years of the Alliance of Sahel States” — align the PSL with capitalist state actors and military juntas opposed to Washington, substituting bloc politics for an independent working-class program. Its denunciation of the SCOTUS TPS ruling is correct as far as it goes but remains within a protest framework. Overall: an “anti-imperialism” that champions rival national-bourgeois and military regimes rather than the international unity of workers.

Socialist Alternative

“The Democrats Won’t Save Us—The Case For A New Party” rejects reliance on the Democrats and calls for a new workers’ party. “Middle East War: Capitalism Offers No Way Out” opposes the Iran war on internationalist grounds, citing 10,000-plus dead. “Cop Kills Black Infant For Walmart’s Profits—Justice for Kohen Wiley!” ties police violence to corporate profit — a class-first framing. While these positions resist channeling struggle through the Democrats, Socialist Alternative’s “new party” call remains a broad-left electoral project detached from the fight to build a revolutionary Marxist leadership.

SWP (UK) and Socialist Appeal / RCP

The two British tendencies are locked in rival anti-fascist strategies centered on Nigel Farage and Keir Starmer. The SWP’s “Marxism Festival debates how to stop international far right” and its front-page “Kick Out Farage the Fraudster” emphasize united-front street mobilization, while “Labour meltdown: Will Keir Starmer make it through 2026?” critiques Labour from outside without endorsing it. The RCP (communist.red) counterposes a “revolutionary” pose: “Five reasons why chanting ‘refugees are welcome here’ doesn’t work” polemicizes against moralistic liberal antiracism, while “Nigel Farage resigns in political stunt” and “Fight the right! Fight racism! Fight for revolution!” call for “revolutionary leadership” in the abstract. Both reduce the fight against fascism to questions of activist tactics, evading the central task of politically breaking the working class from the Labour bureaucracy.
