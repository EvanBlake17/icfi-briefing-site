1. Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan sign trilateral mutual-defense pact

Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed the “Mecca Joint Defence Agreement” on August 7, declaring that an armed attack on any one of the three states constitutes an attack on all. The agreement builds on the bilateral Saudi-Pakistani defense pact concluded in September 2025 and was signed directly in the shadow of the US-Israeli war on Iran that began in February and the Houthi missile and drone campaign now reaching into Saudi territory.

The practical content of the pact is the extension of nuclear-armed Pakistan’s deterrent over the Arabian Peninsula — the first time a nuclear weapons state has formally extended such a guarantee to an Arab government. It represents a significant realignment of regional military blocs, driven by the perception among the Gulf bourgeoisie that Washington’s security guarantees have become unreliable, and by Ankara’s drive to establish itself as an independent regional power.

Key facts: - The pact was signed in Mecca by the three heads of government on August 7, 2026. - It commits all three signatories to treat an attack on one as an attack on all, on the NATO Article 5 model. - Pakistan possesses an estimated 170-plus nuclear warheads; Saudi Arabia has repeatedly stated it would seek nuclear parity with Iran. - Turkey and Saudi Arabia have been on opposing sides of multiple regional conflicts over the past decade, including Qatar, Libya and Syria — the pact papers over rather than resolves those antagonisms. - Neither the United States nor Israel was consulted in advance, according to regional reporting.

2. Houthi strikes widen into Saudi Arabia as Hormuz shipping is hit again

Houthi forces struck Saudi Arabia’s Najran region on August 7 and claimed drone and missile attacks that killed or wounded “hundreds” of Yemeni government troops. Israeli and Saudi-linked strikes on eastern Yemen the same day killed at least two civilians and injured 14. A source close to the Saudi military told the Times of Israel that the coalition “won’t stand idly by.”

In the Strait of Hormuz, the Bermuda-flagged LNG carrier Gaslog Shanghai, loaded in Qatar, was among two vessels hit. Kuwait’s defense ministry reported detecting and intercepting Iranian drones entering its airspace — a further widening of the war’s geography. Iran and Oman are meanwhile negotiating agreed shipping-route coordinates through the Strait, with Tehran reportedly seeking to exclude US and Israeli-linked vessels entirely and to impose transit fees on “hostile” countries.

Key facts: - The truce that had partially held collapsed after a strike on Sanaa airport aimed at preventing an Iranian aircraft from landing. - The US imposed a fresh round of Iran sanctions on August 7, targeting the aviation, carpet, pistachio and gold sectors, following the reported collapse of a nuclear de-escalation framework. - India’s shipping ministry reported more than 4,000 seafarers repatriated and 62 Indian-flagged vessels safely transited as of August 7. - The US resumed direct strikes on Iran in early July after Tehran attacked vessels transiting routes it had not authorized.

3. US Senate passes Russia-Iran sanctions bill 86-11

The Senate on Friday passed the “Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026” by a vote of 86 to 11, imposing primary and secondary sanctions on Russia’s financial system and “shadow fleet,” extending the Iran Sanctions Act of 1996 for a further five years, and authorizing the president to levy tariffs of up to 100 percent on the top five purchasers of Russian oil and natural gas. The bill, originally authored by Graham — who died on July 11 — and Democrat Richard Blumenthal, now goes to the House.

Republicans voted 49-1 in favor; Democrats split 37-10 for the bill. An amendment to strip the tariff provisions failed 32-64, with 16 Democrats joining Republicans to defeat it. The WSWS Perspective published today, “Ukraine and Iran votes expose support by Democrats and Republicans for global war,” notes that Democratic objections were confined to separation-of-powers questions rather than opposition to war itself, and concludes: “The struggle against imperialist war … requires a political break … from the Democratic Party.”

Key facts: - Blumenthal, addressing Putin from the floor: “Your economy … is going to be throttled, even decimated by cutting off … revenue from Russian oil and gas.” - Senator Chris Coons: “What really matters is not this bill; it’s what does Donald Trump do with it.” - Sanctions reach Russian officials, oligarchs, their family members, and financial institutions. - The Iran provisions strengthen penalties on entities investing in Iran’s energy sector.

4. Israel escalates West Bank raids as Gaza “phase two” stalls

Israeli forces completed a two-day raid on the Qalandiyah refugee camp and Kafr ’Aqab, withdrawing on August 7 with the Palestinian Authority reporting 51 injuries. Separate incursions hit the Askar and Balata refugee camps and Beit Furik, where soldiers reportedly assaulted and detained a 40-year-old Palestinian man. Weekend attacks on Gaza on August 1-2 killed 26 Palestinians, 19 of them in a single day.

Netanyahu has stated that Israeli forces will not withdraw from Gaza’s “Yellow Line” — the demarcation behind which Israel still holds 52 percent of the Strip — until the “complete demilitarisation of Hamas,” and has rejected Qatari and Turkish oversight of any disarmament process. Analyst Mtanes Shehadeh told Al Jazeera that Netanyahu is deliberately stalling the second phase of the US-brokered plan to avoid “electoral damage” ahead of October elections.

Key facts: - Roughly 77 percent of Gaza’s population — 1.6 million people, including 800,000 children — remains at “crisis or worse” levels of food insecurity despite the nominal ceasefire. - Tens of thousands of children are assessed at severe malnutrition risk by UN agencies. - Israel retains military control of more than half of Gaza’s territory under the ceasefire’s first phase. - Settler violence in the West Bank has surged in parallel with the army raids.

5. US payrolls fall 23,000 as stocks close at record highs

US nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 in July, against a consensus forecast of roughly +80,000, the first outright monthly decline in months. The Bureau of Labor Statistics attributed the drop primarily to a 53,000 fall in government employment. The unemployment rate edged down to 4.1 percent from 4.2 percent — but the improvement was driven by workers leaving the labor force entirely, with participation falling to 61.4 percent, its lowest level in more than five years.

Financial markets rallied on the data. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757.64 and the Nasdaq gained 1.3 percent, on expectations that a deteriorating labor market locks the Federal Reserve into holding or cutting rates. The inverse relationship could not be clearer: the worse conditions become for workers, the higher asset prices climb.

Key facts: - May payrolls were revised down to +63,000 and June to +20,000 — a combined downward revision of 103,000. - Average hourly earnings rose two cents to $37.62, up 3.2 percent year-over-year, the slowest wage growth since May 2021 and barely ahead of inflation. - Temporary layoffs rose by 153,000 to 921,000. - ISM Services came in at 54.0 with a prices index of 67.7 — inflationary pressure persisting even as employment contracts.

6. Russia suffers record casualties in July while territorial gains collapse

Ukraine’s General Staff reported that Russian forces sustained 42,800 casualties in July, including 30,272 attributed to drone strikes alone — the highest monthly toll of the war. Over the same month Russian forces occupied just 37.85 square kilometers of additional Ukrainian territory, a near-total stall in the offensive that had ground forward through 2025.

Russia compensated with unprecedented aerial bombardment, dropping a record 8,300 glide bombs and firing 376 ballistic missiles in July, up from the previous March record of 288. Russian airstrikes killed six people in Ukraine on August 6, including three civilians in Kharkiv Oblast, and Kherson suffered a total power blackout.

Key facts: - Zelensky pressed NATO allies to release Patriot interceptors from storage: “Anti-ballistic missiles must protect people — not sit in stockpiles.” - Ukraine has intercepted only 437 of the 4,626 ballistic missiles fired at it since the war began. - Drone warfare now accounts for roughly 70 percent of reported Russian casualties, a fundamental transformation in the character of the fighting. - Both sides are burning through working-class conscripts at industrial rates for territorial movements measured in single-digit kilometers.

7. Trump signs two executive orders attacking birthright citizenship

On August 6, Trump signed two executive orders denying US citizenship to categories of children born on American soil — issued just five weeks after the Supreme Court, in a 6-3 decision on June 30, struck down his earlier attempt to abolish birthright citizenship by decree. Justices Thomas, Alito and Gorsuch dissented in favor of Trump’s position.

The new orders deny citizenship to children born in the United States where either parent is designated an “alien enemy” or terrorist, where a parent works for a foreign government, where parents allegedly engaged in commercial transactions to obtain citizenship, or where birth occurs in US territories lacking a federal citizenship statute. Trump said openly: “We had a very unfortunate decision in the Supreme Court concerning birthright … so we’re making adjustments.” Writing on the WSWS, Jacob Crosse compared the doctrine of punishing children for the alleged acts of parents to Nazi Sippenhaft — kin liability.

Key facts: - The Washington Post reported the same week that Trump is treating his two Supreme Court defeats — birthright citizenship and the attempted firing of Fed governor Lisa Cook — as obstacles to be worked around rather than final rulings. - Trump’s “Gold Card” program simultaneously offers residency and a citizenship path to foreigners paying $1-2 million. - ICE arrested more than 51,000 people in July, a record monthly high, with family-unit deportations up over 20 percent in August and family detention reopened at the Dilley and Karnes facilities in Texas.

8. Thailand school shooting kills seven near Bangkok

A 14-year-old student killed seven people — five of them school staff — and injured about 15 at Debsirin Nonthaburi School in Bang Kruai district outside Bangkok on August 7, before killing himself. He had killed his grandparents, with whom he lived, before travelling to the school. Investigators reported he had been watching videos of US school shootings in the days preceding the attack.

It is Thailand’s deadliest mass killing since the 2022 Nong Bua Lamphu childcare centre massacre, in which 38 people died. Thai authorities announced compensation of 20,000 baht (roughly $600) per death and 5,000 baht (roughly $150) per injury — a figure that speaks for itself about the value assigned to working-class life.

Key facts: - Teachers and school staff made up the majority of the dead. - Classmates and neighbours described the attacker as quiet and “well-behaved,” according to the Bangkok Post. - Thailand has among the highest civilian firearm ownership rates in Asia, with an estimated 10 million guns in circulation, roughly half of them unregistered. - The transmission of the American school-shooting phenomenon internationally through social media is a symptom of a globalised social crisis, not a national pathology.

CIA establishes secret Cuba task force as new sanctions hit military conglomerate

The CIA has secretly created a Cuba task force staffed with case officers, analysts, recruiters, cyber-operations personnel and covert influence specialists, according to reporting confirmed this week. Cuba has been elevated to “Priority 1” intelligence target status alongside China, Iran and Russia. The stated purpose is to open fissures within the Cuban political elite and replace “hard-liners” with figures amenable to Washington’s demands, and to “update its options for potential military action against Cuba.”

The last time the agency established a dedicated Cuba task force was 1960; its personnel organized the Bay of Pigs invasion the following April. New Treasury sanctions announced Thursday target five Cuban entities and eight individuals, including subsidiaries of the military conglomerate GAESA, Revolutionary Armed Forces Minister Álvaro López Miera, Chief of the General Staff Roberto Legrá Sotolongo, and Cuba’s military attachés to Russia and China.

  • Cuba’s electrical grid collapsed twice within 24 hours this week; hospitals are without reliable power.
  • UN special rapporteurs warned of conditions approaching a “silent Gaza” and stated that “food must never be used as an instrument of political pressure.”
  • The US military has replaced Joint Task Force-Southern Spear with Joint Task Force-Western Hemisphere.
  • Polling cited by the WSWS shows only 15 percent of Americans support military action against Cuba.
  • Some Trump advisers reportedly believe the Cuban government “could be gone by the time of the midterm election” in November.

Explosive-laden drone found beside Ukrainian cargo plane at Leipzig airport

A drone carrying roughly 800 grams of Semtex and PETN plastic explosive was recovered next to a Ukrainian Antonov An-124 in the cargo security zone at Leipzig/Halle Airport, the principal NATO logistics hub for weapons shipments to Ukraine. The detonator failed. The drone was controlled over the mobile network, allowing it to bypass the airport’s newly installed counter-drone defenses. A DHL cargo aircraft aborted its landing after striking an unidentified object at 400 metres and diverted to Hanover with damage.

German officials immediately attributed the attack to Russia without presenting evidence. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt spoke of a “new quality” of threat and a “hybrid attack scenario,” convening an emergency National Security Council meeting. Green Party security spokesman Konstantin von Notz called Russian responsibility “completely obvious.” Peter Schwarz notes on the WSWS that investigators are reportedly also examining Ukrainian involvement, and that Die Zeit has aired the hypothesis of a staged provocation ahead of East German state elections — the Nord Stream precedent looming over the whole affair.

Taiwan runs blockade drills as Beijing conducts live-fire exercises

Taiwan is conducting August civil-defense exercises simulating a Chinese blockade and invasion, including testing the relocation of weapons production lines and the conversion of civilian factories to military use. For the first time, authorities will deliberately throttle mobile internet speeds nationwide to test crisis communications under degraded conditions.

Beijing conducted a two-day live-fire drill in the Taiwan Strait after President William Lai stated that Taiwan is “not subordinate to the People’s Republic of China,” a remark Chinese officials called “reckless and provocative.” Japan’s newly released Defense White Paper states that China “has intensified its military activities around Taiwan” and warns of the possibility of a China Coast Guard blockade — a formulation that provides the justification for Japan’s own accelerating remilitarisation.

Sudan famine deepens with 24.6 million facing acute hunger

The World Food Programme and IPC report that 24.6 million people in Sudan face acute hunger, with 2 million in famine or at risk of famine. Famine has been formally declared in one area and is threatened in 14 others across Darfur and Kordofan. More than 11.8 million people have been displaced — 7.4 million internally and 4.2 million into neighboring countries.

Aid agencies link the deterioration in part to the global trade disruption from the Iran war and the Hormuz blockade, which has driven up agricultural input and shipping costs across East Africa. Sudan remains the world’s largest humanitarian catastrophe and receives a fraction of the coverage devoted to Gaza or Ukraine, in large measure because no Western government wishes to draw attention to the arms flows sustaining both belligerents.

Turkey’s new bill demands PKK liquidation while offering nothing in return

The Turkish government submitted the “Law on Strengthening National Solidarity and Social Integration” to the Grand National Assembly this week with the signatures of 360 lawmakers, providing the legal machinery for the dissolution and disarmament of the Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK) after four decades of conflict. The bill is backed by the ruling AKP, the far-right MHP and the pro-Kurdish DEM party, but requires 400 votes or a referendum to pass.

Its provisions are narrow. Prosecutions and sentences for PKK membership would be deferred rather than annulled — five years for those sentenced to 15 years or less, ten years for sentences between 15 years and life — and the deferrals take effect only after the National Security Council certifies complete disarmament, with the MIT intelligence agency verifying compliance. There is no general amnesty, no constitutional recognition of Kurdish language rights, and nothing on the release of Abdullah Öcalan. Barış Demir writes on the WSWS that the bill exposes the “peace process” as a mechanism for the liquidation of the Kurdish nationalist movement on Ankara’s terms, undertaken as Erdoğan secures the domestic rear amid the trilateral defense pact and the wider regional war.

Australia prepares first-ever “left-wing terrorism” charges

Australian police and intelligence agencies are preparing terrorism charges against alleged left-wing activists for the first time in the history of the country’s post-2001 terror laws. Operation Braehead concerns an arson attack on July 5 at Lovitt Technologies, a Melbourne machining manufacturer, in which three vehicles were set alight and a building graffitied. If convicted, defendants face life imprisonment.

The Victorian Joint Counter Terrorism Team — comprising the Australian Federal Police, Victoria Police and ASIO — has released blurry surveillance images and is investigating whether “far-left activists with anarchist and revolutionary ideologies” were responsible. An anonymous cell claimed responsibility online, stating it had targeted Lovitt as a “bottleneck” in the Australian supply chain for Lockheed Martin F-35 components used in “colonialism, American imperialism and the arming of Israel.”

ASIO director-general Mike Burgess has publicly grouped together “neo-Nazis,” “Islamic extremism” and “anarchists and revolutionary groups” as sources of antisemitism — the definitional groundwork for prosecuting anti-genocide protest as terrorism, paralleling the UK proscription of Palestine Action. Whatever the character of the alleged act, the invocation of terrorism law against opposition to arms manufacturing establishes a precedent aimed at the working class.

Italy issues red heat alerts for a record 25 cities

Italy’s health ministry issued red-level heat alerts for a record 25 cities as the country entered its fourth heatwave of the summer. The alerts coincide with mounting data on the economic and human toll of climate change across Europe: Germany has recorded 11,900 heat-related deaths through July and France more than 5,700, figures that already exceed initial projections for the continent as a whole.

Wildfires have cost five European countries €3 billion this year, well above the EU’s €2.5 billion annual average, and France temporarily displaced nearly 500,000 people. Grain losses of some 9 million tonnes represent roughly €2 billion, concentrated in France, Hungary and Spain. The European Central Bank projects a 0.3 percent GDP hit in 2025 rising to 0.8 percent annually by 2029 — as much as €126 billion a year. EU member states currently spend €29 billion annually on adaptation against an estimated €70 billion requirement.

More than 20 million pilgrims gather in Karbala

More than 20 million Shia Muslim pilgrims converged on Karbala, Iraq, for the annual commemoration, with substantially higher Iranian participation this year despite the ongoing war and severe travel restrictions. The gathering — one of the largest peaceful assemblies on earth — took place under heavy Iraqi security amid fears of attacks and of spillover from the Iran war into Iraqi territory, where US forces remain deployed.

Tariff wall goes higher on August 19

Senate committee holds Fauci in contempt as outbreaks spread

The Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee voted along party lines Thursday to hold Dr. Anthony Fauci in contempt of Congress. Chairman Rand Paul announced he would hand-deliver the referral to the Justice Department that afternoon, bypassing a full Senate vote. Fauci, who directed the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases for 38 years, had been subpoenaed to testify on July 29; Paul released over 1,100 pages of Fauci’s private diaries days beforehand and ordered Capitol Police to remove Fauci’s attorney from the hearing.

Paul told Fauci that lockdowns “could never have been inflicted without you aiding and abetting the crime.” The proceeding is part of a broader campaign of criminalization directed at public health scientists: epidemiologist David Morens was indicted in April on five counts including conspiracy, Peter Daszak was fired and debarred from federal funding in January 2025, and Kristian Andersen was targeted after his private communications were released.

The contempt vote comes amid multiple uncontrolled outbreaks. A cyclosporiasis outbreak has produced 10,468 confirmed cases across 47 states with more than 12,255 additional cases awaiting confirmation, and Michigan reported the outbreak’s first two deaths this week. A salmonella outbreak linked to jalapeños has infected 345 people across 27 states.

California’s Medi-Cal faces destruction from federal and state cuts

California’s insured rate reached 95 percent two years ago, including coverage for 1.4 million undocumented adults and 217,000 children through Medi-Cal expansion. That is now being systematically dismantled by federal and state action operating in tandem.

  • The One Big Beautiful Bill Act cuts $911 billion from Medicaid over ten years, chiefly through work requirements of 80 documented hours per month for adults aged 19-64.
  • California stands to lose over $30 billion annually in federal funding beginning in 2027; an estimated 1.3 million residents will lose insurance from the federal requirements alone.
  • The state’s own 2025-26 budget cut Medi-Cal by $5 billion and froze undocumented adult enrollment, affecting some 86,000 people. The 2026-27 budget shifts 2 million undocumented immigrants to fee-for-service coverage and eliminates non-emergency care for refugees by 2027 — stripping coverage from roughly 800,000 more.
  • UC Berkeley research projects California’s uninsured rate could approach 15 percent by 2030, with 2.2 million losing coverage over four years.

UAW official suspended two years over death threat against Will Lehman

The court-appointed UAW Monitor, Neil Barofsky, ruled on August 4 that UAW organizer Raymond Jensen Jr. — salary $202,000 — violated federal election rules by posting an AI-generated image on Facebook depicting presidential candidate Will Lehman bound, gagged and bleeding with a rifle pointed at him. Jensen was suspended for two years, stripped of membership and permanently barred from union office.

The image was posted on July 16 from Jensen’s pro-Shawn Fain account. At a July 30 presidential debate, when Lehman confronted Fain over the threat, Fain replied: “If you’re running for president, get ready, brother, because you’ll be getting them every day.” Rank-and-file autoworkers contacted by the WSWS considered the penalty insufficient; a Nexteer committee member said, “Jensen got what he deserved. Although personally, I feel he should be terminated.” Lehman emphasized that “workers face threats and intimidation from the union bureaucracies all the time,” without the legal representation or public platform his campaign has.

US measles cases hit a 35-year high

The CDC’s weekly update added 94 new measles cases, bringing the 2026 US total to 2,465 confirmed cases across 38 distinct outbreaks. Fully 94 percent — 2,309 of 2,465 — are outbreak-associated, meaning sustained community transmission rather than isolated importations. Pennsylvania is the current epicentre with 132 cases, 27 of them diagnosed in the past week.

This is the worst measles year in the United States since 1991. Public health officials expect the country to lose its formal “measles elimination” status when the designation is reviewed later this year — the reversal of a public health achievement declared in 2000, produced by the collapse of vaccination rates under an administration whose health secretary has spent decades attacking immunization.

Australia confirms its first mass H5N1 die-off in wild birds

Testing confirmed H5N1 highly pathogenic avian influenza in a cluster of 49 dead and 35 sick terns off Adelaide — Australia’s first mass mortality event from the virus. By August 5, the agriculture department had logged 101 confirmed wild-bird detections across five states and territories: 58 in South Australia, 10 in Western Australia, seven in Victoria, two in New South Wales and one in Queensland. Authorities warn further spread is likely as the virus establishes itself on a continent previously spared the global panzootic. Australia’s isolation had been the last major barrier to H5N1’s worldwide circulation among wild birds; that barrier has now fallen.

COVID-19 growing in 43 states as “Nimbus” becomes dominant

As of August 5, CDC data show COVID-19 infections growing or likely growing in 43 states and stable in seven, with none declining. The NB.1.8.1 “Nimbus” subvariant is now dominant nationwide. The CDC forecasts weekly hospital admissions climbing from roughly 1,200 to 1,600 by early August. A late-summer wave driven by a newly dominant variant points to a substantial autumn surge, arriving as federal vaccine access has been sharply restricted.

Neuroimaging foundation model trained on 5.24 million scans beats frontier AI

Researchers publishing in Nature Medicine trained “NeuroVFM,” a three-dimensional visual foundation model, on 5.24 million CT and MRI volumes drawn from 566,915 studies accumulated over two decades of routine clinical care at Michigan Medicine. The model achieved state-of-the-art diagnostic performance, generating preliminary radiology reports and triage recommendations, and outperformed frontier models trained on curated public and internet-sourced medical data.

The finding has significant implications: the decisive input for medical AI is not algorithmic novelty but privileged access to the accumulated clinical records of large health systems — data generated by the labour of hundreds of thousands of healthcare workers and the bodies of millions of patients, and now being enclosed as private capital.

EU AI Act’s toughest rules delayed as transparency regime takes effect

As of August 2, the EU AI Act’s Article 50 transparency obligations — chatbot disclosure, synthetic content and deepfake labelling — became enforceable, along with general-purpose AI enforcement powers and a penalty regime of up to €15 million or 3 percent of global turnover. But a July 8 “Digital Omnibus on AI” pushed the Act’s substantive high-risk obligations for Annex III systems back to December 2027 and Annex I product-embedded AI to August 2028, gutting the regulation’s core just as it was to take effect — a straightforward capitulation to industry lobbying.

Arts and culture

UNESCO places Palestinian and Lebanese heritage sites on endangered list

At its 48th session in Busan, South Korea, UNESCO added three heritage sites damaged by Israeli military operations to the List of World Heritage in Danger: the Mount Amel Castles in southern Lebanon, the archaeological site of Sebastia near Nablus in the occupied West Bank, and the ancient city of Tyre in Lebanon.

The Mount Amel group comprises five castles, including Beaufort Castle, constructed between the medieval period and the nineteenth century, which UNESCO describes as having “formed an integrated defensive and administrative network.” Beaufort was seized by the Israeli military in 1982 and held until 2000; it was bombed again in May. Tyre — among the oldest continuously inhabited cities on earth — sustained direct hits on its Roman archaeological zone during the May and June bombardments. At Sebastia, which contains ruins spanning nearly three millennia, Israeli authorities have carried out the largest land appropriation for archaeological purposes in West Bank history, roughly 500 acres.

Israel’s heritage minister dismissed the designation, declaring of Sebastia: “this is the capital of ancient Israel.” The claim is instructive: archaeology is deployed as a title deed, with the material remains of successive Canaanite, Israelite, Hellenistic, Roman, Byzantine, Umayyad and Ottoman civilisations reduced to a nationalist property claim. The destruction of these sites is not collateral damage but the deliberate erasure of the historical record of a people’s continuous presence.

UK local museums face closure wave despite government funding pledge

The Art Newspaper reports that local authority museums across Britain continue to close under council funding pressure, notwithstanding the £1.5 billion “Arts Everywhere” package announced in January and confirmed by the current government. Research commissioned by the charity Kids in Museums found that three in five small museums and galleries fear permanent closure. The Ditchling Museum of Art + Craft secured emergency funding from the Association of Independent Museums, the National Lottery Heritage Fund and local donors to avert closure and undertake a “review and reimagining” of its operating model.

The pattern is consistent across Europe and North America: headline capital grants for prestige national institutions coexist with the systematic starvation of the local, working-class-accessible institutions that constitute most people’s actual encounter with art and history. Meanwhile the sector is subjected to a parallel right-wing campaign — the Daily Sceptic this month attacked the Arts Council for funding “woke” projects while “traditional museums close,” an attempt to redirect anger over austerity into a culture war.

Roundup

  • Sixty years of The Battle of Algiers. Gillo Pontecorvo’s 1966 film — made with Italian Communist Party-adjacent financing and Algerian state cooperation, and studied by both anti-colonial movements and, notoriously, the Pentagon — is receiving anniversary attention. Its enduring power lies in its refusal to sentimentalize either the FLN or the French paratroopers, and in its materialist treatment of colonial violence as a system rather than a moral failing.
  • Violet Hensley, luthier and fiddle player, died August 7 at 109. Hensley built more than 70 fiddles by hand over eight decades and was among the last direct links to a pre-commercial Ozark folk music tradition transmitted entirely through apprenticeship and family.
  • Tony Scott, Surinamese-born Dutch hip house musician, died August 6 at 54 from complications of multiple sclerosis. His 1980s work was central to the emergence of a distinctly Dutch dance music scene shaped by Caribbean migration.
  • Box office: Spider-Man: Brand New Day is projected at $150-160 million in its second weekend across 4,487 theatres, taking a 43 percent showtime market share. The concentration of exhibition capacity behind a single franchise title continues to squeeze out mid-budget and independent releases, with three 2026 romantic comedies grossing under $50 million each.

A “socialism” for the New York Times: Jacobin founder Bhaskar Sunkara’s interview with Ross Douthat — Joseph Kishore

Kishore examines Sunkara’s July interview with New York Times columnist Ross Douthat, arguing that the venue itself is the political message: the ruling class has determined that the DSA is to be domesticated rather than suppressed, and the Times is providing the platform for that operation.

The most revealing passage Kishore isolates is Sunkara’s statement that “my vision of a good society might take centuries to enact, and I’m perfectly comfortable to sit by and wait.” Kishore contrasts this complacency — offered amid genocide, the collapse of democratic forms and preparations for world war — with the actual tempo of the crisis. Sunkara’s programme, described in his own words as “doses of socialism within capitalism” and an expanded welfare state “not unlike Norway,” is the administration of capitalism under a socialist brand name.

Kishore identifies the theoretical formula underpinning DSA practice in Sunkara’s claim that “American socialists are way more liberal than they themselves think… There’s been a sort of reconciliation of liberalism and socialism.” This is offered as sociological observation but functions as programme: the liquidation of the distinction between the working class and the Democratic Party. Kishore draws the parallel to the Communist Party’s Popular Front policy of the 1930s, which subordinated an insurgent working class to Roosevelt and the Democratic Party, with historical consequences that are now legible.

The conclusion: genuine socialism is inseparable from the political independence of the working class and the revolutionary transformation of property relations, not from a programme of reforms explicitly deferred to some future century.

German interior minister exploits Berlin Pride attack to demand police-state expansion — Marianne Arens

Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt has responded to an attack at Berlin Pride by demanding a sweeping expansion of police powers, including extended surveillance authority and data retention. Arens argues that the government’s professed concern for the victims is refuted by the substance of the proposals, which target the democratic rights of the entire population and are being advanced in parallel with the war drive and the Leipzig drone affair. The pattern — an atrocity, an immediate demand for authoritarian measures prepared long in advance — has been repeated after every such incident in Germany over the past decade, with the far-right AfD setting the terms of debate and the established parties implementing its programme.

Sri Lankan educators protest low pay and impossible working conditions — Dayananda Ratnayake, Kapila Fernando

Teachers and principals across Sri Lanka have mounted protests over salaries that have been devastated by inflation and the IMF austerity programme implemented by the National People’s Power (NPP/JVP) government of Anura Kumara Dissanayake. Educators describe class sizes and material shortages that make teaching effectively impossible, and describe the union leaderships as having subordinated them entirely to the government. The article situates the protests within the broader assault on Sri Lankan workers under the IMF programme, and advances the perspective of independent action committees and a turn to the international working class, particularly educators facing identical conditions across South Asia and internationally.

NZ First Party youth leader linked to neo-Nazi groups — John Braddock

Braddock documents connections between the youth leader of New Zealand First — a coalition partner in the National-led government — and openly neo-Nazi organizations. The article places this within the international incubation of far-right forces by the political establishment, noting that NZ First leader Winston Peters has built the party on anti-immigrant agitation while holding the balance of power, and that neither the Labour Party nor the Greens will make any principled fight against it, having themselves normalized the nationalist framework on which such formations feed.

Australia: Multiple fires on Sydney trams in 1 day highlight deepening safety crisis — Leonard Johns

Multiple fires broke out on Sydney trams in a single day, exposing the consequences of privatisation, deferred maintenance and staffing cuts across the New South Wales transport network. Johns connects the incidents to the pattern of infrastructure degradation under both Labor and Liberal state governments and to the role of the transport unions in suppressing opposition from workers who have repeatedly raised safety concerns through official channels without result.

Australian government establishes inquiry to prepare for war — Oscar Grenfell

The Albanese Labor government has established a parliamentary inquiry whose function, Grenfell argues, is not to examine whether Australia should participate in a war with China but to determine how the country’s economy, industry and population are to be organized for one. The inquiry runs in parallel with the AUKUS programme and the integration of Australian forces into US command structures, and dovetails with the preparation of “left-wing terrorism” charges covered elsewhere in today’s WSWS.

Debt-ridden local authorities in Germany plan further social cuts — Lena Sokoll

German municipalities, facing record deficits, are preparing a new round of cuts to social services, libraries, swimming pools, youth facilities and public transport — precisely as federal military spending rises toward historic levels. Sokoll details the mechanism by which the costs of rearmament are transmitted to the local level, where they take the form of closures of the institutions on which working-class communities depend, and notes that the cuts are being implemented by SPD, Green and CDU-led councils alike.

Özgür Özel’s New Party: A defense of Türkiye’s old order — Ulaş Sevinç

Sevinç analyses the new party formation around CHP leader Özgür Özel, arguing that it represents not an opposition to Erdoğan’s regime but a defense of the Kemalist state apparatus and its role in the NATO alliance. The formation is oriented toward sections of the military and state bureaucracy and toward European imperialism, and offers the working class nothing but a change of personnel at the head of the same institutions.

As NATO and Ukraine’s war against Russia escalates, drone attacks on ships in the Black Sea increase — Barış Demir

Demir documents the sharp rise in drone attacks on commercial shipping in the Black Sea, including vessels flagged to third countries, and the corresponding rise in insurance rates and grain export costs. The article emphasizes that the extension of the war to maritime commerce, and the participation of NATO states in targeting intelligence, brings a direct clash between nuclear powers measurably closer.

National Steel Car workers: Reject the corporate/USW “partnership!” — National Steel Car Rank-and-File Committee

Roughly 1,400 workers at National Steel Car in Hamilton, Ontario reached contract expiry on August 8. The company has offered 3 percent in year one and 2 percent thereafter, plus a $3,000 increase in life insurance coverage. The committee’s central demand is the abolition of the piecework “incentive” system, which it characterizes as both wage theft and a lethal safety hazard — naming three workers killed under it: Fraser Cowan, Collin Grayley and Quoc Le, deaths for which the company paid $650,000 in total fines. The committee’s programme: immediate elimination of piecework with base wages set at current incentive rates, a 25 percent wage increase over the contract, and $750 weekly strike pay funded from the USW’s $2 billion in assets, with negotiations placed under rank-and-file control.

Workers in Türkiye defend Will Lehman against death threat from top UAW official — Our reporters

Turkish autoworkers and metalworkers have sent statements of support for Will Lehman, drawing parallels between the UAW apparatus and the Türk Metal union bureaucracy, which has an extensive record of physical violence against oppositional workers. The statements are significant as an expression of the international character of the fight against the union bureaucracies and of the influence of the International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees.

Jacobin / DSA

Branko Marcetic’s “Centrist Dems Really Do Hate the Left More Than Trump” (August 7) argues that the Democratic Party establishment is more hostile to its progressive wing than to the Trump administration. The observation is accurate; the political conclusion drawn from it is not. The article’s implicit premise throughout is that the correct response is a more effective factional struggle within the Democratic Party — better candidates, better messaging, more leverage. The possibility that a party structurally hostile to the left is not a vehicle for the left is never entertained. Marcetic documents the evidence for a break and then declines to draw it.

Paul Heideman’s “The Case for Carceral Minimalism” proposes incremental reduction of the prison system as an alternative to abolitionism. The argument correctly identifies the utopian and often anti-working-class character of abolitionist rhetoric, but substitutes for it a purely administrative programme of policy adjustment, severed from any analysis of the state as an instrument of class rule or of mass incarceration as a response to deindustrialisation and social inequality.

Salar Mohandesi’s “The Left Can’t Win Without an Internationalist Vision” is a striking document: it makes the case for internationalism against nationalist frameworks, in a publication whose practical political orientation is entirely bounded by the American two-party system and whose leading figures have spent a decade arguing that socialists must work within it. The internationalism advocated is moral and cultural rather than organizational — there is no suggestion of an international party.

Joseph Kishore’s article on Sunkara, summarized above, provides the necessary context for reading all of this output. It is worth noting that Jacobin also published Luca Peretti on The Battle of Algiers and Kim Wagner on the 1906 Bud Dajo massacre in the Philippines, in which US forces killed up to 1,000 Moro people — genuinely valuable historical journalism that stands in unresolved tension with the magazine’s practical politics.

Assessment: The day’s output exemplifies the DSA’s division of labour — serious historical and cultural writing that establishes left credentials, alongside strategic commentary that never questions the Democratic Party framework.

Left Voice

Left Voice reproduced a joint declaration with the Revolutionary Workers’ Current (CRT) and Corriente Roja of the Spanish State on the militarization of Spain’s borders, the humanitarian situation of thousands stranded in Ceuta, and the wave of racist agitation. The declaration’s opposition to border militarization is unobjectionable; what is absent is any accounting of the role of Podemos and Sumar — with which the Morenoite tendency’s co-thinkers have repeatedly oriented — in the governments that built this apparatus.

A second piece analyses the Japanese prime minister’s collapsing approval rating and failure to pass economic measures despite a supermajority, alongside her nationalism and rearmament drive, concluding that Japan needs “a strong socialist movement uniting workers and progressive sectors of the youth.” The formulation is the tendency’s signature evasion: a “movement” rather than a party, “progressive sectors” rather than the working class, and no reference whatever to the Fourth International or the problem of revolutionary leadership.

A third article on the trial of Lindsay Clancy, who killed her three children during severe postpartum psychosis, correctly identifies the profit-driven healthcare system as responsible. The framing — that the case demonstrates how capitalism “devalues women’s struggles” — subsumes a question of class and healthcare provision under a gender-identity category.

Assessment: Formally left positions on war and repression, systematically detached from the perspective of building a revolutionary party.

Liberation News / PSL

“Michigan primary: A huge defeat for AIPAC and the Israel Lobby” (August 5) celebrates Abdul El-Sayed’s Democratic primary victory as a blow against Zionism. This is the PSL’s characteristic political method: a candidate of the party that armed and financed the genocide is presented as its opponent, on the basis of campaign rhetoric. The framing also relies on the “Israel lobby” thesis — that US Middle East policy is driven by a lobby rather than by the material interests of American imperialism — which functions to shield the US state itself from indictment and has an unmistakable affinity with conspiracist explanations of foreign policy.

“Counting the horrific waste of the U.S. war machine” (August 6) tallies military spending against unmet social needs. The data is useful; the framing of imperialist war as “waste” — an irrational misallocation correctable by better budgeting — obscures its function as the necessary expression of the crisis of the profit system.

“One month after Lorenzo’s murder, the fight for justice continues” (August 7) covers the killing of Lorenzo Salgado Araujo by ICE agents on July 7. The reporting is valuable; the perspective advanced is protest pressure on Democratic officials rather than the independent industrial and political mobilization of the working class against the deportation machine.

Assessment: Militant presentation in the service of a political orientation to the Democratic Party’s left flank and to nationalist framings of imperialism.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

Adam Booth’s “Another government, same system — Britain needs a revolution!” responds to the installation of the Burnham government by insisting that structural transformation, not electoral change, is required. The formulation is correct in the abstract and empty in practice: the article offers no accounting of the IMT’s own orientation to the Labour Party over decades, nor any concrete perspective beyond recruitment.

Jack Tye Wilson’s “Stand Up to Opportunism — Why the SWP gets everything wrong on racism” polemicizes against the Socialist Workers Party’s Popular Frontist anti-fascism. The criticism of the SWP’s cross-class alliances is accurate as far as it goes, but the RCP counterposes to it a nationally framed “communism” without any reference to the Fourth International or to the historical struggle against Stalinism.

Assessment: Revolutionary phraseology combined with an organizational perspective that remains national and adapted to the existing labour bureaucracy.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. The Mecca pact and the nuclearization of the Middle East

The first formal extension of a nuclear umbrella to an Arab state, agreed between Riyadh, Ankara and Islamabad in direct response to the US-Israeli war on Iran, marks a qualitative escalation in regional militarization. The pact reflects the disintegration of the postwar security architecture and the drive of regional bourgeoisies to acquire independent great-power capabilities — a process that makes nuclear conflict measurably more likely and which no section of the pseudo-left has addressed.

2. Record ICE arrests as family detention reopens

ICE arrested more than 51,000 people in July, the highest monthly figure on record, with family-unit deportations up over 20 percent in August and family detention facilities reopened at Dilley and Karnes. The mainstream press is normalizing this as routine enforcement statistics. The connection to Trump’s new birthright citizenship orders — building a legally stateless class within the United States — requires sustained treatment, as does the role of Democratic-run states and municipalities in cooperating with the apparatus.

3. Labor force participation at a five-year low: the hidden collapse in US employment

The July jobs report’s headline unemployment “improvement” to 4.1 percent was produced entirely by workers exiting the labour force, with participation falling to 61.4 percent. Combined with the 103,000 downward revision to May and June and the slowest wage growth since 2021, the data indicate a labour market deteriorating far faster than the official rate suggests — while equity indices set records on precisely that news.

4. Sudan: 24.6 million facing acute hunger as the world looks away

Famine has been declared in one Sudanese region and threatens 14 others, with 11.8 million displaced, and conditions are being worsened by the Hormuz-driven rise in global food and shipping costs. The near-silence of the Western press is directly connected to the involvement of the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Western arms manufacturers in sustaining both belligerents. This is the largest humanitarian catastrophe on earth and warrants a sustained series.

5. The Thailand school shooting and the globalization of social breakdown

A 14-year-old killed seven people at a school outside Bangkok after killing his grandparents, having spent the preceding days watching footage of American school shootings. The WSWS has developed the most serious analysis anywhere of US mass shootings as a symptom of social disintegration; the international transmission of the phenomenon demands the same treatment, against the inevitable framing in terms of individual pathology or social media contagion.

6. Australia’s H5N1 mass mortality event closes the last gap in the global panzootic

7. Medical AI and the enclosure of clinical data

The NeuroVFM model published in Nature Medicine demonstrates that decades of routine patient scans held by a single health system produce better diagnostic AI than any publicly trained frontier model. This raises directly the question of who owns the data generated by patients’ bodies and healthcare workers’ labour, and whether the resulting diagnostic capacity will be deployed to improve care or to eliminate radiology jobs and ration treatment.
