1. AI rout erases the Huang rally as Anthropic sinks below its IPO price

The rally set off by Jensen Huang’s return to Nvidia on Monday was the biggest in US markets in 18 months, and it collapsed within a day. On Tuesday the Nasdaq fell 1.48 percent and the S&P 500 lost 0.50 percent, while the Philadelphia semiconductor index dropped 7.9 percent. Nvidia, up 31.5 percent on Monday, fell 5.9 percent. Micron lost 11 percent, AMD about 9 percent and Broadcom about 4 percent. The selling spread to Asia overnight. Markets Insider reported that South Korea’s KOSPI fell as much as 7 percent on Wednesday, Taiwan’s Taiex as much as 9 percent and Japan’s Nikkei as much as 4 percent. At 2:16 a.m. ET, Nasdaq futures were down 2 percent.

Anthropic, the first frontier AI company to go public, became the clearest symbol of the reversal. Reuters reported that the stock fell 12.6 percent to $76.60, below its $83 IPO price and well under its $111.50 opening on November 19. That put it on course to erase close to $200 billion in market value. “If we do see a slowdown in the data-center buildout, then that obviously can impact the speed at which Anthropic can develop and therefore future revenue and therefore its valuation,” said StoneX analyst Fiona Cincotta. Reuters noted that last week’s selloff had already pushed the S&P 500 and the Dow into confirmed correction territory.

Key facts: - Asian chipmakers (Wednesday): Samsung fell 10 percent and SK Hynix 11 percent. Kioxia dropped 16 percent, Advantest 15 percent and TSMC about 9 percent before paring losses. SoftBank Group fell as much as 17 percent to a 52-week low. It is down nearly 70 percent from its June peak. - Rates: Reuters notes that rising interest rates threaten to raise the cost of AI infrastructure further. The Fed is widely expected to raise rates at its December 9 meeting (see World economy). - UBS’s Mark Haefele predicted a widening gap between AI firms with “clear paths to profitability” and those “relying on debt-fueled investments.”

2. Houthis strike Riyadh’s financial district and Ras Tanura as the Gulf war widens

On Tuesday, a strike on a tower in Riyadh’s financial district wounded 11 people, according to Saudi civil defence. It was the second hit on the same tower in less than a week. The November 19 strike had damaged the headquarters of Saudi Arabia’s Public Investment Fund but caused no injuries. Maj. Gen. Turki al-Maliki called Tuesday’s attack “cowardly” and “terrorist.” On Wednesday, Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the group had attacked the Ras Tanura refinery, the kingdom’s largest and part of its main Gulf crude-export complex, and that the strike caused fires. He said the financial district had been attacked “in three phases.” The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted four drones over Ras Tanura. India condemned the Riyadh strike, which wounded two Indian nationals. ABC News describes the Yemen front as an “intensified” war between Saudi-backed Yemeni forces and the Houthis, who have repeatedly hit Saudi military, oil and transport sites.

In the Strait of Hormuz, CENTCOM rejected the IRGC’s claim that three tankers had hit mines on Monday. It said US forces had cleared all internationally recognized primary shipping lanes. UKMTO reported that three vessels were struck by “unidentified projectiles” while sailing east through the strait, and that all continued their voyages. The IRGC said Tuesday it had targeted a container ship on an “unauthorised” route. The US moved a third aircraft carrier, the USS Theodore Roosevelt, into the Gulf last week to enforce the blockade of Iranian ports.

Key facts: - Sanctions: On Monday, Treasury sanctioned more than two dozen Iranian exchange houses, front companies and officials under “Operation Economic Outcast.” Secretary Scott Bessent called Iran’s shadow banking system “the last financial lifeline of a bankrupt regime.” - Cyberwar: According to the WSJ and ABC, Washington is investigating Iran as the prime suspect in cyberattacks on as many as 50 energy tankers, some of which received bomb threats. Russia is also under investigation. - Diplomacy: A US–Iran memorandum of understanding signed in June has produced nothing, and talks remain stalled. Vance and Rubio met Qatar’s prime minister separately at the White House on Monday. - Tehran’s line: Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei called the blockade “military aggression” and said Iran “has the right to use any available tool to defend itself.”

3. Army clears way to detonate 9,600 pounds of lead azide in Iowa on Thanksgiving Eve

The Army will blow up about 9,600 pounds of degraded lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown at 9 a.m. CT today, the day before Thanksgiving. The explosive is held in more than 60 barrels in two earth-covered bunkers. On Monday, Trump said the blast would not go ahead unless Democratic Governor-elect Rob Sand approved it, and he threatened to hold Sand responsible for “any and all death or destruction.” Within hours an Eighth Circuit panel lifted its hold and reinstated the EPA permit, rejecting environmental groups’ request for an emergency stay. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle then announced the “unanimous judgement” of federal agencies to proceed with “an extensively mitigated, carefully controlled detonation.” He said “a controlled mitigated detonation is far preferable… than an uncontrolled detonation, which is a worst-case scenario.”

FEMA’s cellphone alert said it will contact residents within 5 miles for evacuation or relocation help, and it told people 5 to 20 miles out to stay indoors with their windows closed. The New Republic reports that plumes of lead dust could travel as far as 100 miles, settling on crops and the Mississippi River. Officials told Iowa Public Radio that the full hazard can only be measured after the blast. Since 2008 the plant has been run by American Ordnance LLC, a subsidiary of defense contractor Day & Zimmermann. As Benjamin Mateus wrote on the WSWS, “Every institutional check has collapsed, and the capitalist state is preparing to disperse a toxic cloud across working-class communities.”

Key facts: - Public announcement: The plan was made public only five days before its original date (CNN). - Neutralization ruled out: Officials say the material is too sensitive to move or to neutralize chemically, because that would mean handling the drums (TNR). - Illinois state agencies have launched an information site and put agencies on alert (Chicago Tribune). - Political alignment: Sand handed the decision back to Washington. Outgoing Republican Governor Kim Reynolds called it a “federal operation.”

4. Israel kills Hamas security chief as aid-worker death toll in Gaza reaches 600

On Tuesday, Israeli airstrikes killed Naeem Abu Nahel, director of Hamas’s internal security apparatus in Gaza, along with six others, two of them police officers. Abu Nahel was killed in western Gaza City. A drone strike on a police post on Street 5 in al-Mawasi, Khan Younis, killed three and wounded five. Another strike near the Netzarim corridor killed three more. The Israeli military and Shin Bet said they had struck “a senior Hamas militant.” On Monday, a strike near a water well in Jabalia camp killed a UNRWA staff member and wounded three others. OCHA said that brings the number of aid workers killed in Gaza since October 2023 to 600, of them 397 UNRWA personnel.

Gaza’s Health Ministry says Israeli fire since the October 10, 2025 ceasefire has killed 1,675 Palestinians and wounded 6,095. Heavy rain has flooded more than 70,000 tents. UN Deputy Secretary-General Amina Mohammed said Palestinians are not receiving “the safety, assistance or recovery they need.” Two senior Democratic senators asked Rubio to press Israel to let in more winter supplies.

Key facts: - Military aid: On November 3, Trump said the $3.8 billion in annual military aid would end this year. In a pre-recorded interview, Ambassador Mike Huckabee said the aid “is supposed to continue for two more years, so I’m not certain that there will be a cessation on an abrupt basis” (Reuters). - Aid blockade: The Christian Science Monitor reports that relief is held up by Israel’s “dual-use” import restrictions. Under international pressure, Israel has promised to speed up delayed sewage repairs (Times of Israel).

5. EU proposes 22nd Russia sanctions package as Washington drops business incentives for Moscow

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen has proposed the EU’s 22nd sanctions package against Russia. It would cut 60 Russian banks off SWIFT, set up a crypto sanctions task force that could reach third countries helping Moscow evade sanctions, and blacklist 70 more shadow-fleet vessels while barring all such tankers from EU ports. It would also bar Europeans from top posts in Russian companies, ban Russian teams and athletes from competing under their flag, and deny visas to Russians who have fought in Ukraine. “Russian ex-combatants pose a security risk to Europe,” she said. All 27 member states must approve the package.

The Financial Times reports that Washington has quietly shelved proposals to offer Russia business deals in energy and aviation as an incentive to end the war. Envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner told European officials that corporate tie-ups were no longer central to the US pitch. “It was made pretty clear that the Russians were not in the market for this kind of thing,” one official said. Kushner had floated a US investor in Nord Stream and US investment in Russian oil and titanium.

Key facts: - Zaporizhzhia: Governor Mykhailo Semikin said an overnight attack on an apartment building killed two people and wounded 40, among them four children. An earlier Reuters headline gave 22 wounded. - Black Sea: At the UN Security Council, Russia’s envoy said “we will not accept any new grain deal,” calling it “Ukrainian food in exchange for Western weapons.” He said Ukraine had targeted more than 200 commercial vessels since July, killing or wounding 12 seafarers. - Zelensky asked partners for more Patriot interceptors, saying “every missile for our Patriot systems represents a setback for Russia.” - War talk: Latvian President Edgars Rinkevics told Reuters the war could reach a “tipping point” next year that leads to Russia’s defeat. Poland’s intelligence chief Tomasz Siemoniak said a peace deal would not end Russian sabotage in Europe.

6. Belgian general strike grounds all flights as De Wever sets December 31 budget deadline

Belgium’s three union federations, the socialist FGTB-ABVV, the Christian CSC and the liberal Synova, held a national strike on Monday that halted all commercial flights. Air traffic controller Skeyes said it could not provide regular service, and only about 25 of Charleroi’s 429 scheduled flights operated. Public transport, schools, universities, the post, health services and refuse collection were disrupted or shut down. Police estimated 50,000 marchers in Brussels and the unions said about 70,000. Their placards read “The rich earn more, workers pay more!” and “BDW – Basta!” “The burden is being placed on the population as a whole, while… the richest 1% of the population, is being let off the hook,” teacher Philippe Naime told Reuters.

The same day, Prime Minister Bart De Wever reported to King Philippe for the third time in a month that budget talks had failed. He offered his resignation on November 2, and the king refused it. His office now says that without a budget by year-end “the government will effectively be unable to govern, thereby bringing its existence to an end.” De Wever wants €10 billion in savings. The Flemish socialist party Vooruit is holding out for higher wealth taxes to soften the healthcare and welfare cuts that its four coalition partners have accepted. WSWS reporters noted that demonstrators linked the cuts and soaring rents to the billions De Wever’s government is spending on rearmament.

Key facts: - Debt: The Commission forecasts Belgium’s debt at 113.4 percent of GDP next year. In the eurozone only Greece, Italy and France are higher. - Bond market: The Belgian 10-year yield stood at 4.25 percent on Monday, up from 3.48 percent five months earlier (France 4.79 percent, Germany 3.29 percent).

Eisenkot and Lieberman sign the first coalition deal in a bid to replace Netanyahu

Former army chief Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar party (21 seats) signed a coalition agreement with Avigdor Lieberman’s far-right Yisrael Beiteinu (7 seats). It is the first formal step toward an alternative government since the October 27 election left no bloc with a majority. Netanyahu’s Likud-led bloc holds 53 seats, short of the 61 needed. Eisenkot’s existing partners hold 48 and the Arab parties 19. Lieberman rejects any government that depends on Arab parties and also refuses a unity government with Netanyahu, and he could seek the defense ministry. Yair Golan’s Democrats suspended their own talks with Eisenkot in protest at Yashar’s parallel negotiations with Likud. Reports point to a December 6 deadline for a unity government, failing which there would be another election in 2027. Eisenkot has also clashed publicly with Netanyahu over the conduct of strikes on Iran (Fox News). The contest is between rival commanders of the same war, not over whether to wage it.

Solomon Islands makes Australia its principal security partner, shutting out China

Australia and Solomon Islands signed a treaty on Wednesday that makes Canberra Honiara’s “defence partner of choice.” It reverses the 2022 security pact with Beijing, which had alarmed Washington. Australia will fund the expansion of the Solomons’ police and border force, along with infrastructure and education, and will ease visas. Honiara will refuse any foreign request to set up military or security bases and must consult Australia on critical infrastructure. “Solomon Islands has been for a number of years a source of uncertainty and doubt in the region. Those days are done,” Prime Minister Matthew Wale said. The treaty follows similar Australian pacts with Papua New Guinea, Vanuatu and Fiji, part of the effort to lock the South Pacific into the US-led war preparations against China.

Victorian Labor polls 20 percent ahead of Saturday’s state election

The final Resolve Political Monitor before Saturday’s Victorian election, published in The Age, puts Labor’s primary vote at 20 percent. That could be its worst result since Federation, and it leaves the party running third. “Labor’s primary vote is at a historic low,” said Resolve’s Jim Reed. Premier Ben Carroll is projected to lose his own seat, and The Conversation says Labor risks losing even its status as official opposition, including in the seats of former premiers. The far-right One Nation is contesting seriously, and its state leader Warren Pickering faces a close race. About 1.5 million people had voted by Tuesday. Labor has governed Victoria for all but four years since 1999. The WSWS’s Morgan Peach details its record of privatization and austerity, and the Socialist Equality Party is standing candidates (see WSWS coverage).

China-linked network targets Taiwan’s local elections with AI deepfakes

BBC Verify found that a China-linked network has put out more than 500 fake videos about the ruling Democratic Progressive Party in the past year. They include the false claim that President Lai Ching-te holds a US green card and would flee in a war, and deepfakes of Taipei mayoral candidate Puma Shen using drugs. Vanderbilt University researchers, reported by the New York Times, found “traditional, hybrid and AI-powered inauthentic activity.” Earlier this month Taiwan’s National Security Bureau flagged about 2,700 accounts to Meta. The Taipei mayoralty has traditionally been a springboard to the presidency, and the incumbent, Chiang Wan-an, belongs to the Beijing-leaning KMT. Coverage of the campaign is framed entirely around Beijing’s interference and leaves out US military integration of the island.

Indonesian candy factory fire kills 26 workers trapped near the emergency exit

Indonesian authorities confirmed on Tuesday that 26 workers died in Saturday’s fire at the PT Fonusa Agung Mulia candy factory in Serang district, Banten province, near Jakarta. Eight are still missing. Rescuers said most victims died on the second floor as they tried to reach an emergency staircase close to where the fire is believed to have started. To get there, workers had to make their way through corridors and turns. “Some victims were found clustered together, apparently trapped while attempting to flee,” AP reported, and elevator problems may have slowed the evacuation. More than 600 rescuers are searching the burned three-story building. No official has yet said who designed the exit routes or when the plant was last inspected.

Washington readies new Section 301 tariffs on 16 trading partners

Reuters reports that the US Trade Representative is about to release the findings of its Section 301 investigation into “excess industrial capacity” in 16 major trading partners, and to propose new duties tailored to each country. They would stack on top of existing second-term tariffs and partly replace those the Supreme Court struck down in February. Mexico and Norway are unlikely to face immediate levies. Washington is expected to honor the tariff caps written into its trade deals: 15 percent for the EU, Japan and South Korea, 19 percent for Malaysia, and 20 percent for China, the last only if the trade truce expiring January 10 is extended. Former Commerce official Ryan Majerus expects rates of 15 to 25 percent. Fear of new tariffs weighed on European stocks, led by Italy. The move comes despite a post-midterm Reuters/Ipsos poll in which 62 percent of respondents backed repealing the tariffs.

Wisconsin reports its third measles death as the US toll passes 14

On Tuesday, Wisconsin’s health department reported the state’s third measles death in under three weeks, an adult in Lafayette County. The earlier deaths, announced November 5 and 13, were an infant and a child. Before this outbreak, Wisconsin had not recorded a measles death since 1990. The state added 88 cases since Friday, for 920 this year, all but two since August. Of these cases, 99.2 percent were unvaccinated or of unknown status, and 20 were hospitalized. State health departments have now reported at least 14 measles deaths in four states this year, while the CDC’s site listed six as of last Thursday. The agency says it is still “developing a standardized case definition” for measles deaths.

The CDC’s 5,838 confirmed cases are the highest annual total since 1991 (9,643) and about 2.5 times 2025’s 2,289. About a quarter are tied to outbreaks that began in 2025. Last week PAHO declared that the United States has lost its measles elimination status, after one strain circulated continuously from January 20, 2025. Pennsylvania’s outbreak, with more than 1,600 cases, is the largest in the country. Kindergarten vaccination rates are below the 95 percent needed for herd protection. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said only that he continues “to recommend MMR vaccination.”

It reports Hegseth’s boast that Venezuela has gone “from our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner.” It calls the November 18 pact “subordination under duress” and an end to the “anti-imperialist phase” begun by Chávez. The vote is a real sign of hostility to the Democrats in the poorest congressional district in the country. But its “socialism” is a national-reformist program that has nothing to say on internationalism or the war.
  • A companion feature on North Carolina claims the movement “defeated the Trump agenda on Election Day,” which credits the Democrats’ midterm sweep to “the movement.”

Assessment: The PSL’s Stalinist “anti-imperialism” leaves it disoriented by the capitulation of its Venezuelan model, while its electoral work moves between opposing the Democrats and celebrating their wins.

  • Hundreds made homeless in Newham due to flats “at risk of sudden collapse”. What the WSWS should cover today

1. Twenty-six workers burned alive trying to reach the exit of an Indonesian candy factory

Most of the PT Fonusa Agung Mulia victims died clustered on the second floor, trying to reach a staircase near where the fire began. Eight are still missing. It is the latest in a long line of deadly factory fires in Asia’s low-wage export manufacturing, and an article could connect it to the Flatbush collapse and other deaths caused by employer and regulatory negligence that the WSWS has covered.

Recommended reading AP Most victims of factory fire died trying to reach emergency exit, rescuers say

2. Anthropic’s $200 billion slide and the unraveling of the AI bubble

The first frontier AI company to go public fell below its IPO price within four trading sessions, while SoftBank is down nearly 70 percent from June. It comes as the Fed prepares to raise rates. The WSWS has analyzed Huang’s reinstatement, and a follow-up could examine the fragility of the financial edifice the AI boom has built, including hyperscaler spending above $700 billion, and the consequences for jobs and pensions.

Recommended reading Reuters Anthropic shares tumble below $83 IPO price, on track to lose nearly $200 billion in value

3. Justice Department admits ICE deported a US citizen

Brian José Morales García was handed to ICE by local police and deported to Mexico, even though he told them his birth certificate was at home. CBP had publicly denied arresting a citizen. The case shows that the deportation machine treats citizenship as no protection, and it ties directly to the WSWS’s coverage of Chloe Freeman’s prosecution and ICE operations.

Recommended reading The Hill US citizen deported to Mexico lawsuit

4. Houthi strikes on Riyadh and Ras Tanura: the Iran war reaches the heart of Saudi finance and oil

The PIF tower has been hit twice in a week, the kingdom’s largest refinery has been targeted, and CENTCOM and the IRGC are trading claims over the mining of Hormuz. A third US carrier has entered the Gulf. An article could examine how the war is engulfing the Gulf monarchies while Washington’s blockade and “Operation Economic Outcast” tighten.

Recommended reading Reuters Yemen’s Houthis say they attacked Saudi oil refinery and Riyadh financial district

5. New Section 301 tariffs: Washington escalates the trade war against allies and rivals alike

Sixteen trading partners, including the EU, Japan, South Korea and China, face new tailored duties of perhaps 15 to 25 percent. A Reuters/Ipsos poll shows 62 percent of Americans want tariffs repealed. This would tie in with Tom Hall’s exposure of the UAW’s and Teamsters’ support for the trade war.

Recommended reading Reuters Trump administration set to unveil new tariffs after probe on excess factory capacity

6. Solomon Islands treaty completes Australia’s Pacific lockout of China

After PNG, Vanuatu and Fiji, Honiara has now agreed to refuse all foreign bases other than its partners’ and to consult Canberra on infrastructure. The Albanese government is drawing the South Pacific into the US war front against China while staging terrorism raids at home. This would fit the Australian SEP’s current election campaign material.

Recommended reading Reuters Australia, Solomon Islands sign security pact in blow to China

7. Vance, Vitol and the plunder of Venezuelan oil

A Senate committee is investigating whether the vice president steered a prized Venezuelan oil contract to a major donor. The probe comes after the January 3 seizure of Maduro and the new US–Venezuela military pact. An article could document who has profited from the operation. The Democrats’ framing as a donor-corruption scandal leaves the imperialist operation itself unchallenged.

Recommended reading Reuters JD Vance’s ties to prized Venezuelan oil contract under investigation

8. US measles deaths reach at least 14 as the CDC lags behind the states

Wisconsin has had three deaths in under three weeks and Pennsylvania nine, and the US has lost its elimination status, while the CDC lists only six deaths. A health article could document the collapse of vaccination coverage and public health reporting under Kennedy.
