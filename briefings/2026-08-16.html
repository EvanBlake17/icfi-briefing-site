1. Israeli airstrikes kill 11 in southern Lebanon

Israeli warplanes struck villages across southern Lebanon on Saturday, killing at least 11 people, in the deadliest single day of attacks since the June 20 arrangement that nominally reduced hostilities between Israel and Hezbollah. Lebanon’s Health Ministry reported seven killed when a house in the village of Ansar was hit, and four more killed in a strike on Deir El Zahrani. Nineteen people were wounded.

The Israeli military said the attacks were retaliation for a Hezbollah operation against its troops in southern Lebanon and confirmed that it had killed Ali Samir Al-Haj Hassan, described as a commander in Hezbollah’s Radwan Force, near Ansar. Hezbollah said the strikes would be “met with what they deserve.” The escalation comes as Israeli forces remain in occupied positions inside Lebanese territory and as the Gaza “ceasefire” enters its eleventh month with a continuing death toll.

Key facts:

  • Among the seven killed in Ansar were three children and two women, according to the Lebanese Health Ministry.
  • This was the most substantial escalation since mid-June and the deadliest attack since the June 20 agreement took effect.
  • In Gaza, 1,259 Palestinians have been killed and 4,146 wounded by Israeli attacks between the October 10, 2025 ceasefire and August 11, 2026, according to UN figures; roughly 160 were killed in July alone.
  • At least 300 children in Gaza have been killed in the 300 days since the ceasefire nominally began, the UN reported this month.
  • The Palestinian National Committee for the Administration of Gaza, the technocratic body created to oversee reconstruction, has still not been permitted to enter the territory.

2. Iran announces Hormuz “safe route” deal with Oman as third ADNOC tanker is hit in 48 hours

Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei announced Saturday that “Iran and Oman have reached an agreement on a safe route for vessels transiting through the Strait of Hormuz.” The announcement came within hours of the Emirates News Agency reporting that a vessel belonging to the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company had been targeted by Iranian forces in the strait — the third strike on an ADNOC ship in 48 hours. The UK Maritime Trade Operations reported an “unknown projectile” hitting the hull of a bulk carrier in the same waterway.

The UAE Foreign Ministry accused the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of “piracy” and of using the strait as a “tool of economic coercion and blackmail,” calling attacks on commercial shipping a “direct threat” to the region. The US naval blockade of Iran, reimposed in July after the collapse of the April ceasefire and June memorandum of understanding on safe passage, remains in place. Mohsen Rezaei, military adviser to Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, said Iran’s armed forces “will not stand idly by” while the blockade continues.

Key facts:

  • Sixteen of the 18 projectile-strike incidents recorded by UKMTO since July 6 occurred on the southern Omani route, the alternative corridor opened when the main channel became impassable.
  • An LNG tanker carrying Qatari cargo was among vessels reported hit; another tanker was struck by an unknown projectile near Oman.
  • A 25 percent US tariff announced in January 2026 remains in force on countries continuing to trade with Iran, hitting China and India directly.
  • The war began with US-Israeli air strikes on February 28, 2026 aimed at regime change; Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed in those strikes.
  • The USS Abraham Lincoln has been kept at sea more than six months and is approaching the record for the longest US carrier deployment away from home port since the Vietnam War.

3. DR Congo Ebola epidemic becomes largest ever recorded

The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo has become the largest ever recorded in that country and is expanding faster than any previous Ebola epidemic anywhere. As of August 12, the World Health Organization reported 4,665 confirmed cases in the DRC, with 2,184 deaths — a crude case fatality ratio of 46.8 percent. Twenty cases have been reported in Uganda and one in France.

WHO also disclosed this week that the outbreak began in February, well before it was formally declared, with early cases misattributed to malaria and typhoid — a delay directly traceable to the collapsed state of public health infrastructure in eastern Congo, a region shattered by decades of imperialist plunder and proxy war. The strain involved is Bundibugyo ebolavirus, for which there is no licensed vaccine and no specific treatment; WHO is urging a trial of the Ervebo vaccine, developed against the Zaire strain.

Key facts:

  • The outbreak has spread from a single health zone in Ituri Province to 54 health zones across six provinces: Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu, Haut-Uélé, Tshopo and Bas-Uélé.
  • WHO describes the epidemic as being in “a phase of intense transmission.”
  • A new treatment centre opened at the epicentre in early August, reported by UN News, months into uncontrolled spread.
  • Cases topped 4,000 on August 7, meaning several hundred additional confirmed infections in under a week.

4. Midwest floods shatter 113-year record as Indiana requests federal disaster aid

A relentless stretch of thunderstorms has produced catastrophic flooding across the Midwest, with Indiana and Ohio hardest hit and more than 11 inches of rain falling in some areas over the past week. Indiana’s White River crested at 24.93 feet, breaking the 23.6-foot record set in 1913, after what meteorologists classified as a 1-in-1,000-year rainfall event. Governor Mike Braun issued a statewide disaster declaration and requested a FEMA emergency declaration.

Six people have been reported killed in Indiana from the storms and flooding, with at least three additional deaths reported across the wider region and more than 150,000 customers without power as of Saturday afternoon. Additional heavy rainfall was forecast through Sunday across the Ohio Valley, Midwest and central Appalachians.

Key facts:

  • Mass water rescues were carried out across central Indiana; the state’s disaster declaration covers the full state.
  • Reporting on the disaster notes that many affected properties fall outside FEMA-designated floodplains, leaving owners without flood insurance — a structural failure of federal flood mapping as extreme precipitation events intensify.
  • The disaster follows the Trump administration’s repeated moves to shrink FEMA’s role and shift disaster costs onto states.

5. Court filings expose DHS mass surveillance of unions and left-wing groups in Minnesota

Court documents filed Thursday and reported through the weekend reveal that the Department of Homeland Security ran a sweeping undercover surveillance operation against labor unions, left-wing organizations and immigrant-defense groups in Minnesota. The program, code-named “Operation Puppet Master,” was launched in the aftermath of the killing of nurse Alex Pretti by ICE agents in Minneapolis in January and the mass protests that followed.

Undercover Homeland Security Investigations agents posed as protesters and activists, attending meetings at the United Labor Center in Minneapolis, in Loring Park, at Augsburg Park Library, St. John’s Church, Zion Lutheran Church and a boxing class at University Baptist Church, secretly making audio and video recordings. Agents also infiltrated private group chats and obtained financial records of organizations never accused of any crime.

Key facts:

  • Targets included the Communications Workers of America, SEIU, the AFL-CIO and the Sunrise Movement.
  • DHS internally described the targets as a network of conspirators aiding “violent opportunists and agitators.”
  • The revelations follow a ProPublica investigation finding that more than a third of some 300 prosecutions of anti-ICE protesters have collapsed.
  • The WSWS, in Jacob Crosse’s account of the documents, situates the operation in the historical line of COINTELPRO and stresses that the unions’ own officials have offered no fight against the spying.

6. CEO pay hits record as S&P 500 ratio reaches 312-to-1

The AFL-CIO’s 2026 Executive Paywatch report, released this week, found that S&P 500 chief executives took home an average of $22.8 million in 2025, a 21 percent increase over the $18.9 million average in 2024 and the highest level recorded in three decades of tracking. The ratio of CEO pay to the median US worker’s wage rose from 285-to-1 to 312-to-1.

Those figures exclude Elon Musk’s $158.3 billion Tesla compensation package, which by itself was 14 times the combined total compensation of every other S&P 500 CEO. Including Musk, average S&P 500 CEO pay was $340.1 million — a 1,700 percent increase over 2024. Musk’s package amounted to 2,522,203 times the pay of Tesla’s median employee.

Key facts:

  • The report covers 2025 compensation, disclosed under SEC pay-ratio rules that corporate lobbies continue to press to weaken.
  • The surge coincides with continued mass layoffs in tech, manufacturing and logistics, and with the erosion of real wages under tariff-driven price increases.
  • Tom Hall’s WSWS analysis stresses that the AFL-CIO publishes these figures annually while its own apparatus enforces the concession contracts that produce the ratio.

7. Three states carry out executions on a single day, a first since 2010

Tennessee, Alabama and Oklahoma each carried out an execution on Thursday, August 13 — the first time in nearly 16 years that three executions took place in the United States on a single day. Robin Maher, executive director of the Death Penalty Information Center, told reporters the convergence was “largely a scheduling coincidence,” but it occurs against a background of sharply accelerating state killing.

Eleven states executed 47 people in 2025, the highest annual total since 2009, with Florida alone accounting for 19. Nineteen executions have been carried out so far in 2026 across four states, including a double execution in Florida in July.

Key facts:

  • The last three-executions-in-one-day occurred in 2010.
  • Kate Randall’s WSWS analysis argues the clustering is not coincidental but reflects a deliberate campaign by state governments, encouraged by the Trump administration’s push to expand capital punishment, to normalize state killing as an instrument of authoritarian rule.

Colombia’s new right-wing government recognizes Israeli annexation of the Golan

President Abelardo de la Espriella’s government has recognized Israeli sovereignty over the occupied Syrian Golan Heights, making Colombia the second UN member state after the United States to do so. The declaration, one of the first major foreign-policy acts of the new administration, cited “regional instability” and the strategic importance of the plateau to Israeli security. The move came as Colombia continues to dig out from the magnitude 7.4 earthquake of August 10, the most powerful to strike the country this century, which devastated Cali, Pereira, Quibdó and Manizales and has killed at least 265 people. Washington has committed $15.5 million in aid and dispatched disaster-response teams; de la Espriella has authorized US military operations on Colombian soil in connection with the relief effort, a step the WSWS’s Andrea Lobo characterizes as using the catastrophe to entrench a US military foothold in the Andes following the abduction of Nicolás Maduro from Caracas.

White House claims credit for crime decline as National Guard occupation of Washington passes one year

The White House is promoting new statistics showing violent crime fell sharply last year as evidence of a “Trump effect,” a claim criminologists reject on the grounds that the decline began well before Trump’s return to office. The publicity push coincides with the one-year mark of the federalized National Guard deployment to Washington, DC, launched in August 2025. About 2,400 Guard members remain in the District and will stay through the end of 2026.

Federal troops were withdrawn from Los Angeles, Chicago and Portland in January 2026 following adverse court rulings, but forces remain in Memphis and Washington under separate arrangements. The ACLU marked the anniversary by arguing that the deployment underscores the case for DC statehood — a framing that obscures the central issue: the establishment of a precedent for the standing use of the military in domestic policing, which no change in the District’s constitutional status would reverse.

US measles cases reach 2,566, the most in over three decades

The CDC reported 2,566 confirmed measles cases in the United States for 2026 as of August 13, across 47 jurisdictions — the highest annual total in more than 30 years and more than double last year’s figure. Thirty-eight new outbreaks have been declared this year, and 94 percent of confirmed cases (2,407 of 2,566) are outbreak-associated: 1,032 from outbreaks that began in 2026 and 1,375 from outbreaks that began in 2025 and never terminated.

Children aged five and under account for 20 percent of cases, and 69 percent of cases are in people aged 19 or younger. Seven percent of patients have been hospitalized. Ninety-three percent of those infected are unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status. Sixteen cases were reported among international visitors. US measles elimination status — held since 2000 — will be formally reviewed in November, and a loss of that status would be the first for any country in the Americas region.

COVID summer surge spreads to all four US census regions

A summer COVID-19 wave that began in the South and West has now reached all four US census regions, arriving during peak summer travel weeks. The CDC’s national wastewater surveillance network shows California as one of only four states at moderate or high activity, with rising signals from Marin County through Palo Alto, Sunnyvale and San Jose. Public health officials describe a mixture of circulating variants rather than a single dominant lineage, with the Nimbus variant — which drove earlier 2026 waves in China, Singapore and Southeast Asia before US airport-screening detection in March — and the more heavily mutated Cicada variant both present. Researchers now describe COVID as having settled into a California summer seasonal pattern as winter waves flatten, a transition that reflects immune escape and abandoned mitigation rather than the virus becoming benign.

Data-center backlash reaches 500 local bans and the first statewide moratorium

Local governments have now enacted more than 500 bans or moratoria on AI data-center construction nationwide, and 75 major projects worth over $130 billion were delayed or cancelled by organized local opposition in the first quarter of 2026 alone. New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed the first statewide moratorium on new large-scale data-center permits, pausing approvals for up to a year while regulators develop ratepayer and environmental standards. More than 300 state-level data-center bills were filed in the first six weeks of 2026, with moratorium proposals introduced in 14 states. Republican governors in Montana, Wyoming and Missouri signed a White House-brokered “Ratepayer Protection Pledge.” Bloomberg separately reported that rapid swings in AI power demand are physically damaging batteries, generators and cooling equipment far ahead of expected service life, and that most of the electricity capacity being sought for US data centers will never materialize.

Benjamin Alire Sáenz, 1954-2026. The El Paso poet and novelist died at 71 after a long illness. Born in Old Picacho, New Mexico, a farming community near Las Cruces, into a family of agricultural workers, Sáenz became the first Latino writer to win the PEN/Faulkner Award, for Everything Begins and Ends at the Kentucky Club (2013), a collection of stories set along the Ciudad Juárez-El Paso border at the height of the cartel war. His young-adult novel Aristotle and Dante Discover the Secrets of the Universe reached an international audience and was named by Time among the 100 best YA novels ever written.

Sáenz’s best work is rooted in a specific social reality: the border as a place of labor, poverty, migration and state violence, not as metaphor. A former Catholic priest who left the priesthood, he wrote about masculinity, class and the interior lives of working-class Mexican-American youth with a directness rare in contemporary American fiction. At his request, the El Paso Community Foundation has established a literary fund to found a book festival in the city and support regional writers.

Venice unveils a competition weighted toward auteur cinema. The 83rd Venice International Film Festival (September 2-12) has announced a 20-film competition notably lighter on studio and streaming product than in recent years. Lee Chang-dong returns with Possible Love, his first feature since Burning (2018); Hirokazu Kore-eda presents Look Back; Werner Herzog, Martin McDonagh (Wild Horse Nine), Florian Zeller (Bunker) and Andrea Pallaoro (The Echo Chamber, with Alicia Vikander and Susan Sarandon) also compete. Casey Affleck debuts as director with Company. Danny Boyle’s Ink opens the festival, and a documentary on the Oasis reunion tour features in the out-of-competition slate.

Only one of the 20 competition films, Danish-Egyptian director May el-Toukhy’s Woman Unknown, is directed by a woman — a fact that will dominate festival commentary and largely displace discussion of the films themselves. The more substantive question is what this selection says about the retreat of serious cinema from the industrial center: Venice’s turn away from studio and streamer product reflects the collapse of the middle of the film industry, not a resurgence of artistic ambition within it.

Bernie Sanders’ AI letter: A bankrupt appeal to the oligarchs — Joseph Kishore

Kishore takes up Sanders’ August 10 letter to Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and Mark Zuckerberg demanding that they “pause AI development” and “stop building machines that humans cannot control.” Sanders wrote that “almost every day, there is a new story about how your companies are losing control of the AI technology you are developing, with potentially cataclysmic results,” citing the first documented use of AI to design novel viruses and warning of bioweapons capable of killing tens of millions. He closed with the threat that “if you do not take appropriate action now, my colleagues and I in the U.S. Senate will.”

The Perspective’s core argument is that the form of the intervention refutes its content. Having spent two years denouncing an “oligarchy,” Sanders responds to the danger he describes by writing a polite letter asking the oligarchs to regulate themselves — and by invoking the authority of a Senate that has ratified every element of the tech billionaires’ program. There is no appeal to the working class, no call for the expropriation of the AI monopolies, and no acknowledgment that the technology’s danger arises from private ownership rather than from insufficiently responsible owners. Kishore situates the letter within Sanders’ decades-long function of channelling social opposition back into the Democratic Party, and counterposes the socialist demand that AI development be taken out of private hands and placed under the democratic control of the working class.

Johannes Stern on RT: Bogdan Syrotiuk convicted “for socialist thoughts” — Andre Damon

Damon reports on the RT interview with Johannes Stern of the Sozialistische Gleichheitspartei concerning the 15-year sentence handed down on August 10 by a district court in the Nikolaev region against Bogdan Syrotiuk, the 27-year-old Ukrainian Trotskyist and secretary of the Young Guard of Bolshevik-Leninists. Syrotiuk was convicted of “high treason” under martial law for calling for the unity of Russian and Ukrainian workers against the war. The conviction rested on antiwar articles he published on the WSWS and on his opposition to the Ukrainian state’s glorification of Nazi collaborators; the court also ordered confiscation of his property and electronic devices and the physical destruction of the socialist books, leaflets and programmatic documents seized from him. He has been imprisoned since April 25, 2024, when the SBU arrested him and declared him a Russian agent, a claim never substantiated in court.

Stern’s central point is that the case exposes the fraud of the “war for democracy”: a government that jails a socialist for 15 years for his ideas, and burns his books, is defending nothing but the interests of oligarchs and NATO. The article is significant for the ICFI’s campaign in that it marks the penetration of the case into a mass international audience, and for the fact that the destruction-of-books order has drawn comparison from historians to the practices of the 1930s.

Three executions in a single day in America: Far more than a scheduling coincidence — Kate Randall

Randall’s contribution beyond the facts reported above is her insistence on the political framework. She rejects the Death Penalty Information Center’s characterization of the convergence as administrative happenstance, arguing that the simultaneous scheduling reflects a coordinated hardening across Republican-controlled state governments, emboldened by a federal administration that has ordered the Justice Department to seek death sentences wherever available and has restored federal executions. She connects the death penalty’s expansion to the broader apparatus of state violence — the deployment of troops in American cities, the ICE killings, the mass surveillance operations — and identifies its class function: the American ruling class is rebuilding the instruments of judicial terror in anticipation of mass social opposition. The article also notes the recurrence of botched lethal injections and the return of nitrogen hypoxia and the firing squad, and stresses that the majority of those executed are poor and were represented by underfunded court-appointed counsel.

Sailor jumps overboard in suicide attempt on USS Abraham Lincoln — Kevin Reed

Reed reports a suicide attempt aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln, the aircraft carrier that has now been at sea for over six months without a port call and is approaching the longest continuous US carrier deployment since the Vietnam War. The sailor jumped overboard and was recovered. Reed places the incident within the documented crisis of military suicide — rates among active-duty Navy personnel have risen consistently, with clusters historically associated with extended deployments and shipyard availabilities — and argues that the Navy’s response has been to treat the epidemic as an individual mental-health problem rather than a consequence of the conditions it imposes. The article’s political point is that the human cost of the Iran war is being paid first by working-class youth in uniform, held indefinitely at sea to enforce a blockade whose purpose is the subjugation of a country of 90 million people. Australia: Labor government tightens war-related powers over universities — Mike Head

Head analyses new legislation extending the Albanese government’s powers over Australian universities on national security grounds, expanding ministerial authority to vet and terminate research collaborations, restrict foreign partnerships and impose security conditions on staff and students. The measures build on the existing Foreign Arrangements Scheme and the Defence Trade Controls regime. Head’s argument is that the ostensible target — Chinese influence — is a pretext for a far broader purpose: bringing the universities directly under the direction of the military-intelligence apparatus in preparation for war with China, and providing the legal machinery to suppress antiwar dissent on campus. He notes the absence of any opposition from the Coalition, the Greens or the National Tertiary Education Union, and warns that the powers are framed broadly enough to be turned against socialist and antiwar organizations, of which the IYSSE is the explicit target.

Anglicare report shows deepening rental crisis for Australian workers and welfare recipients — Jessicah Green

Green reports on Anglicare Australia’s latest Rental Affordability Snapshot, which surveys every advertised rental property in the country on a single day and measures affordability against various household incomes. The report documents a further deterioration: a vanishingly small number of properties are affordable for those on JobSeeker, the Disability Support Pension or the minimum wage, and single parents and young workers are effectively excluded from the private rental market in every capital city. Green places the figures in the context of the Labor government’s housing policy, which channels public money into subsidies for private developers through the Housing Australia Future Fund while refusing to build public housing at scale, and notes the political function of the surge in homelessness among employed workers: it is a disciplining mechanism, forcing acceptance of wage cuts and unsafe conditions. She counterposes the demand for high-quality public housing as a social right.

IG Metall union and works council toast brutal cuts at German steel plant — Philipp Frisch and Dietmar Gaisenkersting

Frisch and Gaisenkersting report on the spectacle of IG Metall officials and works council members celebrating the implementation of the “Steel Realignment” restructuring agreement at a German steel plant — an agreement that eliminates or outsources around 11,000 jobs and cuts steel shipping capacity to roughly 8.7-9 million tonnes, with site closures, reduced working hours and cut bonus payments, running to September 30, 2030. They call for the formation of rank-and-file action committees independent of IG Metall and the linking of German steelworkers’ struggles with those of steelworkers in the US, India and elsewhere against the same transnational corporations.

Jacobin / DSA

“Karl Marx’s Vision of the Good Society” (Jan Kandiyali) is an academic treatment of Marx’s conception of human flourishing under communism, arguing against the individualist readings advanced by G.A. Cohen and Jon Elster. The piece is not without interest, but its function within Jacobin’s output is characteristic: Marxism is presented as a subject for philosophical debate about “the good society,” entirely detached from the question of revolutionary practice or from any assessment of the political situation confronting workers today.

“Trump Gets Real About US Dominance of Latin America” (Andre Pagliarini) critiques a State Department video that openly recasts the Monroe Doctrine as a “tool of American dominance,” and cites the abduction of Maduro from Caracas. The exposure is accurate as far as it goes. What is absent is any call to action: no demand for the withdrawal of US forces, no appeal to American workers to oppose the operations conducted in their name, no examination of the Democratic Party’s support for the intervention. The article treats US imperialism as a subject for informed commentary rather than as something to be fought.

“The Odyssey Is a Thrilling, Reactionary Lament” (Grant Morgan) reviews the Nolan adaptation through the frame of conservatism versus liberalism and Trump.

Assessment: Jacobin’s output on these days is theoretical and cultural, with the DSA electoral work — including coverage of DSA municipal candidacies such as the Providence mayoral race — carried lower on the site. This division of labor is itself the political statement: Marxism as intellectual heritage above, Democratic Party campaigning as practice below.

Left Voice

“The Democratic Party Establishment Punches Back” (Madeleine Freeman) covers the Democratic apparatus’s counteroffensive against “progressive” primary challengers. “A New Political Moment? Trumpism’s Crisis, the Midterms, and Shifts to the Left in the U.S.” discusses the radicalization of workers and youth amid the crisis of the Trump administration, framed around the midterm elections. “Bundism, Zionism, and Bolshevism” (Nathaniel Flakin) reviews the history of Jewish socialist internationalism.

Left Voice’s recent “AOC’s ABC Interview and the Fight Over What Socialism Will Mean” explicitly criticizes AOC and the DSA for working to “renew the Democratic Party” — a formulation sharper than anything in the PSL or Jacobin. But the framing of the political moment through the midterms concedes the essential ground: it accepts the electoral calendar of the bourgeois state as the axis around which the radicalization of workers is to be measured, rather than the independent industrial and political mobilization of the working class. The Morenoite tradition’s characteristic method — verbal orthodoxy combined with practical orientation to whatever mass movement presents itself — remains intact.

Liberation News / PSL

“Flanagan wins big in primary as Minnesotans reject AIPAC and ICE” presents the victory of a Democratic candidate over an AIPAC-backed rival in a Senate primary as a popular rejection of AIPAC and ICE. This is Democratic Party channeling in its clearest form: a favorable presentation of a bourgeois-party candidate, with no counterposition of independent working-class politics and no examination of what a Democratic senator will actually do on war funding or immigration enforcement. “Desperate Democratic establishment narrowly defeats Hong in Wisconsin – but it won’t stop the rise of socialism” treats a DSA-backed candidate’s defeat in a Democratic gubernatorial primary as evidence of “socialism’s rise,” redefining socialism as a current within the Democratic Party. “A year of federal occupation in D.C. shows what’s at stake in our struggle” takes a mass-mobilization approach to the National Guard deployment and is not electoralist.

Assessment: The PSL combines street mobilization against state repression with electoral subordination to the Democrats — a contradiction resolved in practice in favor of the latter. Notably, the PSL’s coverage of the DC occupation makes no reference to the DHS surveillance of unions documented this week, which would raise the question of the Democratic-aligned union apparatus’s silence.

Socialist Alternative

“Democratic Socialist Francesca Hong Concedes In Wisconsin—What Way Forward?” analyzes the narrow primary loss, explicitly withholds support from the corporate-backed Democratic nominee Crowley, and argues for organizing independently “beyond the limitations” of the Democratic primary process. On the identical race, this is a materially sharper line than Liberation News’s.

The limitation is structural: the article’s entire frame of reference remains the Democratic primary. “Beyond the limitations” of that process is offered as a tactical recommendation, not as a principled break with the Democratic Party — and Socialist Alternative’s own record includes running candidates on Democratic ballot lines and supporting Democratic administrations at municipal level. Independence is presented as a matter of degree rather than of class principle.

Socialist Worker (SWP)

“Exclusive: My friend Jason Arday died because of a racist witch-hunt” (August 16) is testimony on the death of a black academic following a right-wing media campaign. “How white academics are protected by a racist system” develops an institutional double-standards critique. “Who’s really feeling the heat this summer?” contrasts care and health workers suffering in heatwaves with “the rich lounge in pools.”

The third article gestures at class, but the dominant frame across the SWP’s output is racial: institutions, not the capitalist state and the Labour government administering it, are the antagonist, and the political conclusion is the “Stand Up to Racism” campaign — a cross-class alliance that has repeatedly served to subordinate workers to Labour and the TUC. Nothing on the war in Iran, the Hormuz blockade or British participation in it appeared in this output.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

Nothing new within the window. The most recent item, “AI, data centres, and the quiet hum of revolution” (August 14), offers generic anti-capitalist framing of automation. The absence of any content on the Iran war, on the Syrotiuk sentence or on the DHS surveillance revelations is itself significant for an organization that has spent two years promoting itself as the “Revolutionary Communist Party.”

Notes for the board: No article across these tendencies in this scan attacked the ICFI, the WSWS or Trotskyism by name, and none carried the Syrotiuk case — the silence of every one of these organizations on the 15-year sentence handed to a Ukrainian socialist for antiwar articles is the most politically revealing fact in the day’s pseudo-left output.
