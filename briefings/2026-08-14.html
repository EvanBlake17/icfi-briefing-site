1. Pentagon admits US strikes killed 153 civilians in Yemen in 2025

A declassified Pentagon review released this week concedes that US airstrikes on Houthi targets in Yemen killed 153 civilians and wounded 243 during 2025 — the first public admission by the Trump administration of significant civilian harm in the Yemen campaign. The review was made public more than a year after the strikes and only under congressional pressure.

The single deadliest incident was the April 17, 2025 strike on the Ras Isa fuel port, which killed 80 people and injured 171. Two additional April 2025 strikes near Sanaa and Saada killed 5 and 47 civilians respectively. CENTCOM’s response to the review was that the Ras Isa strike “was not intended to harm the people of Yemen” and was aimed at cutting Houthi fuel revenue. The Pentagon declined to offer condolence payments to any of the victims’ families — a mechanism that exists precisely for such cases and that has been used in Iraq and Afghanistan.

Key facts: - 153 civilians killed, 243 wounded in 2025 US strikes on Yemen, per the declassified review; nearly 400 casualties in total. - Independent monitoring groups have documented at least 238 deaths — exceeding the Pentagon’s own tally by a minimum of 85 people. - The Ras Isa port strike alone accounts for more than half the admitted death toll. - No condolence payments, no disciplinary action, and no named accountability for any strike. - CENTCOM’s stated objective — cutting fuel revenue — is an explicit admission that civilian economic infrastructure was targeted.

2. English-speaking assailants in US flag patches attack Ecuadorian fishing crews

Fishermen along Ecuador’s Pacific coast have described a pattern of attacks by armed, uniformed, English-speaking men wearing US flag patches, who boarded their vessels, hooded and handcuffed crew members, denied them medical care, and in at least one case left a crew to disappear entirely. The reporting, first developed by the New York Times, comes amid the ongoing US military campaign of lethal strikes on boats in the eastern Pacific and Caribbean.

Three vessels are at the center of the accounts: the Fiorella, whose eight-man crew has never been found; the Negra Francisca Duarte II; and the Don Maca. Investigators traced unidentified aircraft involved in the operations to a company registered in Virginia whose address is a UPS Store mailbox. “Who are these people that are operating, and under what authority?” asked Brian Finucane, a former State Department lawyer. US Southern Command denies any knowledge of or involvement in the attacks. An Ecuadorian prosecutor who had been investigating the incidents was shot dead in June.

Key facts: - At least 66 US strikes on vessels since September have killed over 220 people, according to Pentagon figures cited in the reporting. - Survivors report matching uniforms, US flag patches, hooding, handcuffing, and denial of medical treatment. - Aircraft ownership traced to a Virginia shell entity with a mailbox address — the standard architecture of a deniable covert operation. - SOUTHCOM’s blanket denial leaves no US agency claiming legal authority for the operations.

3. Hegseth announces Colombian joint operations, tells Latin America to reject the ICC

US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced that Colombia under its new far-right president, Abelardo de la Espriella, will conduct joint military operations with the United States and has joined the Pentagon-organized “Americas Counter Cartel Coalition” (A3C). The move reverses the policy of the previous Colombian government and folds the region’s largest US security client back into direct operational partnership.

In the same remarks, Hegseth warned that the International Criminal Court could open an investigation into US strikes on alleged drug-smuggling boats in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific — killings that human rights organizations characterize as extrajudicial executions — and urged Latin American governments to reject the ICC’s jurisdiction outright. The appeal is part of a broader Trump administration campaign against the court, which has been under US sanctions since it issued arrest warrants over Israel’s conduct in Gaza.

Key facts: - De la Espriella authorized joint operations within months of taking office, without any public Colombian parliamentary authorization reported. - Hegseth’s warning is an implicit acknowledgement that the boat strikes may constitute crimes under the Rome Statute. - Washington is simultaneously seeking to delegitimize the ICC over Gaza and over its own killings in the Western Hemisphere.

4. Ukrainian drone campaign blacks out Crimea as grain exports collapse

Ukraine struck 14 energy facilities in Russian-occupied Crimea over three days, producing peninsula-wide failures of electricity, fuel distribution and cellphone service. Some districts have been placed on rolling blackout schedules of roughly two hours of power per twelve. Russia’s Defense Ministry claimed 133 Ukrainian drones were intercepted over Russian territory, the Black Sea and Crimea in a single overnight period.

The intensity of the fighting continues to climb. Ukraine’s General Staff logged 198 combat engagements on August 13 alone, alongside 55 Russian air strikes, 160 guided bombs and 6,124 Russian kamikaze-drone launches. A Russian drone strike on a train in the Odesa region killed two railway workers. Moscow says it is “ready” to resume peace talks but has attached new conditions.

The strikes on port and terminal infrastructure are now registering in world food markets. As Jason Melanovski wrote on the WSWS, the war has moved into a phase in which “hundreds of millions” in the Middle East and Africa dependent on Black Sea grain are directly exposed to the military logic of both governments.

Key facts: - Consultancy SovEcon projects Russian wheat exports of just 3–3.4 million tonnes this month against a five-year August average of 5 million — the lowest August since 2017 — following Ukrainian strikes on Novorossiysk. - Russia’s Union of Grain Exporters warns of a potential 30–35 million tonne seasonal shortfall, roughly 15% of global wheat trade. - Ukrainian drone attacks damaged grain terminals at Novorossiysk and Taman; Russian strikes targeted Odesa, Ukraine’s principal seaport. - Ukraine’s Agriculture Ministry cut its 2026–27 export forecast by 54%. - Poverty in Ukraine rose from 37% to 41.6% year on year.

5. German cabinet approves largest expansion of intelligence powers in the republic’s history

The German federal cabinet approved a legislative package of more than 700 pages overhauling the legal framework of the Bundesnachrichtendienst (BND) and the domestic Verfassungsschutz — the most far-reaching restructuring since the agencies were founded. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt summarized the intent bluntly: “We are turning our intelligence services into real secret services.”

The package converts the Verfassungsschutz from an observational body into an operational one. It authorizes active disruption measures — sabotage, hacking, manipulation of data traffic, deployment of spyware for online searches and infiltration, and offensive counter-measures against foreign cyber infrastructure. It permits AI-enabled mass analysis of public and private camera feeds, extends data retention periods, and grants the foreign intelligence service the power to continue network surveillance inside Germany where it originated abroad. The government cites “hybrid threats,” pointing to an August drone incident at Leipzig airport whose origin remains unestablished.

As Peter Schwarz wrote on the WSWS, the measure dismantles the institutional separation of police and intelligence functions imposed after 1945 specifically to prevent the reconstitution of a Gestapo-type apparatus. Civil liberties organizations have called the package “unconstitutional in large parts” and warn of the “policification” of the intelligence services. Parliamentary approval is still pending; MPs return from summer recess in early September.

Key facts: - Cabinet adopted the draft on August 12–13; the Independent Control Council is designated as the consolidated oversight body. - No establishment party has mounted serious opposition; the Left Party’s criticism is rhetorical, having strengthened the same agencies where it governs at state level. - The reform arrives amid record German military spending and mounting domestic opposition to austerity and rearmament.

Key facts: - Siege in its fifth day as of Thursday; families inside without electricity, running water or resupply. - The Israeli military has sealed the village rather than removing the settlers. - Huckabee’s statement marks a rare US condemnation, prompted by the involvement of a US citizen.

7. MSF: Israel’s Gaza blockade is “a policy of attrition by design”

Médecins Sans Frontières issued a statement charging that Israel’s continued restriction of entry of engine oil and spare parts into Gaza is placing thousands of lives at risk. Without these supplies, hospital generators, ambulances, water pumps and water trucks cannot operate. “What we are witnessing in Gaza is not a series of isolated shortages, it is a policy of attrition by design,” said the organization’s head of mission in Palestine.

The generator at MSF’s Deir al-Balah field hospital failed on August 5 for lack of engine oil, forcing the hospital to ration electricity, halt sterilization procedures and stop X-ray services altogether. Engine oil prices inside Gaza have risen twentyfold in six months, exceeding $1,300 per litre by the end of July.

Separately, Gaza’s Government Media Office reported that Israel has violated the ceasefire more than 3,795 times since October 2025, killing at least 1,259 Palestinians. The figures underline that the “ceasefire” has functioned as a framework for continued killing and territorial consolidation rather than a cessation of hostilities.

Key facts: - Engine oil above $1,300/litre — a twentyfold rise in six months. - Sterilization and X-ray services suspended at a major field hospital since August 5. - 3,795+ recorded ceasefire violations; 1,259 Palestinians killed since October 2025, per Gaza’s Government Media Office.

8. Sailors attempt to go overboard from USS Abraham Lincoln on record 250-day deployment

Multiple sailors aboard the aircraft carrier USS Abraham Lincoln have attempted to jump overboard during a deployment that has now exceeded 250 days at sea, with more than 200 days elapsed since the ship’s last port call — a modern-era record for a US carrier. CNN confirmed that one sailor went overboard last month and was rescued.

Family members have gone public. Annabelle Loma said her husband, a sailor aboard the ship, cited severe burnout. Maria Rodriguez said her husband intervened to stop a shipmate from going over the side. Sailors describe moldy showers, broken toilets, inconsistent hot water and food shortages aboard the vessel. The Navy responded that leadership is “continuously assessing sailors’ psychological readiness,” with counselors and chaplains available.

The Lincoln’s deployment is a direct product of the sustained US naval concentration in the Middle East. The conditions aboard — and the refusal to rotate the crew — illustrate the human cost borne by working-class enlisted personnel of Washington’s permanent war footing.

Key facts: - 250+ days at sea; 200+ days without a port call. - At least one confirmed man-overboard incident last month, plus additional reported attempts. - Reported conditions: mold in showers, broken sanitation, intermittent hot water, food shortages. - Navy response confined to counseling availability; no announced rotation or relief.

Western Europe records its hottest summer ever

June and July 2026 were the hottest such months ever recorded in Western Europe, Copernicus confirmed, with a combined average of 21.62°C — 2.79°C above the 1991–2020 norm and almost half a degree above the previous record set in 2022. June alone ran 3.06°C above the historical mean. Wildfires have burned more than 1.23 million acres, mainly in Spain, France and Italy, displacing hundreds of thousands.

  • Germany recorded roughly 12,500 heat-related deaths between the start of April and the end of July; France documented over 5,700.
  • The WHO estimates approximately 200,000 heat-related deaths across Europe over the past four years.
  • Portugal, Spain, France, Italy and Greece experienced major wildfires; Spain declared a state of emergency in July for the first time.
  • The Gironde region near Bordeaux suffered its largest forest fire since 1949, destroying 42,000 hectares.
  • Low water levels on the Rhine and Danube disrupted industrial production and forced temporary shutdowns of nuclear power stations in several countries.

The deaths are overwhelmingly concentrated among the elderly, the poor and workers in unairconditioned housing and outdoor employment — a class distribution that no European government has moved to address through the mass public investment required.

One year of military occupation in Washington, DC

This week marks one year since Trump declared a “crime emergency” in the District of Columbia, federalized the Metropolitan Police Department and deployed the National Guard onto the streets of the capital. The deployment has now been extended through January 2029 — the end of the presidential term. Taxpayers have spent at least $600 million on it.

Protests marked the anniversary. The Free DC movement organized eleven days of action, including a rally at Black Lives Matter Plaza on the morning of the anniversary and a gathering on 16th Street near the White House. Organizers honored Julian Bailey and Nolberto Meza, two DC residents killed by federal agents during the occupation. National news coverage this week has largely normalized the deployment, with outlets reporting that troops “have become part of DC life.”

The DC operation established the template subsequently applied to other cities: an emergency declaration, federalization of local police, and an open-ended Guard deployment insulated from local electoral accountability. Washington’s residents have no voting representation in Congress and no mechanism to end the occupation.

DOJ files record wave of denaturalization cases

Federal prosecutors filed 25 new civil denaturalization complaints between July 20 and August 3, bringing the total to 123 since January 2025 — the largest number of such cases in US history. The campaign has been made an explicit priority by Assistant Attorney General Brett Shumate.

Civil denaturalization proceedings carry no right to appointed counsel and a lower evidentiary threshold than criminal proceedings. Legal scholars warn that the volume and the political selection of targets could trigger judicial pushback, but note that the campaign establishes a precedent for treating naturalized citizenship as a revocable status subject to executive discretion — a category of second-class citizenship affecting more than 25 million people.

Puerto Rico imposes 48-hour water cuts on 500,000 people

Puerto Rico’s water utility PRASA began a strict 48-hours-on, 48-hours-off rationing rotation on August 7–8 affecting roughly 180,000 customers — some 500,000 people — across San Juan, Carolina, Juncos, Gurabo, Trujillo Alto, Canóvanas and Loíza. Officials say the regime will last at least through the end of August. San Juan recorded its driest July in more than 120 years, and over 72% of the island is in drought.

The Carraízo reservoir serving the metropolitan region has fallen to critical levels, compounded by a June pipeline rupture. Between 60% and 65% of the water PRASA produces is lost to leaks and unbilled losses before reaching consumers: Carraízo produces 70 million gallons a day, of which only 30–35 million reach homes. FEMA water trailers are covering an initial 30-day period at levels residents describe as grossly inadequate.

As Andrea Lobo noted on the WSWS, the Roosevelt Roads military base is receiving $79 million in infrastructure funding, including a dedicated four-million-gallon-per-day filtration system, while the civilian population rations. The decay of the island’s aqueduct system is the direct product of the austerity imposed by the PROMESA fiscal control board on behalf of Wall Street bondholders.

US measles cases reach 2,465, highest since 1991

As of August 6, the CDC has confirmed 2,465 measles cases in the United States in 2026 — the highest annual count since 1991 and since elimination was declared in 2000. Thirty-eight new outbreaks have been reported this year, and 94% of cases (2,309) are outbreak-associated. Cases have been reported by 47 jurisdictions, with South Carolina reporting the most. Ninety-three percent of confirmed cases were in people who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown. Seven percent of cases required hospitalization; no deaths have been reported.

US elimination status will be formally reviewed in November. Loss of that status would be the first for any country in the Americas region in a quarter century and follows the gutting of federal vaccination outreach and the removal of vaccine advisory committee membership under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.

Cyclosporiasis outbreak reaches 13,895 cases across 47 states

A cyclosporiasis outbreak has produced 13,895 confirmed cases across 47 states, with 740 hospitalizations and two deaths. Michigan alone accounts for 12,485 cases; the state health department received roughly 475 previously unreported cases in a single July 24 batch owing to delayed laboratory reporting — an indication of the degradation of surveillance capacity. The outbreak runs alongside separate salmonella clusters: 345 cases traced to jalapeños and 98 to shell eggs.

Nature links anellovirus reactivation to long COVID

The August 13 issue of Nature (vol. 656, issue 8127) carries several significant findings:

  • Anelloviruses and long COVID. Reactivation of anelloviruses — a family of viruses previously considered harmless commensals — appears linked to the development of long COVID, offering a candidate mechanism and potential biomarker for a condition affecting tens of millions.
  • TBX5 and cardiac genome architecture. Loss of a single copy of the congenital heart disease gene TBX5 causes the three-dimensional folded structure of the cardiac cell genome to unravel, disrupting expression of the genes required for heart development — a striking demonstration of genome architecture as a disease mechanism.
  • Amazon tipping point. New modeling suggests the Amazon rainforest may be closer to an irreversible transition than previously estimated, with drought and fire capable of pushing the system into decline.

Chinese courts side with workers displaced by AI

Chinese firms are adopting AI at a pace exceeding US deployment, and courts in several cities have begun ruling for workers dismissed and replaced by AI systems — a Guangzhou graphic designer was awarded roughly $6,000 in severance. The rulings have not slowed displacement. Youth unemployment remains near one in six for those aged 16–24, and fiscal constraints limit the state’s capacity to absorb the labor-market shock. The pattern — juridical acknowledgement of harm alongside continued mass displacement — is the same one now emerging in the US and Europe.

Also this week: - COVID-19: roughly 315,000 new infections daily in the US on current modeling estimates, with no federal mitigation program in place. - Ebola: the outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has reached 4,566 confirmed cases and 2,128 deaths. - H5N1: 71 human cases identified in the US between 2024 and May 2026, overwhelmingly among dairy and poultry workers; wastewater surveillance remains the principal early-warning system.

Nolan’s The Odyssey passes $1 billion, drawing an audience “starved for challenging material”

Christopher Nolan’s adaptation of Homer’s Odyssey has crossed $1.008 billion worldwide — $429.65 million domestic, $578.8 million international — in three weeks, his first film since The Dark Knight Rises (2012) to reach the mark and shortly to become his highest-grossing release. Its $264.1 million global opening was the largest of Nolan’s career. It passed Oppenheimer’s entire domestic run of $330 million in thirteen days.

The exhibition figures are as striking as the grosses: the BFI IMAX Southbank sold out 173 showings, running screenings around the clock; Milwaukee’s Oriental Theatre drew 3,600 attendees in its opening days. Over 100 million people have now seen the film.

Writing on the WSWS, David Walsh treats the phenomenon seriously rather than dismissively. He acknowledges that earlier WSWS reviewers judged Nolan’s work harshly, but argues that Oppenheimer and The Odyssey represent “a considerable jump” — “substantial and complex films about issues that matter.” Walsh locates the appeal in the material: “the horrors of war, the foul role of a usering and cruel aristocracy,” themes that resonate against present conditions. His central formulation is worth noting for editorial discussion: “Life-and-death themes embodied in a classic, treated seriously and generously, have encountered a sizeable global viewing public clearly starved for challenging material.”

That is the significant fact. The commercial success of a three-hour adaptation of an ancient epic, in a period when studio output is dominated by franchise product, indicates an audience whose appetite for serious art has been systematically underestimated and underserved by an industry organized around risk-minimization. The figure of Odysseus — resourceful, enduring, returning to a household seized by parasites — has evident contemporary purchase.

Deaths

  • Benjamin Alire Sáenz, poet and novelist, died at 71. Raised on a farm outside Las Cruces, New Mexico, and long resident in El Paso, he became in 2013 the first Latino writer to win the PEN/Faulkner Award for Fiction, for Everything Begins and Ends at the Kentucky Club. His young adult novel Aristotle and Dante Discover the Secrets of the Universe brought international readership. Sáenz used the US-Mexico border not as exotic backdrop but as the concrete social terrain on which questions of love, humiliation, redemption and survival were posed — writing that gains rather than loses force as the border becomes the site of mass detention and militarized policing.
  • Plas Johnson, tenor saxophonist, died July 15 in Granada Hills, Los Angeles, at 94. Best known for the solo on Henry Mancini’s “The Pink Panther Theme,” Johnson was one of the most recorded session musicians in American popular music, a fixture of the Los Angeles studio scene whose playing appears on hundreds of records by artists who never learned his name. His career is a reminder that the actual labor of American popular music was performed largely by anonymous, mostly Black instrumentalists working for scale.

Roundup

  • The 2026 Whitney Biennial, which opened in March against the backdrop of the administration’s assault on arts funding and DEI programs, continues to draw divided criticism. Fifty-six artists are included, among them three Palestinian visual artists and one born in Baghdad. Reviewers have described it as politically charged but evasive about the specific character of the present American crisis — Frieze called it “an underwhelming show for overwhelming times.” The tension between an institution dependent on the same financial elite it gestures at criticizing, and artists attempting to address war and repression, remains unresolved.
  • Regional opera and theater continue to operate under acute funding pressure. The West Coast premiere of American Apollo — an opera on the painter John Singer Sargent and his model Thomas Eugene McKeller — was staged in Oakland on August 13, one of a number of new works being produced by smaller companies as major houses retrench.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: Disease outbreaks multiply, as Trump and Kennedy wage war on science by Evan Blake — read in full on wsws.org

The Perspective assembles the current outbreak data into a single indictment of the dismantling of public health infrastructure. Its central political conclusion: “there is no faction of the ruling class to which the defense of public health can be entrusted.” Blake draws an explicit comparison between the coordinated purging of scientific institutions and Nazi Gleichschaltung, and roots the collapse not in Kennedy’s individual crankery but in the ruling-class response to the COVID pandemic, in which profit was elevated over life across every party and every capitalist state. The programmatic conclusion is the conscious planning of the world economy on a socialist basis.

Opposition mounts worldwide against 15-year sentence for Ukrainian socialist Bogdan Syrotiuk — Andre Damon

The Pervomaisk City District Court sentenced the 27-year-old Trotskyist to 15 years for “high treason,” on the basis of his writings advocating the unity of Russian and Ukrainian workers, his opposition to the veneration of Nazi collaborators, and his correspondence with WSWS editors. The court additionally ordered the seizure of his property and the physical destruction of his organization’s literature. Syrotiuk, arrested by the SBU on April 25, 2024, is reported to be in poor health.

Over 5,700 people in more than 40 countries have signed the petition demanding his release. The historian Mario Kessler asks: “How does this fit with the accusation that Mr. Syrotiuk, a self-proclaimed socialist and Trotskyist, is playing along with Putin’s game?” Professor Kerby Miller called the sentence “cruel, unjust, and excessive,” questioning Ukraine’s democratic credentials.

The article’s sharpest point is documentary: the New York Times, Washington Post, BBC and Reuters have published nothing whatever on the case, despite saturation coverage of Ukrainian civil liberties in other contexts. The blackout is itself evidence of the political function of the Western press in the war. The International Editorial Board urges distribution of the statements and direct appeals to the court at inbox@pm.mk.court.gov.ua.

Company offers worse contract after SEIU shuts down strike of 950 Pennsylvania healthcare workers — Landon Gourov

After SEIU ordered 950 healthcare workers back to work and terminated their strike, the employer returned with terms inferior to those on the table during the walkout — the predictable result of surrendering the workers’ only source of leverage before a settlement was secured. The article develops the pattern documented across the WSWS’s coverage of the healthcare sector: the bureaucracy treats the strike as a pressure tactic to be switched off at will, and the employer, understanding this, waits it out. Gourov calls for the formation of rank-and-file committees independent of SEIU to take control of the dispute.

Corewell Health nurses in Michigan denounce sellout Teamsters deal — Riley Ward

Nearly 10,000 registered nurses across nine southeastern Michigan hospitals are voting on a Teamsters tentative agreement offering 5.5% immediately, then 4%, 4% and 1.5%. One nurse’s calculation, quoted in the article, is the sharpest datum: “Without the union over these same 3 years we would receive a 17.5% raise” through normal step increases and market adjustments. The agreement caps health insurance subsidies at 85% for medical and 50% for dental and creates a “staffing committee” — the nurses’ central demand — with no enforcement power whatever. Contract language states explicitly that “nothing in this provision requires CHE to add an additional RN on a unit,” and committee decisions are excluded from the grievance procedure. The deal comes after ten months of negotiations and a March strike authorization that was never acted on, and while 750 Henry Ford Genesys nurses remain on strike in their eleventh month, systematically isolated by the same leadership.

Notes on US political repression and state violence — Staff Reporter

The regular compilation documents recent incidents of federal and local state violence, arrests of protesters and journalists, and expansions of surveillance authority. Useful as a running record for editorial board reference; the individual items are drawn from local reporting that receives no national aggregation elsewhere.

North London bus drivers strike against extreme heat — Henry Lee

Drivers struck over cab temperatures and the refusal of operators to install or maintain air conditioning, in the context of Britain’s exceptional summer. The dispute is a concrete instance of the climate crisis registering directly as a workplace safety struggle, and of the union apparatus’s reluctance to generalize such disputes across the industry.

Financial Times embraces Burnham’s “public control” agenda for crisis-ridden water companies — Paul Mitchell

Mitchell examines the FT’s favorable treatment of Andy Burnham’s proposals for “public control” of England’s water utilities. The argument: sections of finance capital are prepared to accept a state takeover of failed, debt-loaded water companies precisely because it socializes the losses of the private equity owners who stripped them, while guaranteeing bondholders. “Public control” on these terms is a bailout, not a nationalization, and Burnham’s promotion of it is positioning for a Labour leadership bid rather than any measure in the interest of water workers or consumers.

Pinochet’s Operation Colombo: A fascist crime and its cover-up — Mauricio Saavedra

An account of the 1975 disinformation operation in which the Pinochet dictatorship, working with Argentine and Brazilian intelligence and with planted articles in fabricated foreign publications, presented 119 disappeared Chilean left-wing militants as having killed one another in internecine conflict abroad. The Chilean press — El Mercurio above all — amplified the fabrication. Saavedra draws the connection to Operation Condor and to the continuing impunity of those responsible, and to the present, in which the Chilean state and press again prepare public opinion for repression.

Australia: Teachers on the front line of the NDIS cuts — Carolyn Kennett

Cuts to the National Disability Insurance Scheme are displacing responsibility for disabled children onto public school teachers without additional staffing, training or funding. Kennett documents classroom conditions and the role of the Australian Education Union in policing teacher opposition, and situates the cuts within the Labor government’s broader austerity program and its accompanying military expenditure.

Jacobin / DSA

“Socialist David Morales Wants to Be Providence’s Next Mayor” is a sympathetic profile of a DSA-aligned mayoral candidate who argues explicitly that socialists should contest Democratic Party primaries, and who criticizes his own local DSA chapter for declining to do so. The article presents this as strategic realism. It is the clearest statement of Democratic Party electoralism in the week’s output: there is no conception of class independence, only “engagement within Democratic primaries” as a permanent method.

“Don’t Back Down on Medicare for All” urges the left to resist Democratic-sponsored “public option” dilutions of single-payer. The content of the appeal is defensive and legislative: the strategic horizon is winning or protecting a bill inside the capitalist state, addressed to Democratic legislators as the agency of change. The class question — that the insurance and hospital corporations are represented in both parties and will not be legislated out of existence — is not posed.

“John Deere Is Testing Its Workers’ Resolve” reports on the UAW-Deere contract vote scheduled for August 23. The article treats the formal bargaining process as the terrain of struggle and does not address the role of the UAW apparatus, which has imposed successive concessionary agreements at Deere. Workers are positioned as an audience for the union’s negotiating performance rather than as an independent force.

Assessment: Jacobin’s output this week is consistently oriented toward the Democratic Party as the vehicle and the legislature as the arena. The absence of Gaza coverage over an extended period is itself a political fact, reflecting the tendency’s integration into a Democratic milieu for which the question is an electoral liability.

Left Voice

“A New Political Moment? Trumpism’s Crisis, the Midterms, and Shifts to the Left in the U.S.” rejects the channeling of radicalized layers into Democratic campaigns, characterizing the party as “a graveyard for social movements,” and argues for an independent socialist alternative.

“Sanders-Backed Flanagan Wins Minnesota Senate Primary” covers a Democratic primary result while stating flatly that the party “is capitalist, imperialist, and Zionist to its core, and cannot be reformed.”

“The Democratic Party Establishment Punches Back” argues that the contradictions the Democrats face over Israel cannot be resolved within a capitalist party and calls for the construction of an independent working-class party.

Assessment: On the question of the Democratic Party, Left Voice’s formulations this week are the most consistent of the six outlets. The unresolved question — which the WSWS has addressed at length in relation to the Morenoite tradition — is what independent organization means concretely: the calls for a “workers’ party” remain programmatically undefined, and the tendency’s orientation to the existing union apparatus and to protest movements has repeatedly cut across the class independence its articles assert.

Liberation News (PSL)

“Desperate Democratic establishment narrowly defeats Hong in Wisconsin” calls the Democratic Party “a party of big business and empire” — but the practice being reported and defended, a socialist running on the Democratic ballot line, is electoral fusion with that party. The rhetorical denunciation coexists with the tactic it denounces.

“Flanagan wins big in primary as Minnesotans reject AIPAC and ICE” goes further, favorably contrasting one Democratic primary candidate against another over AIPAC funding. This lends relative political credibility to a faction inside a bourgeois party rather than maintaining independence from the contest as a whole.

“A year of federal occupation in D.C. shows what’s at stake in our struggle” describes Trump’s DC takeover as “soft-launch martial law,” rejects electoralism and calls for revolutionary class organizing — the strongest of the three pieces, and directly at odds in method with the primary coverage.

Assessment: The PSL’s line remains internally divided between anti-Democratic rhetoric and practical intervention in Democratic primaries. The underlying orientation is toward a radicalized milieu whose activity is defined by whatever electoral or protest vehicle is available.

Socialist Alternative

Nothing new was published in the past 24–48 hours; the most recent items date to August 2–7. “Support Left Independent Candidates — Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties” rejects the Democratic Party outright, urges Mamdani-aligned figures to break from it, and endorses Kshama Sawant’s independent run. “What’s Really Behind The Crisis In Ceuta?” attributes the migration crisis to imperialism and border regimes while mixing housing and jobs demands with a revolutionary horizon. “South Africa: Xenophobia & The Street War Against Black Immigrants” blames the ANC and COSATU leadership for xenophobic violence and calls for an independent workers’ party and nationalizations.

Assessment: Formally independent on the electoral question. The persistent problem is the conception of “left independent candidates” as an assembled coalition of progressive figures rather than a party built on a definite international socialist program.

Socialist Worker (SWP UK)

“Clacton by-election: Farage is wounded, but still dangerous” attacks Labour’s Andy Burnham for attempting to “out-Reform Reform UK” on immigration and warns against relying on Labour to stop Farage. “Food prices are soaring amid war and climate breakdown” attributes food inflation to “fossil fuel capitalism” and calls on the unions to fight for a “just transition” — a demand pitched as achievable through union pressure within the existing system rather than tied to the expropriation of the energy and agribusiness conglomerates. “Revolt spreads in India and Pakistan” attributes the South Asian uprisings to broken promises, corruption and exploitative trade deals, and identifies revolutionary potential in them.

Assessment: The SWP’s opposition to Labour is sharper than in previous periods, but the programmatic content remains trade-union pressure politics. The absence of current Gaza coverage from a tendency that built its recent profile on the Palestine marches is notable.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (communist.red)

“Defend the Cuban revolution! Defeat US imperialism! Fight for world socialism!” opposes the US oil blockade while criticizing the absence of workers’ democracy in Cuba and warning against the market reforms — explicitly rejecting reliance on a rival power bloc. “Burnham and British capitalism — bouncing into an abyss” argues Labour under Burnham cannot resolve capitalism’s contradictions in any direction and calls for revolutionary leadership rather than reforming Labour. “British imperialism: The emperor has no clothes” charts Britain’s decline into subordination to the US and concludes that only revolutionary overthrow offers a way forward.

Assessment: No campism in this window on either Cuba or Britain, which distinguishes the IMT’s output from much of the milieu. The tendency’s characteristic weakness — a propagandist perspective disconnected from any concrete intervention in the class struggle, and its long record of adaptation to Labour and to bourgeois nationalist movements internationally — is not tested by this week’s material.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Who is attacking Ecuadorian fishermen in the name of the United States?

Survivors describe uniformed, English-speaking men in US flag patches boarding fishing vessels, hooding and handcuffing crews, and in one case a crew of eight vanishing entirely; the aircraft trace to a shell company at a UPS Store mailbox and SOUTHCOM denies all knowledge. This is the visible edge of an undeclared covert war being waged across Latin America outside any legal authorization, and the killing of the Ecuadorian prosecutor investigating it demands the strongest exposure.

Recommended reading New York Times They spoke English, wore US flags and attacked fishermen. Who were they?

2. Record wave of denaturalization cases creates a second-class citizenship

The DOJ has filed 123 civil denaturalization complaints since January 2025 — the most in US history — with 25 in a two-week window this summer. Civil proceedings carry no right to appointed counsel and a reduced evidentiary standard, effectively rendering the citizenship of more than 25 million naturalized Americans revocable at executive discretion. This is a democratic-rights question of the first order and connects directly to the WSWS campaign against the deportation machine.

Recommended reading The Conversation Justice Department’s effort to strip citizenship from naturalized Americans could face widespread judicial pushback

3. Sudan’s forgotten war: Blue Nile offensive and the siege of el-Obeid

RSF-allied forces have claimed Geissan; a mass grave with 25 civilians was found in Kurmuk; hundreds of thousands in el-Obeid face drone strikes, cholera and water shortages after more than 1,000 days of war. Sudan is the largest displacement crisis on earth and receives a fraction of the WSWS’s Ukraine and Gaza coverage. An analysis situating the war within the rivalry of the UAE, Egypt, Saudi Arabia and the major powers over the Red Sea and Sudanese gold would fill a genuine gap.

Recommended reading UN News Sudan: More than 1,000 days of war

4. CIA doubted the Israeli intelligence used to justify escalation against Iran

A CIA assessment reportedly held low confidence in the Israeli claim that Iran plotted to shoot down Air Force One — a claim deployed publicly to build the case for military action. The pattern of Israeli-sourced intelligence being laundered into US war justification, and the readiness of the American press to repeat it, warrants the same treatment the WSWS gave the Iraq WMD fabrications.

Recommended reading Democracy Now! Headlines for August 13, 2026

5. Israel now controls nearly 70% of Gaza under cover of a “ceasefire”

Nine months into the declared ceasefire, Israeli forces have expanded territorial control from roughly half to nearly 70% of the Gaza Strip, alongside near-daily strikes. The slow-motion annexation proceeding under a truce framework is systematically underreported relative to the “ceasefire” headline, and connects to the MSF findings on the engineered collapse of Gaza’s remaining infrastructure.

Recommended reading NPR 9 months into a ceasefire, Israel now controls nearly 70% of Gaza

6. AI displacement in China outpaces courts, policy and the state’s fiscal capacity

Chinese firms are replacing workers with AI faster than in the US, with courts in several cities awarding severance to dismissed workers while youth unemployment sits near one in six. The juridical acknowledgement of harm alongside accelerating displacement is a preview of what confronts workers internationally, and cuts against both the techno-optimist and the nationalist-protectionist framings dominant in the Western press.

7. Six months after Maduro’s extraction: Venezuela’s political prisoners and the “recovery” narrative

The US press is now producing triumphal accounts of Venezuela’s post-Maduro “recovery” while relatives of political prisoners protest outside the reopened US embassy and Rubio postpones elections indefinitely on conditions Washington will judge. The precedent — forcible removal of a head of state followed by open-ended US supervision — requires sustained socialist analysis as it is being normalized.
