US and Iran trade direct strikes as war widens into Jordan and Iraq

The US-Iran war entered a sharply escalated phase over the past 24 hours, spilling directly into Jordan and Iraq. Iran’s Revolutionary Guard Corps fired ballistic missiles at Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base, a central US military hub; Jordan’s military said it intercepted and destroyed five incoming missiles. In parallel, joint US-Saudi strikes hit Iran-backed militia positions in eastern Iraq.

US Central Command described a “heavy wave of strikes” carried out over roughly two hours against “dozens” of IRGC targets, including command centers and missile and drone production facilities. Trump made no attempt to frame the escalation defensively: “In the meantime, we’re going to be hitting them very hard because it’s our turn to hit them.”

Key facts:

  • At least 20 fighters killed and 32 wounded in the US-Saudi strikes on eastern Iraq, according to the Popular Mobilization Forces; Iraqi militia officials say six Iranian military advisers were among the dead.
  • Iran’s deputy foreign minister Kazem Gharibabadi rejected an Omani mediation proposal on the Strait of Hormuz, stating that Tehran’s policy is that the strait will “never return to its prewar situation.”
  • Brent crude jumped 7.3% to $88.09 a barrel on the news; WTI rose 6.44% to roughly $84.20.
  • The widening of strikes into Jordanian and Iraqi territory draws two additional states — one a formal US client, the other nominally sovereign — directly into the theater.

Writing on the WSWS, Johannes Stern argued that the Ukraine and Iran fronts have fused into a single conflict: “World war is not a future threat but a presently unfolding reality.”

Russian missile barrage kills at least eight in Ukraine as Kyiv strikes Iranian vessel

Russia launched a country-wide missile barrage overnight, killing at least eight people and reaching as far west as Lviv, forcing Poland to scramble fighter jets to secure its own airspace. The attack came one day after Zelensky returned from Washington, where Trump agreed to license Patriot missile production for Ukraine — a deal that does nothing to address Ukraine’s acute near-term shortage of PAC-3 interceptors.

Key facts:

  • Casualties: one killed and two injured in Kyiv; six killed in Kryvyi Rih and the wider Dnipropetrovsk region, including two children, aged 5 and 12, killed when an Iskander-M struck a family home; one killed in Poltava; 26 injured in Lviv.
  • Kryvyi Rih defense council head Oleksandr Vilkul warned the toll could climb as rescuers clear rubble.
  • Russia’s Ministry of Defence claimed it struck “military facilities” from Kyiv to Lviv and Dnipropetrovsk, and separately hit three cargo ships near Odesa it accused of carrying weapons.
  • In a significant widening of the war’s geography, a Ukrainian long-range drone struck the Iranian cargo ship Ana in the Caspian Sea, killing one sailor and wounding another. Kyiv claimed the vessel was carrying drones and missiles to Russia; Tehran denounced the attack on a commercial vessel as aggression.

The Caspian strike is the clearest demonstration to date that the NATO proxy war against Russia and the US-Israeli offensive against Iran are operationally interlocked. Ukraine, a state with no Caspian coastline and no independent quarrel with Iran, is now conducting strikes on Iranian shipping.

Gaza killings pass 1,200 since “ceasefire” as Israel razes mosque and tent camps

Israeli forces killed at least two more Palestinians on July 29 — one by drone strike in Gaza City’s Rimal neighborhood, one in a strike on civilians at the al-Mawasi tent camp near Khan Younis, according to the Palestinian news agency WAFA. Israeli forces also destroyed a mosque and dozens of surrounding tents in Gaza City after ordering a forced evacuation the previous night.

Key facts:

  • More than 1,200 Palestinians, the overwhelming majority civilians, have been killed by Israeli attacks since the ceasefire formally took effect, according to Gaza health officials. Four Israeli soldiers have been killed in the same period.
  • Mediators from Egypt, Qatar and Turkey met Hamas leaders in Cairo on “phase two” of Trump’s Gaza plan.
  • Hamas has told mediators it will “confine and store” heavy weapons rather than fully disarm — the central unresolved sticking point with Israel.
  • The al-Mawasi area was designated a “humanitarian zone” by the Israeli military and has been repeatedly struck since.

The ratio — 1,200 to four — is the operative measure of what the “ceasefire” consists of: a continuation of the extermination campaign at a reduced tempo, with the international press having largely moved on.

Israel launches most intense West Bank operation in years amid settler rampage

Israel has launched what observers describe as its most intense West Bank military operation in years, storming towns and detaining hundreds. The trigger was a July 24 clash near Tell, outside Nablus, in which a villager seized an armed settler’s rifle and killed him, wounding four others. In the aftermath, four Palestinians and two Israeli soldiers died.

Key facts:

  • Two mosques were set ablaze in apparent settler revenge attacks. A military raid on a Nablus hospital resulted in the capture of two suspects.
  • Between January 2025 and June 2026, the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs recorded 3,033 Israeli settler attacks in the West Bank.
  • Netanyahu’s office indicated Israel may soon launch “another counterterror operation” in West Bank refugee camps.
  • Separately, the Israeli cabinet approved a ₪3 billion (roughly $800 million) development package for Jewish communities in the Galilee.

The pattern is consistent: settler violence proceeds under army protection, an act of resistance is used to justify collective punishment, and the state simultaneously subsidizes further colonization.

Global equity rout deepens: Dow suffers worst day since April 2025, KOSPI posts worst month on record

Wall Street sold off hard on July 29, with the Dow Jones Industrial Average falling 1,153.18 points (−2.19%) to 51,594.14 — its worst single session since April 2025. The S&P 500 fell 1.52% to 7,316.15 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 1.74% to 24,442.94, now more than 10% off its all-time high. The proximate driver was the bond market’s conclusion that the Federal Reserve is falling behind on inflation.

The more dramatic damage has been in Seoul. South Korea’s KOSPI has fallen roughly 23% in July — a peak-to-trough decline exceeding 31%, from 9,114 in June to 6,806.93 on July 13 — wiping out approximately ₩250 trillion in market capitalization. It is the worst month in the index’s history.

Key facts:

  • Market-wide circuit breakers were triggered on consecutive trading sessions, July 28 and 29, for the first time ever. Seven had already fired by mid-July, exceeding most full-year totals.
  • Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix recorded single-day declines of 9–12% during the heaviest selling; SK Hynix fell as much as 14.7% in one session.
  • Cited triggers: China’s move to mass production of DUV chipmaking tools, raising semiconductor-competition fears; a broad reassessment of AI-chip demand sustainability; heavy institutional selling; and leveraged-ETF rebalancing cascades.
  • Nick Beams noted on the WSWS that the chip selloff reflects the unwinding of a speculative structure in which a handful of AI-linked stocks carried global index gains.

Divided Fed holds rates as 30-year yields hit highest level since 2007

The Federal Open Market Committee voted 9-3 on July 29 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75%, the fifth consecutive hold. The three dissents came from regional Fed presidents Beth Hammack (Cleveland), Neel Kashkari (Minneapolis) and Lorie Logan (Dallas) — and all three wanted a hike, citing inflation running above the 2% target for more than five years.

Key facts:

  • 30-year Treasury yields rose above 5.2%, the highest since 2007.
  • Core PCE has risen from 3.0% in December 2025 to 3.4% in May 2026; June headline CPI was 3.5% year-on-year.
  • The FOMC statement cited “elevated uncertainty” tied partly to the Middle East conflict, alongside what it described as strong productivity growth and continued job gains.
  • Futures markets now price two 25 basis-point hikes, in September and December 2026.

A three-vote dissent bloc is highly unusual and reflects a genuine split in the American ruling class over whether to defend the value of financial assets or to avoid triggering a recession into an election period. For workers the question is settled either way: five straight years of above-target inflation has already been extracted from real wages.

Magnitude 7.1 earthquake kills at least 18 in southern Japan

A magnitude 7.1 earthquake struck 20km south of Kumamoto City on July 28, killing at least 18 people and injuring at least 62, six seriously. Around 260,000 residents were ordered to evacuate, with more than 8,800 sheltering in over 400 evacuation centers.

Key facts:

  • An Aeon Mall partially collapsed and was hit by a gas explosion; the collapse of a chimney at a Nippon Paper Industries factory killed five workers; the stone walls of Kumamoto Castle partially collapsed.
  • Nearly 34,900 homes lost power and 15,000 lost running water.
  • Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi: “There are people who are still waiting to be rescued, and it’s a race against time. We will go all out to find and rescue as many people as we can.”

Peter Symonds wrote on the WSWS that the deaths concentrated in industrial and commercial structures — a factory chimney, a shopping mall — point to the results of decades of deferred seismic retrofitting in a country that knows precisely what its geology requires.

Wildfires force 325,000 from their homes in France and Spain

More than 325,000 people have been evacuated across France (roughly 220,000–260,000) and Spain (roughly 100,000) as wildfires burn across the western Mediterranean. President Macron described the situation as “the most severe we ever recorded, the toughest since the Second World War.” Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said simply: “We are in a situation that is unprecedented.”

Key facts:

  • Fires in France’s Gironde/Bordeaux region have burned more than 100,000 acres since July 23.
  • A fire near Madrid grew past 111,000 acres before containment on July 29; a separate blaze in Castellón has burned nearly 25,000 acres, remains out of control, and has forced more than 10,000 evacuations.
  • At least 12 people were killed in an earlier Andalusia wildfire on July 10.
  • World Weather Attribution has found that the intensity of Europe’s recent heat events is now tens to hundreds of times more likely than in 2003 and was effectively unheard of 50 years ago.

The scale of evacuation is inseparable from the state of the public services expected to manage it. France’s firefighting capacity has been squeezed by successive austerity budgets even as the fire season lengthens by a matter of weeks each decade.

Manufactured confrontations in the South China Sea as ASEAN meets

Three separate clashes between Chinese and Philippine vessels occurred on July 20, 23 and 24 — all within the week of the ASEAN Foreign Ministers’ Meeting (July 18–24). The incidents centered on Second Thomas Shoal, site of the grounded BRP Sierra Madre, and Scarborough Shoal, under Chinese control since 2012.

  • Secretary of State Marco Rubio: “we’re the only treaty partner the Philippines has. We intend to live up to our treaty obligations.”
  • Washington announced $100 million in new military aid to the Philippines for 2026 and $2.5 billion in broader strategic investments across Southeast Asia; Australia doubled its maritime assistance to $18 million over four years.
  • A coordinated 14-nation statement on South China Sea conduct was issued ten days before the incidents occurred.

  • Manila says Chinese Coast Guard personnel struck a Philippine navy sailor on the head with a wooden baton and used water cannon on two vessels; Beijing says Philippine ships rammed a Chinese vessel. Foreign Secretary Ma. Theresa Lazaro and Wang Yi exchanged formal protests on the summit sidelines. Notably, no ASEAN member state issued a statement backing the Philippines.

Bolivia’s Paz government drives poverty up 6.6 points in a single year

President Rodrigo Paz Pereira, in office since November 2025, has presided over one of the sharpest social collapses in recent Latin American history. His government raised gasoline prices 87% and diesel prices 162% in a single day.

  • Poverty rose from 38.1% to 44.7% in one year — roughly 807,000 additional people pushed below the line. Extreme poverty rose from 12.8% to 16.7%.
  • Urban poverty rose 7.4 percentage points; 52% of the population is food insecure and 53.7% of young children are anemic.
  • Food inflation is running at 31.48% annually against record overall inflation of 20.4%.
  • A 53-day general strike from May 2 to June 23 paralyzed the country over fuel subsidy elimination and land privatization.
  • The cuts were imposed by Supreme Decree 5503, the “Decreto por la Patria,” which declared a national economic emergency in December 2025.
  • The cabinet is drawn directly from agribusiness: the former president of the soybean producers’ association heads development planning; the ex-president of the Eastern Chamber of Agriculture serves as productive development minister.

Two women investigating Ecuador’s president and US boat strikes found dead

Two women conducting separate investigations touching President Daniel Noboa and US military operations were killed in Ecuador in June, with no arrests in either case.

  • Monika Silva Koniuszek, 41, mother of two, was found in her Montañita home with a noose on June 8. She had been investigating cocaine trafficking connections to the Noboa family’s banana business, and had delivered evidence to the US Embassy and the DEA in April. Ecuador’s interior minister announced her death as a suicide within 24 hours — before the autopsy. A Polish autopsy concluded her death was “very probably the result of a deliberate act committed by third parties,” citing premortem head trauma. A journalist covering her case was sentenced to 30 days in prison.
  • Gloria Alexandra Bravo Cedeño, 46, a 15-year prosecutor in the Manabí office, was shot on June 14 while walking to her car in Manta; her sister Olinda was killed trying to shield her. Bravo had documented US drone strikes — carried out under Washington’s Operation Southern Spear — on the fishing vessels La Negra Francisca Duarte II and Don Maca and the disappearance of the Fiorella. She told Human Rights Watch: “The danger is always present.”

Human Rights Watch Americas director Juanita Goebertus: “The killing of prosecutor Bravo is a tragedy, but sadly not an isolated one in Ecuador.”

India’s student revolt suspended after token Modi concession

The Cockroach Janata Party (CJP) suspended its indefinite mass protest at New Delhi’s Jantar Mantar after Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan resigned — 36 days after the agitation began. The movement originated as an online satirical campaign launched by Abhijeet Dipke in May 2026 in response to the chief justice of India comparing unemployed youth to cockroaches; it accumulated more than 2 million online supporters and became a mass street movement over the May 3 leak of NEET examination papers. More than 500 public defenders on strike in New York and Detroit

More than 500 public defender workers are on strike across two organizations: 230 at Neighborhood Defender Service in Harlem and Detroit since July 24, and roughly 300 at Bronx Defenders since July 27–28. Informational pickets have appeared in Houston, San Antonio and San Marcos, Texas.   • Strikers are opposing management proposals to eliminate comprehensive healthcare coverage and impose raises of roughly 1–3% against 3.5% inflation — a real wage cut every year of the contract.

  • Will Lehman, the Mack Trucks worker and socialist candidate for UAW president, called for a unified walkout: “I send you my unconditional solidarity, and I call on all UAW members and workers in every industry to give your strike active support.”

Florida carries out first double execution in six decades

Florida executed two men on July 28, the first time a US state has carried out two executions in a single day since Arkansas in 2017, and the first double execution in Florida’s modern history.

  • James Aren Duckett, 68, a former police officer convicted in the 1987 murder of an 11-year-old girl, was pronounced dead at 1:19 p.m. The Death Penalty Information Center had flagged serious innocence concerns in his case.
  • Dominick Anthony Occhicone, 80, convicted of two 1986 murders, was pronounced dead at 6:13 p.m., becoming the oldest person executed in Florida since modern record-keeping began.
  • These were Florida’s 11th and 12th executions of 2026. Nationally there have been 19 executions this year, 12 of them in Florida. There were 47 in 2025 and 25 in 2024.
  • Governor Ron DeSantis oversaw a state-record 19 executions in 2025 — 40% of the US total — and is accelerating the pace before leaving office in January.

ICE seizing travelers at 15 airports under TSA data-sharing deal

ICE agents have begun seizing travelers at approximately 15 US airports, targeting individuals with visa issues or pending immigration applications — many facing no criminal accusation of any kind. The operation rests on a May 2025 agreement under which the TSA supplies ICE with names from its Secure Flight database, alerting agents when flagged individuals are booked to travel. Agents are working to a quota of 2,000 immigration arrests per day.

  • Chantal Morales Rojas, a software engineer, was seized at Denver airport on July 20 despite holding valid federal work authorization. Her attorney: “For more than three years, she has continued to live here with federal authorization.”
  • Lorenzo Thompson, a Southwest Airlines flight attendant with a valid work permit and a pending immigration case, was detained at Nashville airport. His union waited eight days before publicly acknowledging his detention.
  • Phu Nguyen, 57, an Australian citizen with no criminal charges, was targeted at Las Vegas airport on July 13, arrested in Los Angeles and detained at the Adelanto ICE Processing Center.
  • Reuters reporting on internal ICE data found the agency arrested more than 800 people on TSA leads between January 2025 and February 2026, and that TSA supplied ICE with records on more than 31,000 travelers. Secure Flight was created in 2007 for counterterrorism watchlist screening.

Safety-net cuts projected to destroy over 1 million jobs in 2026

A Commonwealth Fund analysis projects that federal Medicaid and SNAP cuts enacted under the Trump tax and spending law will cost more than 1 million jobs in 2026 — including 477,000 in healthcare — and roughly $95 billion in state-level GDP.

  • SNAP enrollment is already down more than 10% nationally.
  • The deeper Medicaid funding reductions do not begin until January 2027, meaning the projected job losses precede the largest cuts.
  • The healthcare job losses will fall hardest on rural hospitals and nursing homes, where Medicaid reimbursement is the dominant revenue source.

Class mortality gap in US COVID deaths quantified

A WSWS analysis by Benjamin Mateus assembled the mortality data on the American pandemic, and the class gradient is the central finding. The United States recorded roughly 1.2 million confirmed COVID-19 deaths — 16% of the global total from 4% of the world’s population — with excess mortality analyses placing the true domestic toll near 1.5 million.

  • Age-adjusted COVID-19 death rates were nearly threefold higher for working-class Americans (165 per 100,000) than for those with bachelor’s degrees (57 per 100,000).
  • By 2021, the educational life expectancy gap reached 8.5 years: 83.31 years for degree holders against 74.82 years for those without.
  • Adults aged 35–44 saw a 44.5% increase in death rates in 2021 compared with 2019 — the largest relative increase of any age group, and squarely the prime working-age cohort.
  • A 2025 analysis found 14.7 million US excess deaths relative to 21 other high-income countries between 1980 and 2023; by 2023, US adults aged 25–44 faced mortality 2.6 times higher than international peers.
  • Sources include the WHO excess mortality assessment published in Nature (2023), the Society of Actuaries Research Institute (2022), and Case and Deaton in Brookings Papers on Economic Activity (2023).

Phase 1 inactivated Ebola vaccine results published in NEJM

The New England Journal of Medicine published Phase 1 results on July 29 for iEvac-Z, an inactivated Ebola vaccine tested in healthy volunteers in Japan. Glycoprotein-specific IgG seroconversion reached 100% by week 8 in both the full analysis population (29 participants) and the per-protocol population (26). Geometric mean titers rose 4.7-fold in the low-dose group and 11.6-fold in the high-dose group. A neutralizing antibody response occurred in 21% of low-dose and 50% of high-dose recipients — a substantial caveat, since neutralizing antibodies are the more meaningful correlate of protection. An inactivated platform would be far easier to store and distribute in West and Central Africa than the current vector-based vaccines.

Measles and H5N1 surveillance roundup

  • Measles cases reached 2,318 in 2025, the highest US total since 1991, driven by collapsing MMR coverage; the US lost its measles elimination status in November 2025.
  • CDC’s MMWR published a July 2026 report on genotype-specific detection of measles virus through wastewater surveillance in Wisconsin, dating to February 2026. Maryland, California and Connecticut are piloting wastewater as an early-warning system for measles.
  • H5N1 continues to circulate in wild birds, poultry and dairy herds across the US. Human infections remain limited, but CDC advises providers to test hospitalized influenza patients for avian exposure — guidance that depends on a surveillance apparatus now being dismantled.
  • Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has forced out more than 20,000 employees, dismissed all 17 voting members of the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, terminated 49 scientific advisory committees, and cancelled $500 million in mRNA research.

FERC orders grid operators to rewrite rules for AI data centers

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission issued show-cause orders to the six largest US grid operators, directing them to defend or rewrite interconnection rules for large loads. The orders set deadlines for defining processes to handle gigawatt-scale load requests, evaluate co-located generation, assign upgrade costs and ensure sufficient generation exists when projects come online.

  • Virginia imposed a $0.011/kWh consumption tax on data center electricity from July 1, projected to raise $600 million annually.
  • Oracle is challenging a Wisconsin court decision requiring hyperscale data center developers to post hundreds of millions in financial security.
  • The unresolved question in every jurisdiction is who pays for the grid buildout: the AI companies, or ratepayers.

Arts and culture

Glen Hansard, 1969–2026. The Irish singer-songwriter died in a single-vehicle motorcycle crash near Chapelizod, on the outskirts of Dublin, in the early hours of Wednesday, July 29. He was 56, and is survived by his wife and a three-year-old son.

Hansard’s trajectory is worth noting precisely because it ran against the grain of the culture industry. He left school at 13 to busk on Grafton Street, formed The Frames in 1990, and spent nearly two decades in the margins of the Irish music scene before Once — a film made for roughly €112,000 on handheld cameras, in which he played a Dublin busker opposite Markéta Irglová — won the 2008 Academy Award for Best Original Song with “Falling Slowly.” His acceptance speech (“Make art!”) became one of the few genuinely unguarded moments in the ceremony’s recent history.

What distinguished the work was its refusal of the sentimentality that the material invited. Once was a film about two working people — an immigrant Czech musician and a vacuum repairman — for whom art is squeezed into the interstices of wage labour, and it declined to give them a romantic resolution. Hansard’s songwriting carried the same tension between lyricism and an unsentimental view of circumstance. He was also consistently visible on questions of housing and homelessness in Dublin, playing benefit concerts through the years of Ireland’s housing catastrophe.

Roger Waters and Mona Miari reimagine “Comfortably Numb.” Kevin Reed reviewed on the WSWS the nine-minute reworking of the 1979 Pink Floyd track, released June 12 as a collaboration between Waters and Palestinian singer Mona Miari. The arrangement — piano, strings, percussion and layered English and Arabic vocals — inverts the original’s meaning. Where the 1979 song rendered a private, chemical anesthesia, the new version turns numbness outward into a description of collective political apathy toward Gaza. Miari’s Arabic verses run as continuous counterpoint; Waters’ new English passages reference the Nakba. The video incorporates Gaza footage by Palestinian photographer Suhail Nassar, and proceeds go to the Palestine Children’s Relief Fund.

Waters describes the work as part of an “existential battle for the whole human race” and invites listeners to “join the resistance to the occupation.” Miari frames her role differently and more directly: “I am my people. I am telling my story.” Whatever one’s assessment of Waters’ broader political positions, the piece is a serious attempt to make a canonical work of the rock repertoire carry the weight of a contemporary atrocity — an artistic gesture almost entirely absent from an industry that has spent two years in disciplined silence on Gaza.

State Department report blames Cuba for social unrest: Rubio prepares total war at home and abroad — Andrea Lobo

The State Department released a 100-page report on July 20 titled “Cuba: the Capital of 21st Century Communism,” which asserts that major American social movements — from the civil rights movement through the George Floyd protests — were substantially directed by the Cuban government. The report includes formulations such as: “The black American in Detroit… was a colonized subject, a revolutionary brother of the Vietnamese peasant.”

The operative content is not historical but legal. The report designates numerous US organizations as having Cuban ties, which enables their prosecution under existing terrorism statutes without any new legislation. Four days before publication, Rubio convened a “Ministerial on the Resurgence of Political Terrorism” attended by 66 countries, telling participants: “It is time to crush this evil forever.”

Lobo notes the scale disparity underlying the claim: US GDP is 300 times Cuba’s and US military spending roughly 5,000 times greater. She also cites the human cost of the blockade — a 148% increase in Cuban infant mortality between 2018 and 2025, approximately 1,800 infant deaths.

Jacobin / DSA

“Socialist Francesca Hong Could Be Wisconsin’s Next Governor” promotes a DSA member’s bid for the Democratic gubernatorial nomination as an advance for socialism. The framing is the essential content: the article’s own subject is quoted saying “Democrats have to win.” Socialism here is defined as the capture of a bourgeois party’s nomination — the entire question of independent working-class political organization is not so much rejected as never raised. Wisconsin’s Democratic Party is the same organization that has administered the state’s manufacturing decline and public-sector wage suppression for four decades.

“Things Keep Getting Worse for Brazil’s Bolsonaro Dynasty” chronicles the Bolsonaro family’s legal and political collapse and treats Lula’s electoral position as the bulwark against the far right. There is no examination of the PT government’s own record — its fiscal framework, its agribusiness accommodations, its role in demobilizing the working class — which is precisely what created the space Bolsonarism occupied. The article’s operative advice is to vote for Lula, which is also the advice of most of the Brazilian bourgeoisie’s liberal wing.

Jacobin’s day-to-day orientation remains the subordination of every social question to the electoral fortunes of the Democratic Party in the US and its analogues abroad. The LACMA architecture piece is the exception that clarifies the rule: when the subject is a museum rather than a party, Jacobin’s writers are capable of a genuine class critique.

Left Voice

Nothing was published within the strict 24-hour window. The two most recent pieces are worth noting. “China’s Upsurging Strike Trend” documents rising labour unrest in China, correctly identifying the shift from offensive to defensive struggles and calling for independent, self-organized workers’ organizations outside the state-controlled ACFTU. “India’s Massive Gen Z ‘Cockroach’ Protests Show How to Fight Modi and the Right” insists that “nobody is going to save the working class except us” and rejects reliance on bourgeois politicians. Neither piece contains the reformist adaptations characteristic of Jacobin. The persistent problem with the Morenoite tradition Left Voice represents is not electoral opportunism of the DSA type but the substitution of movement enthusiasm for the construction of a party on a definite programmatic foundation — the Indian article’s confidence is not accompanied by any account of why the movement it praises has just been suspended.

Liberation News / PSL

“Defend our Haitian neighbors! We’ve stopped ICE before and can do it again!” frames the raids on Haitian communities as a continuation of a century of US destabilization of Haiti, calling for mass mobilization to abolish ICE. “Mass action and workplace walkouts are needed to defend TPS holders and defeat ICE’s surge” explicitly criticizes the mainstream “progressive movement” for relying on courts and Democratic officeholders instead of strikes and walkouts.

The call for workplace action is correct and stands in sharp contrast to Jacobin’s orientation. What is absent is any conclusion about political power: the PSL’s perspective terminates in pressure on the state to reverse a policy, without addressing the party question or the fact that the union apparatus — which would have to authorize such walkouts — is politically committed to the Democratic Party. The campist framework also means the PSL’s opposition to US imperialism does not extend to a class analysis of the Chinese, Russian or Iranian states.

Socialist Alternative

“Justice For Corey Ruiz—Fight Police Brutality With A One-Day Citywide Shutdown” responds to the Madison police killing with a call for a one-day political strike and explicitly states the Democratic Party is “incapable of leading” the fight. The demand set, however, is “defunding the police” and “democratic community control” — a program for the administrative reform of the repressive apparatus of the capitalist state, which assumes that apparatus can be made to serve the community that it exists to police. “Student Protests Force Modi Into Retreat” calls for a general strike and a revolutionary socialist party in India. The tension between the two is characteristic: internationally, revolutionary phraseology; domestically, a program of municipal reform demands.

Socialist Worker / SWP UK

“Kenyan president cites ‘benefits’ of British army base despite abuse scandals” uses the Agnes Wanjiru murder case to argue for the closure of British and US bases abroad — a clear anti-imperialist line. “Why are Kashmiris rising up against Pakistani state?” opposes both Indian and Pakistani control of Kashmir and calls for self-determination through revolutionary movements in both countries.

“What Burnham should do to solve the crisis in social care” is the day’s significant flag. The piece is structured as policy advice to a sitting Labour prime minister — criticizing him for not breaking with the Starmer-Reeves fiscal rules and proposing a wealth tax. On the day Burnham’s government deepened Britain’s military commitment against Russia, the SWP addressed him as a potential instrument of social reform. This is the national-reformist reflex of the Cliffite tradition in its purest form.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

Now publishing at communist.red under the masthead The Communist. “India’s Cockroaches rise: no retreat until Modi falls” argues the student protesters erred in pausing after a partial win and calls for indefinite protest to bring down the government. “Victory is the fuel, not the finish line!” reports RCP-organized solidarity protests in Britain and argues the movement must extend to workers and farmers.

No tendency reviewed today made any direct reference to the ICFI or the WSWS.
