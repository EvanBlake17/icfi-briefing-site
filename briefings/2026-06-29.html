1. US-Iran ceasefire collapses as IRGC strikes US bases in Kuwait and Bahrain

The June 17–18 ceasefire memorandum between Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian has collapsed. After fresh US airstrikes on Iranian military sites at Sirik and Qeshm — the second consecutive day of US strikes near the Strait of Hormuz — the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps launched a joint Navy-Aerospace operation between 2:00 and 3:00 a.m. local time on June 28, targeting eight US military installations across the Gulf. The war, which began February 28, 2026 when the US and Israel launched an air war against Iran, has now killed over 3,400 Iranians.

As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, both Republican and Democratic establishment factions oppose the ceasefire “as a sign of weakness” and are pushing for further escalation despite demonstrated Iranian military capability and broad popular opposition.

Key facts: - Confirmed targets: Ali Al Salem Airbase in Kuwait and the US Fifth Fleet headquarters at Salman Port in Bahrain, struck with ballistic missiles and drones; the IRGC claims all were “destroyed.” - The Fifth Fleet headquarters has been rendered unusable, with a reported $400 million required to rebuild; more than 20 US regional bases have been struck and 13 US troops killed. - Bahrain called the attack “a dangerous escalation”; Kuwait condemned a “flagrant violation” of its sovereignty. A Qatari national was killed by shrapnel from an Iranian drone. - Iranian casualties include 376 children; the Pentagon has run up $29 billion in costs, with an $87.6 billion White House supplemental spending request. - Trump warned Iran “will no longer exist” if strikes continue; Tehran threatened a “complete halt” to negotiations.

2. Iran reasserts sole control of Strait of Hormuz as 14 million barrels a day stay offline

The Strait of Hormuz crisis has re-intensified alongside the ceasefire collapse. On June 29, Iran’s Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi warned against “any attempt to adopt new or separate arrangements” for the waterway, declaring that Iran holds “sole control.” An adviser to Supreme Leader Khamenei separately warned Oman against aligning with Gulf states on Hormuz transit arrangements.

The strait — through which roughly 25% of world seaborne oil and 20% of global LNG normally transits — has been largely blocked since February 28. The IMF warns the war has “halted momentum in global growth” and risks a “major energy crisis.”

Key facts: - Global oil supply has been cut by an estimated 14 million barrels per day since the closure began. - Oil prices surged more than 50% at the height of the conflict before retreating on mid-June ceasefire hopes. - On June 27, the US Navy’s Joint Maritime Information Center announced a widened navigational corridor near Oman.

3. Venezuela twin earthquakes kill more than 1,450 as US troops deploy

Two powerful earthquakes struck Venezuela’s coast Wednesday evening in rapid succession — a magnitude 7.2 followed seconds later by a 7.5 — leaving at least 1,450 dead and roughly 3,150 injured, with “tens of thousands” reported missing. The capital Caracas, the main port region of La Guaira state, and the town of Caraballeda suffered severe building collapses. Venezuela is not historically earthquake-prone, catching residents unprepared.

The WSWS Perspective by Andrea Lobo frames the catastrophe as “a crime of US imperialism” rooted in decades of sanctions and a January 2026 US military invasion (see WSWS coverage below).

Key facts: - Rescue teams from Bolivia, Ecuador and El Salvador are on the ground; a 60-year-old woman was extracted alive after 86 hours trapped. - 100 US Air Force personnel arrived to repair Simón Bolívar International Airport; 130 US Marines deployed to reopen La Guaira port for aid delivery. - Infrastructure recovery: La Guaira electricity 75% restored, water 68%, roads 90%.

Sources ABC News Venezuela earthquakes: live updates · WSWS The earthquake disaster in Venezuela: A crime of US imperialism

4. Gaza war reaches 1,000 days as IDF warns Hamas is rearming

June 29 marks 1,000 days of fighting since Hamas’s October 7, 2023 attack. IDF Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir says the military is at “a strategic crossroads in the war.” Senior Military Intelligence and Southern Command officers warned Zamir that Hamas’s military wing is producing “hundreds of explosive devices and anti-tank missiles every month” and preparing for renewed war. The Palestine Red Crescent reported two killed in a strike on Beit Lahiya in northern Gaza on June 29.

A UN Commission of Inquiry concluded on June 23 that Israel’s “deliberate targeting of Gaza children” constitutes part of genocide.

Key facts: - A senior Israeli official reported Iranian cyberattacks surged from ~1,600 incidents in June 2025 to 4,800 this month — a threefold increase. - The 1,000-day mark arrives with no ceasefire in Gaza, even as Israel’s separate arrangements with Hezbollah and Iran fracture.

5. Ukraine records 197 combat engagements in a day as drones hit Russian refineries

Ukraine’s General Staff recorded 197 combat engagements along the front on June 28, with the heaviest fighting in the Sloviansk and Pokrovsk sectors. On the night of June 28, Ukrainian drones struck two oil refineries on Russian territory, igniting a fire at a refinery in Slovyansk-on-Kuban, Krasnodar Krai. Al Jazeera characterized the moment as Ukraine’s “first chance to win” as “war horrors come home to Russia.” The WSWS frames the conflict as a NATO proxy war prolonged at the expense of working-class lives on both sides.

Key facts: - Ukraine reported Russian losses on June 28: 1,250 troops killed and 63 artillery systems destroyed. - Russian shelling of Kharkiv and 17 surrounding settlements injured 7 civilians; 25 were injured in the Sumy region, including 3 children.

6. Sudan civil war: 1,000 civilians killed by drones in five months as 33 million face starvation

Sudan’s civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the RSF paramilitary has entered its fourth year. Drone strikes killed more than 1,000 Sudanese civilians in just the first five months of 2026 — a 600% increase over 2024–2025. More than 33 million Sudanese are now in what aid agencies call the “world’s largest hunger crisis.”

In May, Sudan formally accused Ethiopia and the UAE of orchestrating drone attacks on Khartoum International Airport, alleging drones launched from Bahir Dar in Ethiopia and military support routed through the UAE to the RSF. Bloomberg’s June 26 report highlights Western energy companies scouting Sudanese oil fields even amid active fighting — underscoring the resource dimension of the conflict.

Key facts: - The SAF has retaken Khartoum; the RSF controls Darfur and parts of the west. - Chad shut its eastern border with Sudan indefinitely in February over cross-border incursions.

8. US tariffs reach highest level since 1993 amid global trade war

Trump’s tariff regime now constitutes the largest US tax increase as a share of GDP since 1993, amounting to an average burden of roughly $1,500 per US household in 2026. On June 3, the administration proposed additional tariffs of up to 12.5% on imports from 60 countries — including China, the EU and Japan — over alleged forced-labor trade practices. The IMF warns the combination of the Iran war and widening trade restrictions has “halted momentum in global growth.”

Key facts: - A June 11 US-China deal locked in existing 30% tariffs (20% fentanyl + 10% reciprocal) while pausing higher tariffs for 60 days. - The EU has moved to eliminate tariffs on US industrial goods to de-escalate transatlantic tensions; US pressure is driving Europe and Brazil into closer trade ties.

Colombia’s right wins presidency as Petro concedes

Right-wing political outsider Abelardo de la Espriella won Colombia’s June 21 presidential runoff with 49.66% against 48.70% for left-wing senator Iván Cepeda of the ruling Historic Pact. Trump religion commission attacks church-state separation

A 224-page draft report from Trump’s Religious Liberty Commission — chaired by Texas Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick with Vice Chairman Ben Carson, and including Franklin Graham, Cardinal Timothy Dolan and Dr. Phil — urges replacing the separation of church and state with the building of “bridges” between them, casting the doctrine as a misapplied invention rather than a founding principle. As Patrick Martin notes on the WSWS, the commission ignores that most early American states eventually disestablished their official churches.

UAW monitor finds Fain abused office, threatens sanctions

Federal Monitor Neil Barofsky’s report, filed June 25 in the Eastern District of Michigan, found UAW President Shawn Fain exhibited a “recurring pattern of retaliation” and “abused the authority of his office” in matters involving his fiancée and her sister. US measles cases top 2,100 as vaccination rates fall

The US recorded 2,134 confirmed measles cases for 2026 as of June 25 — among the highest annual totals in decades — as childhood vaccination rates continue to slide. Public-health specialists warn that the erosion of measles-mumps-rubella coverage, compounded by the Trump administration’s hostility to vaccine mandates (now reflected in the Religious Liberty Commission’s call for expanded vaccination exemptions), is reopening the door to diseases that were effectively eliminated. The outbreak is a direct consequence of the defunding and political delegitimization of public-health infrastructure.

Lancet study finds HPV vaccine prevented cervical-cancer deaths

A study in The Lancet led by Queen Mary University of London and funded by Cancer Research UK estimates the HPV vaccine has prevented around 200 deaths from cervical cancer in England, finding that no women aged 20–24 died of the disease between 2020 and 2024 — the first such period on record. The result is a striking demonstration of what socialized, publicly funded preventive medicine can achieve, in sharp contrast to the market-driven and increasingly anti-science direction of US health policy.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural event documented this cycle is the 2026 World Press Photo exhibition at De Nieuwe Kerk in Amsterdam, reviewed for the WSWS by Daniel Woreck. The exhibition’s prize-winning works confront the central catastrophes of the present: Carol Guzy’s Photo of the Year, “Separated by ICE,” depicts an ICE detention; Saber Nuraldin’s first-finalist image captures Palestinians struggling for food aid in Gaza; and Saher Alghorra’s series documents a family breaking the Ramadan fast in the ruins of their home. The WSWS argues that while these images document real suffering with genuine artistic power, the World Press Photo organization claims a false neutrality — partnering with imperialist-aligned institutions such as Bellingcat while presenting crises as isolated tragedies rather than systemic products of capitalism and war. The contradiction is instructive: photographers risk their lives to capture the human cost of imperialist violence, while the institutional framing strips that violence of its political authorship.

The exhibition’s themes — forced separation under ICE, famine engineered as a weapon in Gaza — dovetail directly with the political content of this briefing, a reminder that the most serious documentary art of the period is inseparable from the wars and repression dominating world events.

Roundup:

  • Music — obituary: The death of musician Oliver Tree Nickell, 32, in a Rio de Janeiro accident on June 14, continues to reverberate through the music world ahead of a July 25 memorial. A genre-blending performer who styled himself as a multidisciplinary creator across music, film and performance, Tree had been mid-way through an ambitious seven-continent world tour. His death at the height of his career is a loss to a generation of younger artists experimenting across forms.
  • Film: The summer festival circuit continues, with the Annecy International Animation Film Festival (June 21–27) and a slate of regional documentary festivals foregrounding work on artists, activists and questions of identity. The proliferation of identity-themed documentary programming reflects the institutional pull of identitarian framing in arts funding — a tendency the WSWS framework distinguishes sharply from art that situates the individual within social and class relations.
  • Visual arts: The World Press Photo show, traveling internationally, remains the period’s flashpoint where photojournalism, war and the politics of cultural institutions collide.

The dominant pattern across the cultural field is clear: the most vital work engages directly with war, displacement and state violence, even as the institutions that platform it work to neutralize its political implications.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “The earthquake disaster in Venezuela: A crime of US imperialism” by Andrea Lobo — read in full on wsws.org (confirmed June 29, 2026).

The earthquake disaster in Venezuela: A crime of US imperialism — Andrea Lobo

Beyond the casualty figures reported above, the Perspective advances a distinct historical and political analysis. It traces US interference in Venezuela from Theodore Roosevelt’s dispatch of warships during the 1902–1903 Venezuela Crisis, through Washington’s backing of the dictators Juan Vicente Gómez and Marcos Pérez Jiménez, down to the present — a century-long record that, Lobo argues, systematically stripped the country of the capacity to build resilient infrastructure.

The article’s pivotal claim is that in January 2026 the Trump administration militarily invaded Venezuela, abducted President Nicolás Maduro, and installed a puppet government under Delcy Rodríguez; the ensuing six months of “chaos and disorganization” crippled the state’s disaster-response capacity. Lobo cites UN Special Rapporteurs who found US sanctions “produced mass suffering and contributed to over 100,000 excess deaths” before the quake. Drawing an explicit analogy to Haiti after 2010, the piece argues the US military deployment serves to consolidate imperial control, not deliver aid. The programmatic conclusion calls on Venezuelan workers to “organize independent rank-and-file relief committees” outside both the US-installed government and the Maduro-era bureaucracy, and demands expropriation of US corporate holdings in the oil sector as the basis for reconstruction.

Rank-and-file candidate Will Lehman issues statement to Nexteer workers — Our reporters

This WSWS-only labor story warrants attention. Lehman’s statement follows the June 26 ratification of a Nexteer Automotive contract that passed narrowly (54–46% among production workers) after three prior rejections by margins of 96%, 73% and 55%. Lehman argues the vote is “null and void” due to voting irregularities, use of company property for balloting, and the firing of dissident worker Antwiane Sanders. He demands Sanders’s reinstatement, recall of the bargaining committee, and rank-and-file committees independent of the UAW apparatus. The contract preserves the same $27/hour top rate as 2005, even as inflation has eroded real wages. The dispute is a concrete application of the analysis in the Fain monitor story above: the same apparatus implicated in Barofsky’s report is here pushing through a contract over repeated rank-and-file rejection.

New Zealand Greens advance fraudulent wealth tax policy — Tom Peters

Tom Peters critiques the NZ Green Party’s proposed 2.5% annual wealth tax on assets over $10 million, 33% inheritance tax on transfers over $1 million, and increases to the top income (39%→45%) and corporate (28%→33%) rates. The key data: the policy would affect only 0.3% of New Zealanders and raise just $5.15 billion, while the country’s ten richest billionaires alone hold $64 billion. The WSWS notes the Greens’ record of advancing such proposals at election time only to abandon them in Labour-led coalitions, and calls for an independent socialist party rather than capitalist reform.

Australia: Permanent jobs under attack at Smith’s Brisbane chip factory — Martin Scott

PepsiCo is driving to eliminate protections for over 400 permanent workers at its Smith’s Snackfood factory in Tingalpa, Brisbane — abolishing priority-of-hours rules and letting casuals operate forklifts previously restricted to permanent staff. Level 3 wages stand at $1,451.51/week while Brisbane median apartment rent has surged 62.5% to $660/week since 2021; Smith’s wages rose 18.17% over five years against a 22.7% CPI increase. Three unions (AWU, UWU, ETU) have filed claims, but the WSWS calls them insufficient and demands rank-and-file committees fighting for a 40% pay rise and an end to all labour-hire.

Pseudo-left press review

  • Jacobin / DSA — solid ICE and Lebanon exposés, but no break with the Democratic Party; “left-labor presidential candidate” line
  • Left Voice — among the cleaner anti-imperialist framings this cycle; links trans defense to anti-war politics
  • Liberation News / PSL — the cycle’s most egregious capitulation: Democratic primary wins recast as “the movement for socialism”
  • Socialist Alternative — competent economic analysis; class-independent electoral line but worth watching for lesser-evil drift
  • Socialist Worker / SWP UK — strong strike reportage, but trade-union militancy substituted for a political program
  • Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT) — sharp polemics against the imploding Starmer government

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output this cycle is journalistically competent but politically contained. “GEO Group Claims Qualified Immunity From Forced-Labor Lawsuits” is straight exposé reporting on ICE detention abuses, raising no demand beyond legal accountability. “Israel Is Emptying Lebanon of Its People” condemns Israeli ethnic cleansing but stops short of implicating US arming and funding, and issues no call to break with the Democratic Party over its war support. “No Illusions and No Retreat for Union Militants at Labor Notes” frames militancy as a pressure lever rather than a challenge to the political system. Read against Eric Blanc’s recent “We Need a Left-Labor Presidential Candidate,” the orientation is unmistakable: a strategy of running candidates on or adjacent to the Democratic ballot line — class collaboration in textbook form. Overall: capable journalism harnessed to the subordination of the working class to the Democratic Party.

Left Voice

Left Voice remains among the more consistent tendencies in this survey. “At This Year’s Queer Liberation March, Unite To Defend Trans Rights and Oppose Imperialist War” explicitly links LGBTQ+ defense to anti-imperialism and calls for independent class politics rather than lobbying Democrats. “Has Israel Crossed the Threshold of Annexation in the West Bank?” situates de facto annexation within US imperial strategy. “Elections in Colombia Signal Another Victory for the Right” frames the right-wing wave as a product of the limits of left-reformist governments rather than calling for support to them. Consistent with its FT-CI Trotskyist line, Left Voice shows no visible capitulation on imperialism this cycle — though its relationship to the broader milieu it orbits remains a question.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL is the cycle’s clearest capitulation to bourgeois electoralism. “New York Is Ready for Socialism” celebrates the June 23 Democratic primary wins by Mamdani allies over AIPAC-backed incumbents as evidence of socialist advance — though these candidates ran on the Democratic Party ballot line. “Mamdani’s Victory and the Movement for Socialism” frames a mayoralty won as a Democrat as a step toward socialism, continuing the PSL’s documented pattern of critical support for Democratic candidates (Biden 2020). Only “Another Murder by Federal Agents in Minneapolis — Expand the General Strike!” offers stronger class-struggle content with its anti-repression framing. The dominant orientation is national-reformism: substituting electoral gains inside the bourgeois party for independent working-class organization.

Socialist Alternative

“AI IPOs: Next Stage of the Bubble?” offers competent Marxist analysis of SpaceX/AI IPO mania as speculative capital without adventurist conclusions. “Trans Healthcare Under Siege — Fight Texas’ ‘Detransition Clinic’” mounts a militant defense framed in class terms, calling for direct action over lobbying. “Which Way Forward for the Left? — International Marxism Issue #3” surveys global left formations. As the US section of the ISA/CWI tendency, SA generally contests elections on its own ballot line rather than inside the Democrats — distinguishing it from DSA/Jacobin — but its trajectory heading into the 2026 midterms is worth watching for any drift toward lesser-evilism.

Socialist Worker / SWP UK

“Reports Round-Up: Rail Strikes Go Cross-Country” and “Unions Round-Up: Over 1,000 Council Workers Strike for Pay” provide the SWP’s characteristic rank-and-file strike reportage with an agitational tone but no political framework beyond trade-union militancy. “Posie Parker Supporters Outnumbered Five to One in East London” covers anti-fascist mobilization through the SUTR front. The persistent limitation is the substitution of industrial action for a revolutionary program; the SWP’s continued channeling of work through Labour-aligned unions leaves the Labour bureaucracy’s political grip unchallenged even as the Starmer government implodes.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

The RCP’s polemics are the sharpest against the British establishment this cycle. “Labour’s Defence Minister Resigns: Another Shattering Blow for Starmer” treats the Healey and Carns resignations as symptoms of a terminal government crisis, framed as an opening for the RCP with no illusions in Starmer. “Anti-Muslim Attacks in Edinburgh: We Must Mobilise to Protect Ourselves” calls for working-class self-organization against racist violence, avoiding the liberal “report it to the police” reflex. “Ask The Communist: What Can We Do in Britain to Fight Imperialism?” tackles a question most other tendencies dodge. The IMT’s aggressive post-2023 rebranding and growth give it momentum, though its legacy of Labour entryism remains worth scrutinizing as it operates more independently.

The blockage of 25% of world seaborne oil since February has driven energy, food and transport costs higher for workers worldwide. The IMF frames its “major energy crisis” warning around geopolitical management, not the interests of those hit hardest. A socialist analysis would tie the energy shock directly to the decision by the US and Israeli governments to initiate war against Iran.

The collapse of these companies under shifting subsidy regimes demonstrates that the climate transition, left to the market, is hostage to speculative capital and political whim.
