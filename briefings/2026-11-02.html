1. US prepares new strikes on Iran as tankers burn in Hormuz

On the eve of the US midterm elections, Washington and Tel Aviv are openly discussing when to resume the war on Iran. Axios reports that Netanyahu wants strikes immediately after Tuesday’s vote, while Trump prefers to let Qatar-mediated talks run a little longer. The two spoke by phone Saturday. MS NOW, citing three senior Gulf Arab officials, reports that the administration told Gulf governments to prepare for strikes as early as November 4. The Wall Street Journal reported a possible large attack on nuclear sites and infrastructure.

The Strait of Hormuz remains the flashpoint. On Monday the IRGC said two crude tankers caught fire after hitting mines in the strait. At least four tankers were reportedly hit on Friday and Saturday, and Gulf states condemned an attack on a Qatari LNG carrier. Iran attacks vessels that refuse to coordinate with its “Persian Gulf Strait Authority.” Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said talks on a two-day plan to reopen the strait are under way and that he hopes the US has “enough sense to choose diplomacy.” As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, the plan to bomb “the day after” the vote shows the war’s timing is set by the US political calendar, with the Democrats keeping silent.

Key facts: - Trump: “We still have to take care of, I would say one or two sites, but we can do that in 10 minutes.” He said the war will end “very soon, one way or the other,” that Iran will either make a deal or “we’ll just end it,” and, at a Halloween party, “The only reason why I’m not finished with Iran is I want to be nice.” At an Iowa rally he spoke of giving Tehran “the hammer.” - Iran’s posture: Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei called for “maximum deterrence” and an immediate response “without geographical restriction.” IRGC spokesperson Hossein Mohebbi said: “In a conflict with America, there are no geographical limitations.” Army spokesperson Mohammad Akraminia said a new war “could last longer than the previous conflict.” Tehran has also warned that seabed cables and regional infrastructure could be targeted. - Iran’s conditions: Security chief Mohsen Rezaei listed what Tehran wants before normal transit resumes: an end to the US naval blockade, oil sanctions waivers, unfrozen assets, a halt to fighting on all fronts including Lebanon, and recognition of an Iran-Oman arrangement for Hormuz. He called a letter from Vice President JD Vance a “positive” change in tone. - US military buildup: The USS Theodore Roosevelt, carrying about 9,000 Marines and sailors, is due in late November as the third carrier in the region. More Patriot batteries have reportedly been sent to Qatar and Saudi Arabia. - Economic siege: A US Treasury report on “Operation Economic Outcast,” publicized by Axios, found no new oil loaded at Iran’s terminals since August 25. It put Iranian inflation near 90% year on year in September and described the economy as “crippled” but not collapsing. - Civil defense: Iranian officials have identified 14 electricity-dependent sectors that would need support if the war resumes, and they are weighing “network islanding” of the power grid. - Analysts: “Each side wants the other to act first” (Negar Mortazavi, Center for International Policy). Tehran aims to make another attack “so costly and unpredictable” that Washington reconsiders (Hamidreza Azizi, International Crisis Group).

2. Mass youth revolt spreads across Germany and France

The youth protest movement that began in French high schools in late September is now a Europe-wide phenomenon. In Germany on Saturday, October 31, the organizer “GenZ regelt!” (“Gen Z has got this!”) counted about 120,000 marchers in more than 30 cities. Their demands were education funding, relief from the cost of living, and opposition to soaring military spending and a new military service model that many fear will lead to conscription. Signs read “Time for resistance” and “Merz must go.” The daytime marches were largely peaceful, but police used water cannon and pepper spray in Frankfurt, Hamburg and Cologne that evening. A police union official said officers “intervened very decisively and also forcefully.” According to the WSWS, Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt blamed “violent left-wing groups” and people from migrant backgrounds for the evening clashes.

In France, the Interior Ministry counted about 320,000 protesters on Saturday, including 70,000 in Paris. Organizers counted nearly half a million. It was the third consecutive weekend of mass protest, after 550,000 on October 17 and 410,000 on October 24 by official counts. The student group “Lycéens!” says high school blockades restart today, Monday, as classes resume after the two-week holiday, and will intensify from Tuesday. Macron’s concessions include a new university admissions platform, loans for school renovations, and smaller class sizes for 10th grade only. Lycéens! head Pierre Sourzat called them “smoke and mirrors.” In a Perspective on the WSWS, Johannes Stern argued that the German protests are part of an international movement “on a collision course with capitalism.” He called for rank-and-file committees and a Europe-wide general strike.

Key facts: - France, economic stakes: The Chamber of Commerce puts retailers’ losses from three weeks of unrest above €1 billion. Fitch has revised its outlook on French debt to negative, public debt is near 120% of GDP, and the economy shrank in the third quarter. - France, repression: A court upheld a ban on the traditional Nation–Bastille march route. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez warned of firm responses to Halloween-night unrest, and the government is preparing measures to make protesters pay for riot damage. - France, the unions: The union leaderships met Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu on October 21 and pushed national cross-sector action back to November 5. - Germany, voices: Marius, a 23-year-old care worker: “the situation is slowly reaching boiling point… this just isn’t working anymore.” Milla, 20: it is “really important to show solidarity with all the other protests currently happening across Europe.” - Politics: Marine Le Pen leads polls for April’s French presidential election and has gained from the unrest. The German organizers explicitly distanced themselves from “extremist groups… no matter from which side.” - Next dates: Blockades of French universities and high schools are set for Tuesday and Thursday, cross-sector strikes in France for November 5, and international demonstrations for November 20.

3. US heads into midterms with $11 billion in ad spending

All 435 House seats, 35 Senate seats and 39 governorships are on Tuesday’s ballot. More than 51 million Americans have already voted. Democrats need a net gain of three House seats and four Senate seats to win both chambers. Their lead in national polls is wide: the RealClearPolling average has Democrats at 51.5% and Republicans at 43.1%, and Emerson has a 10-point Democratic lead. Analyst Natalie Jackson: “We can be fairly confident that Democrats will win the House majority, but the Senate picture is unclear.” The anticipated Democratic gains reflect hostility to Trump rather than enthusiasm for his opponents. Democratic strategist Celinda Lake conceded that “the feelings about the Democrats are negative, too.”

The scale of oligarchic money is unprecedented. AdImpact estimates $11.21 billion in ad spending and reservations as of October 26, against $3.2 billion for the entire 2024 presidential cycle.

Key facts: - Top donors: Elon Musk about $446 million, including more than $150 million for Ken Paxton in Texas; Richard and Elizabeth Uihlein about $150 million; Jeff and Janice Yass about $110 million. On the Democratic side: George Soros about $102 million and Michael Bloomberg $84.2 million plus a further $36 million. - Costliest Senate races: Texas, Talarico vs. Paxton, more than $534 million (nearly $300 million from outside groups for Paxton). Ohio, Husted vs. Brown, more than $435 million. Michigan, El-Sayed vs. Rogers, about $333 million. - Party committees: As of October 14, the RNC had raised about $341 million and the DNC about $252 million. - Key Senate races: In Kansas, Democrat Adam Hamilton leads Sen. Roger Marshall 52–43 (Washington Post–Schar School). Iowa, Michigan, Texas, North Carolina and Alaska are also seen as deciding control. - The economy: Rasmussen finds 48% of likely voters rank the economy as their top issue, and Ipsos puts Trump’s approval on the economy at 34%. Energy prices driven by the Iran war and the Hormuz blockade dominate the cost-of-living debate. - Confidence in the count: AP-NORC finds only 55% of Democrats and 38% of Republicans confident the final midterm count will be accurate.

4. AI bubble shows cracks as Intel suffers record collapse

Last week’s earnings season produced the sharpest test yet of the AI investment boom. Intel beat on third-quarter earnings per share but guided fourth-quarter results far below estimates, citing an explosion at its fab in Israel. The stock fell about 29% on Friday, its worst day on record, wiping out about $140 billion. Alphabet fell 13% on Thursday, erasing about $500 billion in market value, after Google Services revenue missed estimates ($96.3 billion against $98.3 billion expected) and search growth slowed to 12%. Meta reported its first negative free cash flow since 2012. Amazon’s AWS revenue rose 39% to $46 billion, but quarterly capex reached $75.9 billion and fourth-quarter sales guidance missed consensus. Nick Beams wrote on the WSWS that the hyperscalers’ spending, which continues despite uncertain returns, is a structural compulsion and a sign of the boom’s fragility.

Key facts: - Capital spending: The four hyperscalers (Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet) spent $207 billion on capex in Q3, up from $113 billion a year earlier. They plan $232 billion more in Q4, for roughly $740 billion in 2026. - Google’s cash position: AI spending topped $50 billion in Q3 and is projected at about $71 billion this quarter. Free cash flow fell to minus $9.1 billion. - Breadth of the selloff: Nvidia fell 4.2% on Friday and the SOXX semiconductor ETF fell more than 8%. In Asia, SoftBank, a major OpenAI investor, is down 48% from its June peak, and Kioxia fell nearly 16% Monday on a cautious outlook. - Software: Twilio, ServiceNow, Roblox and Reddit all guided Q4 below expectations. - Torsten Slok (Apollo): AI has “delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech,” and the story “will be decided in the S&P 493, not the Mag 7.” - Peter Boockvar: “Right now, we’re seeing tremors in the ground.”

5. Bangladesh garment workers strike across 500 factories

The garment strike in Bangladesh reached its third day Monday. Thousands of workers blocked highways and clashed with police in Dhaka’s Mirpur industrial area, and the walkouts spread to Savar, Ashulia, Jirani and Gazipur. Industry representatives said workers left at least 529 factories on Sunday, and about 500 were temporarily shut. Workers demand a monthly minimum of 30,000 taka ($242). The current minimum is 12,500 taka ($101), and the Bangladesh Center for Workers Solidarity says typical pay is about 13,000 taka. Police used tear gas and sound grenades. Home Minister Salahuddin Ahmed ordered law enforcement to “crack down on those involved in violence.”

The upsurge appears to have broken out largely outside union control. Kamrul Hasan of the Ekota Garments Sramik Federation said some labor leaders had gone into hiding and admitted, “We were not prepared for this unrest either.” The government of Prime Minister Tarique Rahman announced a wage board on Sunday and held an emergency cabinet meeting.

Key facts: - Employers’ threat: Mahmud Hasan Khan, president of the Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association (BGMEA), said owners would invoke “no work, no pay” if protests continued through Monday. - Industry: Bangladesh is the world’s second-largest garment exporter after China, in a sector worth about $38 billion. Owners blame energy costs linked to the US-Israel war on Iran and slowing export demand. - Babul Akhter, Bangladesh Garment and Industrial Workers Federation: “They cannot build a business on the backs of underpaid workers.” - Gazipur: Hundreds blocked the Dhaka–Tangail highway north of the capital.

6. Kherson truce ends as Russia launches 433 drones

The six-day localized ceasefire around Russian-occupied Oleshky, the longest truce of the war, expired at 10:59 p.m. Sunday Kyiv time. A Ukrainian official told AFP that Russia rejected Kyiv’s proposal to extend it. The truce was mediated by First Lady Melania Trump with involvement from Pope Leo XIV. It allowed a rare joint Kyiv-Moscow evacuation from Oleshky, where about 2,000 civilians remained when it began. Melania Trump said nearly 800 people were evacuated. Kherson Governor Oleksandr Prokudin warned residents to avoid “red zones” near the Dnipro.

Fighting elsewhere never stopped. Ukraine’s Air Force said Russia launched 433 drones overnight, of which 371 were downed or suppressed. Zelensky said Sunday’s strikes cut power to hundreds of thousands of households in four regions, as Moscow targets the energy grid ahead of winter. On Monday, both sides accused each other of hacking their national air-raid alert apps. Ukraine’s app sent out false warnings.

Key facts: - Vinnytsia: A Russian drone strike on the grounds of a vocational school killed three people, including a 13-year-old girl. - Ukrainian strikes on Russia: Over 100 Ukrainian drones were reported downed over Nizhny Novgorod region, and a fire broke out at the Kstovo oil refinery. Kyiv says it struck a missile-component factory in Rostov region. - Dnipro: A Russian strike on the train station on October 29 killed 15 (per CNN). - Kremlin: Spokesman Dmitry Peskov called the Oleshky operation positive. Moscow nonetheless declined to extend the truce.

Netanyahu claims Israeli election was stolen

Last week’s Israeli election left Netanyahu’s bloc with 53 seats and Gadi Eisenkot’s opposition bloc with 48, so neither can govern alone in the 120-seat Knesset. Israeli forces withdraw from Hebron after 10-day siege

Governor Khaled Dudin announced Monday that Israeli forces had withdrawn from Hebron, ending a siege that began October 23. It started after two Palestinians killed two Israeli soldiers in a shooting attack. The closure cut off a governorate of about 800,000 people.   • Toll: More than 537 families displaced and more than 3,300 Palestinians displaced or affected, especially in Al-Jalda and Ein Deir Baha. 15 homes and structures were demolished, including the attackers’ homes, and at least 317 shops were forced to close.

  • Analysts: Researcher Ibrahim Rabaia called the siege “part of the collective-punishment system pursued by Israel,” testing reactions to “a new pattern.” Mohamed Helles called it “an attempt to harvest electoral points.”

UN Western Sahara mission ends after Russian veto

The UN peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara (MINURSO) ended on November 1 after 35 years, when the Security Council failed to renew its mandate. On Friday, Russia vetoed a US draft that would have endorsed Morocco’s sovereignty claim and wound the mission down next year. A rival Russian resolution to extend the mission was then voted down. UN spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric said November 1 “marks the start of the liquidation period.”

  • Reactions: Morocco’s palace welcomed the end of an “instrument to internationalize the dispute.” The Polisario Front called it “an unacceptable decision.” Algeria condemned the “excessive bias” of the US draft.
  • Background: Morocco annexed the former Spanish colony in 1975, and more than 16,000 people died in the war that followed. The self-determination referendum promised in 1991 never took place. The Polisario left the ceasefire in 2020, and the UN reported low-intensity hostilities last month.
  • Alignment: Washington, Paris and London back Morocco. Algeria, Russia and South Africa back the Polisario. Algeria and Morocco severed relations in 2021, and analysts warn of an arms race.

Iotova leads Bulgarian presidential runoff

Three exit polls in Sunday’s runoff gave incumbent Iliana Iotova 50.7–52.5% against 43.4–44% for Andrey Gyurov, a banker and former deputy central bank governor backed by the pro-Western opposition. Iotova is an ally of Prime Minister Rumen Radev. She opposes military aid to Ukraine and says “peace can come only through negotiations,” while supporting EU and NATO membership. The race was shaped by October drone strikes on ships in the Black Sea off Bulgaria, which left 21 people missing. Gyurov attacked Iotova for not blaming Russia. One voter said he backed “the lesser evil.”

US plans deportation of Afghans through Pakistan

Anonymous US officials told the New York Times that Pakistan will oversee the expulsion of more than 150 Afghans who lack legal residency there, because Washington cannot deport directly to Afghanistan. Binance hack becomes largest digital heist

Binance, the world’s largest crypto exchange by volume, resumed withdrawals in phases on Sunday after the theft of about $6.3 billion in tokens on or around October 29. It is the largest digital heist on record. The FBI attributed it to the North Korea-linked group TraderTraitor. The FBI says the hackers are converting the assets into Ethereum for laundering through decentralized finance platforms, and it urged firms to block the transactions. Binance says it set aside more than $6.34 billion of its own funds and that users will not bear losses. Coinbase and Strategy shares fell. Trump: “I’m not concerned… We don’t know if it’s North Korea.”

Trump administration escalates campaign against voting rights

The administration has tried to insert the federal government into nearly every part of an election process the Constitution leaves to the states. Jonathan Diaz of the Campaign Legal Center says this has created “an atmosphere of fear and uncertainty.” Noncitizen voting is vanishingly rare: the Heritage Foundation’s own database lists 100 cases from 1982 to 2025.

  • Prosecutions: In mid-October the DOJ announced charges against 113 people in 20 states over seven weeks for voting or registration offenses. One defendant, Jose Antonio Banuelos-Diaz, faces five years over an alleged 2007 registration attempt. He says, “I have never voted because I know I can’t vote.”
  • SAVE Act: The bill would require documentary proof of citizenship to vote. More than 21 million Americans, nearly 10% of voting-age adults, lack it, and about 69 million women who changed their names at marriage could face matching problems. It passed the House in February, and allies want to push it through budget reconciliation.
  • SAVE database: The federal citizenship-verification database has scanned 67 million registrations in at least 25 states. The Supreme Court lifted a block on it in September. In Travis County, Texas, between 10% and 21% of flagged people were US nationals.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Bangladesh garment workers defy police and union leaders in wage revolt

The strike over a living wage has shut about 500 factories, and union leaders admit they “were not prepared.” It is one of the largest workers’ revolts in the world this week. It links directly to the Iran war through energy costs and to the global apparel supply chain serving Western brands. The WSWS should cover the government’s crackdown, the BGMEA’s “no work, no pay” threat and the role of the wage board in containing the struggle.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Bangladesh garment factories disrupted as protests continue for third day

2. $11 billion midterms: the oligarchy buys both parties

With Musk alone giving $446 million and record spending on both sides, an article on election-eve campaign finance would give concrete data on the class character of Tuesday’s vote. It would complement Damon’s account of Democratic silence on the Iran strike plan, and could include the SAVE Act and DOJ prosecutions as part of the attack on democratic rights.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Where are the most expensive midterm races and who is funding them?

3. Data centres versus workers: the AI boom drives up utility bills and drains water

The Georgia fight over Prologis’s $17 billion Project Sail is a concrete working-class story about the AI capex race that Beams analyzed. Electricity bills are rising in 13 states, and some residents’ water has turned brown. The story connects the financial bubble to living standards and environmental destruction.

Recommended reading France 24 Midterms 2026: Georgians on the front lines of the people’s war against data centres

4. End of UN Western Sahara mission opens door to new war in North Africa

The lapse of MINURSO after the US–Russia standoff at the Security Council, with Washington, Paris and London backing Morocco’s annexation, raises the danger of war between Morocco and Algeria. It continues the collapse of the postwar UN framework. A WSWS piece could trace the history from Spanish colonialism to the 1975 annexation and today’s great-power maneuvering.

Recommended reading France 24 UN’s peacekeeping mission in disputed Western Sahara ends after 35 years

5. Modi government strikes 133 million from India’s voter rolls

The mass disenfranchisement, which hits Muslims hardest, is one of the largest attacks on voting rights anywhere. The youth-led protests around the Cockroach Janta Party show the same generational radicalization seen in Europe. The WSWS should address it independently of Congress, which is trying to channel the anger behind Rahul Gandhi.

Recommended reading France 24 Indian opposition parties stage mega rally seeking election chief’s resignation

6. Trump deports Afghans who aided US forces into Taliban hands via Pakistan

The plan to expel more than 150 Afghans, including families of US collaborators, through Pakistan exposes the brutality of the mass deportation program. Even the Taliban registered concern. It fits the WSWS’s ongoing coverage of attacks on immigrants.

Recommended reading France 24 Taliban voices ‘serious concern’ over US plan to deport Afghan migrants via Pakistan

7. Israel’s 10-day siege of Hebron: collective punishment as election theater

More than 3,300 Palestinians were displaced or affected, 15 structures demolished and 317 shops closed. Analysts link the siege to Netanyahu’s electoral calculations, and the story ties West Bank repression to the Israeli political crisis and the threats against Iran.
