1. US-Iran war escalates as Iran strikes Kuwait airport and threatens Strait of Hormuz

The war that began February 28, 2026, when US and Israeli airstrikes killed Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, entered a sharp new escalation phase. On June 2–3 Iran attacked Kuwait International Airport with drones, injuring 63 people and killing one Indian national, and struck US bases in Kuwait and Bahrain as well as a vessel near the Strait of Hormuz. The US military responded by using a Hellfire missile to strike a Botswana-flagged oil tanker bound for Iran’s Kharg Island terminal.

Iranian state media tied to the IRGC announced suspension of ceasefire talks. President Trump disputed this on Truth Social, claiming negotiations were proceeding “at a rapid pace,” and told ABC News a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz was reachable “over the next week.” The central sticking point is Iran’s insistence that any ceasefire must also halt Israeli operations in Lebanon and Gaza — a demand Israel refuses.

Key facts:

  • Iran struck US bases in Kuwait and Bahrain and a vessel near the Strait of Hormuz; the Kuwait airport drone strike injured 63 and killed one Indian national.
  • The US destroyed an Iran-bound, Botswana-flagged oil tanker with a Hellfire missile near Kharg Island.
  • Oil prices climbed for a third straight session on the war-risk premium (WTI ~$95, Brent ~$98).
  • The WSWS frames the war as a product of US-Israeli imperialism aimed at remaking the region by force.

Sources CNN Iran-Israel-US war live updates · CBS News Iran war, US strikes and ceasefire talks · NPR Iran, Israel, US, Lebanon and Gaza

2. Russia launches largest aerial assault in months on Ukraine

Russia fired 73 missiles and 656 drones at Ukraine on June 2 in its largest combined aerial attack in months. Ukrainian air defenses intercepted 602 drones and 40 missiles, but 38 sites were still struck. At least 22 civilians were killed and 130–138 wounded. In Dnipro, nine people — including one child — died when a four-story apartment building collapsed; four were killed in Kyiv with 58 or more wounded.

The attack underscores both Russia’s intensifying air campaign and Ukraine’s depleted defenses. May 2026 saw a record 8,150 long-range drones launched, a 24 percent jump over April. President Zelenskyy said bluntly that “the current level of supplies for our air defense does not allow us to shoot down a significant share of the missiles.” The shortfall traces directly to undelivered US-made systems. The WSWS covers the war as a NATO-driven escalation whose costs fall on the working class on both sides.

Key facts:

  • 602 of 656 drones and 40 of 73 missiles were intercepted; 38 sites were hit.
  • Dnipro apartment collapse killed nine, including a child; Kyiv toll was four dead, 58+ wounded.
  • Foreign Minister Sybiga called Putin “a war criminal and loser who has no cards except terror.”

Sources CBS News Russia missile and drone attacks kill several · NPR Russian attack on Ukraine · Kyiv Independent Explosions rock Kyiv amid mass attack warnings

3. US plans new 10–12.5% tariffs on 60 economies via forced-labor probe

The Office of the US Trade Representative announced new tariffs on 60 economies using Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — a fresh legal vehicle after the Supreme Court struck down the earlier IEEPA-based tariff regime. Canada, Mexico, the EU, Taiwan, and the UK face an additional 10 percent tariff for allegedly failing to enforce forced-labor import bans; China, Japan, India, South Korea, Brazil, and Switzerland face 12.5 percent.

Exemptions reveal the priorities: aircraft parts, coffee, beef, and rare-earth minerals used in semiconductors and electric vehicles are spared, while basic consumer goods are hit. USTR Jamieson Greer said the “failure of our most important trading partners to address the importation of goods made with forced labor is unacceptable.” Public hearings begin July 7, with tariffs expected before the temporary 10 percent tariffs expire July 24.

Key facts:

  • Trump tariffs now represent the largest US tax increase as a share of GDP since 1993, averaging roughly $1,500 per household in 2026.
  • The “forced labor” framing functions as a humanitarian cover for a regressive tax borne by working-class consumers.
  • The WSWS analyzes the trade war as a manifestation of intensifying inter-imperialist rivalry.

Sources WSLS/AP US plans extra tariffs after forced-labor probe · Fortune Trade war: new tariff plan

4. EU approves harshest migration crackdown in its history

The EU struck a landmark migration deal on June 2 allowing member states to establish deportation centers outside Europe — modeled on Italy’s detention arrangement with Albania. Maximum legal detention for irregular migrants rises from six months to two years, with unlimited detention for those deemed security threats. Entry bans increase from 5 to 10 years in most cases, with lifetime bans now possible.

Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Denmark, and Greece are already negotiating with African nations to host “return hubs.” The policy is explicitly designed to raise deportation rates: currently only 29 percent of migrants ordered removed from the EU actually leave. Critics from Spain to Romania warned the regulation “will endanger migrants and undermine human rights.” The “return hub” model functions as a colonial-era outsourcing of repression, placing what are effectively European prisons on African soil.

Sources NPR EU migration deal · Euronews EU set to back return hubs · Washington Post Brussels deportation and detention deal

5. Tens of thousands of ultra-Orthodox Jews paralyze Israel over conscription

Tens of thousands of ultra-Orthodox demonstrators blocked major highways and paralyzed public transit across Jerusalem and the Tel Aviv metro area on June 2. Protesters set cars on fire and attacked a soldier disembarking from a bus; police deployed water cannons and mounted units but struggled to control crowds.

Military service is compulsory for most Jewish Israelis, but ultra-Orthodox parties have long maintained draft exemptions for seminary students. Roughly 13,000 ultra-Orthodox men reach conscription age annually and fewer than 10 percent enlist. “Going into the Israeli army means giving up religion … from our perspective it’s a war for our lives,” one protester said. The unrest comes as Israel prosecutes wars simultaneously in Gaza, Lebanon, and following the Iran conflict, intensifying pressure on Netanyahu’s governing coalition, which depends on the ultra-Orthodox parties. The crisis exposes a structural threat to Israel’s capacity to sustain its multi-front war machine.

Sources NPR Ultra-Orthodox protesters block roads and trains · Al Jazeera Ultra-Orthodox protesters clash with police · KPBS Protesters block roads and trains over draft

6. Sudan’s famine deepens as the lean season begins

Sudan’s war — between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces since April 2023 — has produced what the UN calls the world’s worst humanitarian crisis. UNICEF reports 19.5 million people, roughly two in every five Sudanese, face acute food insecurity, with 5 million at emergency-level hunger. Some 135,000 people are in “catastrophic” IPC Phase 5 conditions across 14 hotspots in Darfur and South Kordofan, at immediate risk of famine.

An estimated 825,000 children under five will suffer severe acute malnutrition in 2026 — a 7 percent increase over 2025 and 25 percent above pre-conflict levels. Forty percent of health facilities are non-functional, 17 million lack safe drinking water, and 24 million lack adequate sanitation. The lean season (June–September) is expected to push conditions further toward famine. The crisis is the product of an imperialist proxy war — the RSF backed by the UAE, the SAF supported by Egypt and others — not a natural disaster.

Gaza ceasefire stalled seven months in

A ceasefire declared roughly seven months ago remains on paper only. The IDF is actively building fortifications along the ceasefire line and reinforcing positions, with no further withdrawals since October 10. Israeli air strikes on Gaza have continued throughout the nominal truce. Hamas has rejected the “Board of Peace” disarmament plan, conditioning any disarmament on an Israeli halt to military operations and full entry of humanitarian aid.

Iran’s foreign minister has stated that mounting Israeli activity in both Lebanon and Gaza constitutes a violation of the broader ceasefire framework with the US — the linkage now driving the collapse of US-Iran talks. The UN Security Council heard on June 2 that humanitarian needs are “soaring” despite the truce, with reconstruction stalled.

Sources Al Jazeera Iran warns Israeli attacks threaten ceasefire talks · UN Press Security Council on Gaza humanitarian needs

Portugal general strike grounds 65% of flights

Portugal’s unions staged their second nationwide strike in six months on June 3, protesting the government’s plan to overhaul labor laws. The cabin-crew union SNPVAC reported more than 340 flight cancellations — roughly 65 percent of all scheduled flights departing or arriving in Portugal. The walkout is part of a broader pattern of labor resistance across southern Europe to austerity-inflected “reforms.” That this is the second such action since late 2025 signals sustained working-class resolve rather than a one-off protest — and a class confrontation, not a routine “labor dispute.”

Sources Bloomberg Portugal strike disrupts flights · AFL-CIO Strike map

Chile’s Kast unveils blueprint for social counterrevolution

Chilean President José Antonio Kast delivered his first State of the Nation address, 82 days into his presidency, laying out a sweeping program of repression and austerity. Citing 378 homicides through May 31, Kast proposed extending arrest windows from 12 to 24 hours, criminalizing masked protesters, and creating a “vandal registry.” His economic program cuts the corporate tax rate from 27 to 23 percent, slashes 435 billion pesos from the National Health Fund and 15 percent from pensions, and streamlines environmental permits on a model drawn from Pinochet-era policy. “When a police officer uses legitimate force, they will have the backing of this President,” Kast declared. The WSWS warns the program is a blueprint for dismantling workers’ rights under cover of a security crisis (see WSWS coverage below).

Primary elections in six states reshape the midterm map

Voters cast ballots in California, Iowa, Montana, New Jersey, New Mexico, and South Dakota on June 3 — the largest primary day of the 2026 midterm cycle. In Iowa’s Republican governor primary, businessman Zach Lahn defeated incumbent Rep. Randy Feenstra despite a late endorsement from Trump, a notable rebuke of the president’s political influence. In California’s governor race under the top-two system, Democrat Xavier Becerra (26%) narrowly trailed Republican Steve Hilton (27%) with roughly half the vote counted.

In the race for Nancy Pelosi’s House seat, Democrat Connie Chan advanced to an all-Democratic November matchup against state Sen. Scott Wiener; Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass advanced to a November runoff. Republicans hold a narrow 218–214 House majority, and the midterms could flip control of Congress.

Democrats enforce curfew at Delaney Hall as Congress readies $72 billion for ICE

Democratic Newark Mayor Ras Baraka imposed a 9 p.m.–6 a.m. curfew around the Delaney Hall ICE detention center, producing 61 arrests on the first night, while detainees inside conducted a two-week hunger and labor strike protesting filthy conditions, medical neglect, rotten food, and lack of access to counsel. Senate Republicans simultaneously proposed roughly $72 billion for ICE and CBP, including over $38 billion for ICE alone. The WSWS notes Democrats strategically separated this funding from broader DHS legislation to enable its passage while staging theatrical opposition — providing “democratic cover” for the bipartisan repression apparatus (see WSWS coverage). In Spokane, three anti-ICE protesters were convicted on federal conspiracy charges and face up to six years.

Houston train derailment spotlights deregulated railroads

A Canadian Pacific Kansas City train derailed on Union Pacific tracks in northwest Houston on the morning of June 3, sending 11 rail cars carrying automobiles off the tracks near Old Katy Road and Hempstead Road. No injuries or hazardous materials were involved. The incident comes days after CPKC workers struck on May 31 over “extensive on-call obligations” and safety concerns. The WSWS situates the derailment within the industry-wide devastation of Precision Scheduled Railroading, which has cut nearly 45,000 jobs (about 30 percent of the workforce) while financing multibillion-dollar stock buybacks (see WSWS coverage).

Science, technology, and public health

  • US measles cases approach 2,000 — 1,983 confirmed across 40 jurisdictions, threatening elimination status; South Carolina leads with 668
  • WHO reports a fatal H5N1 case in a Bangladeshi child; cumulative global death toll spans 25 countries at ~48% fatality
  • JAMA study finds Long COVID affects 1 in 6 — roughly 18 million Americans, double official estimates
  • Trump signs AI executive order rejecting any mandatory model pre-clearance

Measles is the most urgent domestic outbreak. As of May 28, the CDC reported 1,983 confirmed cases across 40 jurisdictions in 2026, 93 percent of them outbreak-associated. South Carolina leads with 668 cases, followed by Utah (425), Texas (180), and Florida (134). The US is on pace to shatter last year’s record — already the highest in three decades — and the CDC warns that summer travel will accelerate spread, putting the nation’s measles-elimination status at risk.

A major study published in JAMA Network Open (DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.14909) sharply revised estimates of Long COVID burden. A Mass General Brigham team used an AI phenotyping algorithm across medical records of roughly 460,000 COVID-19 patients at 58 US hospitals, finding about 16 percent — one in six — developed Long COVID, roughly 18 million Americans and double current surveillance estimates. Eighty-nine percent of those patients developed at least one chronic condition requiring ongoing management.

Roundup:

  • H5N1: The WHO tracked 13 avian and swine influenza cases in humans in Q1 2026, including a previously unreported fatal H5N1 infection in a child from Chattogram, Bangladesh (clade 2.3.2.1a), linked to household poultry. US farm-worker cases continue sporadically with no human-to-human transmission detected; the cumulative global H5N1 death toll since 1997 spans 25 countries at a ~48 percent case-fatality rate.
  • COVID-19: US activity is at its lowest since spring 2021. For the week ending May 23, test positivity was 0.79 percent and hospitalizations ~0.2 per 100,000; wastewater activity is rated “very low.” Only two states show growing transmission; 12 show decline.
  • AI policy: On June 2 Trump signed an executive order directing AI firms to voluntarily submit frontier models for government testing up to 30 days pre-release and establishing an AI cybersecurity clearinghouse — but explicitly barring any mandatory licensing or pre-clearance, in contrast to the EU’s binding AI Act.
  • Federal-state collision: Colorado’s comprehensive AI Act — requiring risk assessments and algorithmic-discrimination safeguards for high-risk systems — takes effect June 30, days after the deregulatory federal order, setting up a governance conflict echoing the GDPR-versus-US-privacy dynamic.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural event of the day is the federal court ruling ordering Trump’s name removed from the Kennedy Center. A judge held that the administration’s addition of the president’s name to the facade was unlawful because only Congress holds the authority to rename the federally chartered institution. The decision is a meaningful check on executive attempts to rebrand national cultural bodies as personal monuments — a tendency characteristic of authoritarian politics — and arrives amid a broader assault on public arts funding.

That assault is most consequential in the NEA standoff. Following the administration’s 2026 budget proposal to eliminate the National Endowment for the Arts, hundreds of previously awarded theater grants were abruptly rescinded, hitting organizations including the Drama League and Classical Theater of Harlem. In a partial counterweight, a separate federal judge permanently barred the cancellation of more than $100 million in humanities grants, ruling the move unconstitutional. The defunding represents the most significant restructuring of federal arts support in decades — a deliberate effort to strip working people of access to culture while channeling resources to militarism.

The WSWS marked the day with Erik Schreiber’s review of “Raphael: Sublime Poetry” at the Metropolitan Museum of Art (through June 28), the first comprehensive US exhibition of Raphael (1483–1546). Tracing his development from apprenticeship under Perugino through his Florentine absorption of Leonardo and Michelangelo to the Vatican frescoes “The School of Athens” and “The Parnassus,” Schreiber argues that Raphael’s achievement reflected the rising Renaissance bourgeoisie’s genuine engagement with scientific and artistic progress — a capacity, he writes, that modern capitalism has “repudiated” in favor of “religious obscurantism, fascism, genocide and war.”

Roundup:

  • Film festivals: The Tribeca Film Festival opened its 2026 edition (June 3–14) in New York, coinciding with the opening of the Sydney Film Festival; San Francisco’s Frameline marks its 50th edition later in June — the start of the summer independent-film circuit.
  • Literature: The Carol Shields Prize for Fiction — at $150,000 one of the world’s richest literary awards, honoring women and non-binary writers — announced its 2026 winner on June 2.
  • Obituary: Hilde Lynn Helphenstein, who ran the influential anonymous Instagram account “Jerry Gogosian” satirizing the art market, died June 1; her account helped democratize art-world criticism.
  • Theater: June Broadway openings are headlined by Billy Porter leading an all-Black cast in a new production of La Cage Aux Folles, timed to Pride Month amid renewed culture-war pressure on queer expression.

Sources WSWS Raphael, the “Prince of Painters,” at the Met · Playbill NEA cuts grants for theaters · Cultured Hilde Helphenstein obituary

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “UAW apparatus shuts down grad student strike at Harvard University: The political issues” by Tom Hall and Mike Ingram — read in full on wsws.org

The Perspective examines the abrupt end of a 40-day strike by some 4,000 Harvard Graduate Students Union (HGSU-UAW) members, arguing the UAW bureaucracy deliberately isolated and strangled the struggle. The strike began April 21; demands included wages of $18–21/hour adjusted to Boston-area cost of living (current pay leaves many grad workers eligible for food assistance), protections against advisor harassment, childcare subsidies, expanded health coverage, protections for international students against ICE, and an end to Harvard’s integration with the “military-intelligence apparatus.” Harvard — sitting atop a $56.9 billion endowment, the largest of any university on Earth — stonewalled. When leadership put a weekend vote to workers, 81 percent voted to end the strike without a contract; the authors argue this reflected exhaustion, not victory. The article places the defeat in a pattern: UC academic workers accepted management’s terms despite a 93.3 percent strike authorization; at Columbia, UAW Region 9A director and DSA member Brandon Mancilla ordered grad workers to “abandon demands for protections against ICE, an end to campus surveillance and divestment from weapons manufacturers.” The political conclusion: workers must “break the stranglehold of the bureaucracy” through independent rank-and-file committees and build “a socialist movement rooted in the working class.”

UAW bureaucracy rushes fourth sellout at Nexteer as American Axle workers walk out — Jerry White

With roughly 1,000 American Axle workers having walked out and Nexteer workers having rejected three straight contracts via an 86 percent strike-authorization vote, UAW leadership is pushing a fourth tentative agreement at Nexteer — scheduling the ratification vote inside the plant under management supervision. About 400 of Nexteer’s 1,700 workers (24 percent) face job elimination if the deal passes, and several bargaining-committee members have already transferred to higher-paying positions, giving them a personal stake in ratification. White argues President Shawn Fain is engineering controlled, isolated strikes to appear militant before the June 15 constitutional convention while preventing the coordinated walkout across Nexteer, American Axle, and Dana that could genuinely threaten the automakers. A worker is quoted: “We should all be out on strike together — Nexteer, American Axle, Dana, everybody.”

“This is capitalism gone awry”: A veteran nurse speaks — Katy Kinner

An interview with Mary Jo Marinelli, a Detroit hospital nurse with 47 years’ experience and mother of a son with severe autism, who underwent six back surgeries from work injuries. She describes the deterioration of care under private equity and hedge-fund ownership: staffing shortages causing medication errors and patient falls, eliminated pensions, unqualified “Wellness Directors” hired instead of licensed nurses, and owners who during COVID resisted testing to avoid losing paying residents. Assisted living costs $6,000–$15,000 monthly, placing care out of reach for working families. Disgusted with both parties, she concludes: “This is capitalism gone awry. It’s just not working for everyday people.”

Zohran Mamdani promised free buses; New Yorkers are getting a fare-enforcement crackdown — Daniel de Vries

NYC Mayor Zohran Mamdani, elected in January 2026 partly on a free-bus pledge, has instead expanded MTA EAGLE inspection teams — tactical-vested officers who board buses to check fares — while piloting mid-route checks and electronic summonses. The OMNY rollout brought enforcement to working-class local routes; glitches that wrongly flag paid fares have not slowed citations. One rider, cited despite paying, said: “When you are promising New Yorkers something, New Yorkers are expecting you to give what you’re promising.” De Vries connects this to Mamdani’s increased police hiring and support for federal immigration enforcement, presenting the DSA mayor’s trajectory as proof of the impossibility of reform through Democratic Party structures.

Anthropic IPO to intensify Wall Street frenzy — Nick Beams

Beams analyzes Anthropic’s announced IPO targeting roughly a $1 trillion NASDAQ valuation — a 15-fold jump from $65 billion in March 2025 — despite the company remaining unprofitable since its 2021 founding. He situates the AI frenzy within a speculative bubble resting on government deficit spending rather than genuine productivity, warning a major financial crisis is structurally inevitable absent a socialist restructuring. One Wall Street Journal analyst warns of capital “oxygen depletion” as SpaceX “consume[s] an absolute ton of capital.”

The balance sheet of Castroism as Trump prepares war on Cuba — Andrea Lobo

With the Trump administration reportedly preparing escalated military and economic war on Cuba, Lobo offers a Trotskyist reckoning with Castroism’s record, arguing the Cuban leadership is now negotiating “surrender terms with the CIA” rather than mobilizing working-class resistance. She traces the failure of petty-bourgeois nationalism to resolve democratic tasks under imperialism without permanent revolution, criticizes Joseph Hansen and Pabloite revisionism for elevating Castro to revolutionary status within the Fourth International, and calls for unconditional defense of Cuba against US aggression alongside a sober evaluation of the government’s capitalist character.

Australian Labor government appeases big business over budget tax changes — Mike Head

Head reports Australia’s Labor government is retreating from minimal budget tax reforms proposed May 12 — changes to capital gains, negative gearing, and investment trusts projected to raise only $3.6 billion over three years despite $75 billion in net capital gains in 2022–23. The wealthiest 10 percent receive over 80 percent of CGT-discount benefits; home ownership among 25–29-year-olds has collapsed from 50 percent in the 1970s to 36 percent in 2021. Workers get about $24 weekly in relief from 2027–28 while the budget cut the NDIS by 300,000 beneficiaries and allocated $53 billion to the military.

The right-wing politics of New Zealand’s Opportunity Party — Tom Peters and John Braddock

Ahead of New Zealand’s November 7 election, the authors dissect the Opportunity Party (TOP) — founded by millionaire economist Gareth Morgan, now polling around 3 percent — as a vehicle for upper-middle-class interests masquerading as pragmatic centrism. Led by former KPMG consultant Qiulae Wong, TOP proposes a 1.75 percent land-value tax and a $370 weekly “Citizen’s Income” while declaring readiness to join either Labour or National. The authors call its claimed neutrality “a fraud,” noting it lacks positions on war, climate, or workers’ rights.

Humboldt University meeting calls for freedom of Bogdan Syrotiuk — Our reporters

About 30 students and workers gathered at Berlin’s Humboldt University on May 28 for an IYSSE meeting demanding the release of 27-year-old Ukrainian socialist Bogdan Syrotiuk, arrested by Ukraine’s SBU in April 2024 on “high treason under martial law” charges for advocating working-class unity against the war — charges two independent expert reports found baseless. His case has been accepted by the European Court of Human Rights, though Ukrainian courts keep extending his detention. Speaker Peter Schwarz argued “Kyiv and Berlin do not want peace — but rather the defeat of Russia, no matter what the cost.” Participants unanimously adopted a resolution demanding his immediate release.

Jacobin / DSA

“Getting Beyond the Politics of Powerlessness” argues the left prioritizes ideological purity over strategic power-building and calls for multiracial working-class organizations willing to wield power. The framing is characteristically soft on the decisive question — independent working-class politics versus “strategic” accommodation with the Democratic Party establishment, which Jacobin consistently treats as the terrain on which power is built. A second piece on “Gaza and the rules-based international order” critiques Western liberal framings of international law but, true to form, stops short of naming US imperialism as a systemic feature rather than a policy failure to be corrected through electoral pressure. The day’s orientation reaffirms Jacobin’s role as a sophisticated funnel redirecting anti-capitalist sentiment into the Democratic Party.

Sources Jacobin Getting beyond the politics of powerlessness

Left Voice

“Neoliberalism’s Demise and the Crumbling Bipartisan Regime” offers a Trotskyist-inflected analysis of the structural crisis of US capitalism and the collapse of bipartisan consensus, framing the moment as a revolutionary opening. A second piece reports the reinstatement of three Brooklyn College adjuncts fired for pro-Palestine activism, consistent with Left Voice’s rank-and-file labor focus. Of the tendencies reviewed, Left Voice’s analysis is the closest to the ICFI’s on the objective crisis — but its Morenoite lineage and recurrent orientation toward broad-front regroupment and electoral interventions diverge from the struggle to build an independent revolutionary party.

Its anti-war work is channeled through ANSWER Coalition popular-front politics that subordinate the independent mobilization of the working class to alliances with bourgeois and liberal forces.

Its history with figures like Kshama Sawant has included entryist tactics that blur the line of class independence, leaving the door open to subordination to the Democratic Party machinery it claims to oppose.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Sudan’s engineered famine as an imperialist proxy war

Nearly 19.5 million Sudanese face acute hunger and 825,000 children under five are projected for severe acute malnutrition in 2026, yet the catastrophe is almost universally framed in corporate media as a natural disaster rather than the product of a deliberate proxy war — the RSF backed by the UAE, the SAF by Egypt and others. The WSWS should analyze the engineered destruction of agriculture, water, and health systems and the role of regional and Western powers in marginalizing the crisis.

Recommended reading Stimson Center How Sudan became marginalized in the global system

2. EU’s offshore detention network as colonial outsourcing of repression

The EU’s June 2 migration deal makes the Italy-Albania “return hub” model continental policy, with five governments negotiating to place what are effectively European prisons on African soil while extending detention to two years. This criminalizes poverty-driven migration that European and US policies helped create, and deserves sustained socialist analysis of who profits and which governments are driving it.

Recommended reading Euronews EU set to back return hubs

3. Portugal’s general strike and the southern European labor offensive

Portugal’s second nationwide strike in six months grounded 65 percent of flights against a government labor-law overhaul — part of a Europe-wide ruling-class drive to erode workers’ rights. The WSWS should examine the specific content of the proposed “reforms,” which sectors are striking, and the political parties backing the overhaul, connecting it to the role of the union bureaucracies in containing the struggle.

Recommended reading Bloomberg Portugal strike disrupts flights

4. Ukraine’s air-defense shortage and NATO’s broken supply promises

Russia launched a record 8,150 drones in May, and the June 2 attack killed civilians precisely because promised US-made air-defense systems have not arrived in sufficient quantities. The WSWS should ask where the equipment is, which defense contracts have been signed with which corporations and at what profit margins, while Ukrainian civilians die in the gap between promise and delivery.

Recommended reading PBS NewsHour Massive Russian attack on Kyiv kills 22

5. Long COVID burden doubles official estimates in landmark AI study

A JAMA Network Open study finds one in six COVID patients — roughly 18 million Americans — developed Long COVID, double current surveillance figures, with 89 percent acquiring at least one chronic condition. As the political establishment declares the pandemic over and dismantles surveillance, the WSWS should connect this mass-disabling event to the bipartisan “let it rip” policy and the gutting of public health.

Recommended reading Mass General Brigham Long COVID detected through AI algorithm

6. Ultra-Orthodox draft revolt and the class fault lines of Israeli wartime society

The conscription protests reflect a real class and cultural divide: a politically powerful, coalition-propping ultra-Orthodox leadership versus poorer, isolated followers, as secular Israelis bear the military burden of simultaneous wars in Gaza, Lebanon, Iran, and the West Bank. This tension is a structural threat to Israel’s capacity to sustain its war machine and warrants analysis beyond the religious framing dominant in the press.

Recommended reading NPR Ultra-Orthodox protesters block roads and trains
