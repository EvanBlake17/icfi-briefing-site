1. Trump vows to declare Strait of Hormuz “a territory of the United States”

Speaking at the David S. Mack Center for Training and Intelligence in Garden City, New York on Friday, President Trump declared that “after we finish defeating Iran, which is being very badly defeated, pretty soon I’ll be declaring the Hormuz Strait a territory of the United States.” He described the naval blockade the US reimposed in July — after the collapse of the June memorandum of understanding that had briefly paused hostilities — by saying, “No ships get through unless we want them to.”

The statement is an open assertion of colonial annexation of an international waterway, made in the 170th day of a war launched with “major combat operations” against Iran on February 28. Asked about gasoline prices, Trump said he would “never apologize” for the war; press secretary Karoline Leavitt said keeping fuel prices low and denying Iran a nuclear weapon “are equally as important to the president.”

Key facts:

  • Cross-source casualty counts for the 2026 Iran war stand at roughly 7,144 to 9,676 killed and about 46,965 injured across all fronts as of August 13. Iranian deaths are put at 3,468 (Foundation of Martyrs) to 3,636 (HRANA), with US and Israeli estimates above 6,000, and 15,000–26,500 injured.
  • Lebanon has recorded more than 4,000 killed, according to its health ministry.
  • Iran’s parliament is reviewing legislation to bar US-, Israeli- and other “hostile country”-flagged shipping from the strait and to levy tolls on all other traffic.
  • Transits of the strait rose to 13 vessels from 9 the previous day — a fraction of normal traffic.
  • A US official confirmed a carrier strike group is en route to relieve the USS Abraham Lincoln, which has been deployed roughly eight to nine months.

2. Magnitude 7.7 earthquake strikes Indonesia’s Flores island

A shallow magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck off Flores island in East Nusa Tenggara province at 5:58am local time Saturday (21:58 GMT Friday), collapsing buildings and triggering a tsunami warning across four provinces. The USGS put the depth at 10 kilometres, with the epicentre about 68 kilometres north-northwest of Ende.

Indonesia’s Meteorology, Climatology and Geophysics Agency (BMKG) issued tsunami warnings for East Nusa Tenggara, West Nusa Tenggara, South Sulawesi and Southeast Sulawesi. Hundreds of residents of Maumere fled to higher ground before the alert was lifted. Early figures from the Maumere Search and Rescue Agency put the toll at two dead and two injured, but communications with outlying districts remain incomplete and the count is expected to rise.

Key facts:

  • Three aftershocks within 30 minutes, measuring M5.9, M5.6 and M6.1.
  • The region has a catastrophic history: the 1992 Flores earthquake and tsunami killed some 2,500 people; the 2021 Flores Sea quake caused widespread damage across the same island chain.
  • The quake is the second major seismic event in the same 24-hour window: a magnitude 4.8 earthquake damaged buildings in Granada province, Spain.

3. Court filings expose DHS “Operation Puppet Master” spying on unions and left groups

Court documents filed Thursday revealed that the Department of Homeland Security ran a sweeping undercover surveillance program in Minnesota against labor unions, left-wing organizations and immigrant-rights groups protesting the ICE “Metro Surge” deportation campaign in Minneapolis and St. Paul. The program, code-named Operation Puppet Master, was justified internally as targeting a network of conspirators aiding “violent opportunists and agitators.”

Investigators secretly recorded conversations, infiltrated encrypted Signal group chats, monitored meetings held in public libraries and churches, and obtained the financial records of organizations never accused of any crime. Among those targeted were the Communications Workers of America, the SEIU, AFL-CIO affiliates, and climate activists with the Sunrise Movement.

Key facts:

  • The operation was conducted under National Security Presidential Memorandum 7, which directs federal agencies to investigate individuals and groups expressing “anti-Christian,” “anti-capitalism” or “anti-American” views.
  • Surveillance began last winter as hundreds of thousands of Minnesotans joined protests against the ICE crackdown, which ended after two fatal shootings and thousands of arrests.
  • Financial records were obtained without the targeted organizations being notified or charged.
  • Jacob Crosse notes on the WSWS that the operation represents the criminalization of opposition to government policy itself, with the state treating trade unions as domestic enemies.

4. Washington Post: CIA ran covert drone strikes, abductions and torture off Ecuador

The Washington Post reported Thursday that the series of unexplained attacks on Ecuadorian fishing boats near the Galápagos Islands this year were carried out under a covert action program run by the CIA — a category of operation authorized by the president and never publicly acknowledged. The program is separate from the Pentagon’s declared boat-bombing campaign against alleged drug traffickers launched in September 2025, in which more than 200 people have been killed.

Survivors of a March attack described being fired on by small quadcopter drones that either dropped munitions or flew directly into their vessels. Crews from two other boats said that after being shipwrecked they were picked up by armed men, hooded, cuffed and tortured aboard a ship, then flown to El Salvador before being returned to Ecuador without explanation or charge. According to the United Nations, one boat disappeared on January 20 with eight crew members, all presumed dead.

Key facts:

  • The strikes constitute extrajudicial killing of civilians of a country with which the US is not at war, conducted by an agency operating under presidential covert-action authority.
  • The rendition of survivors to El Salvador replicates the CIA black-site apparatus of the “war on terror,” now redeployed in the Western Hemisphere.
  • Over 200 killed in US maritime strikes since September 2025 across the declared and covert programs.

5. Three US executions in a single day, a first since 2010

Tennessee, Alabama and Oklahoma each carried out a lethal injection on Thursday, August 13 — the first time in nearly 16 years that three US executions occurred on the same day. The last such day was January 7, 2010, when Louisiana, Ohio and Texas put prisoners to death.

Those executed were Anthony Darrell Hines, 66, in Tennessee; Jeremy Williams, 41, in Alabama; and Carlos Cuesta-Rodriguez, 71, in Oklahoma. Twenty-two people have now been executed in the United States in 2026, following 47 in 2025 — the highest annual total since 2009, with Florida alone accounting for 19 of them.

Key facts:

  • The convergence of three execution dates was not a scheduling accident, Kate Randall argues on the WSWS, but a product of state governments and federal courts clearing the way for accelerated killings.
  • The resurgence follows an extended period of decline: US executions fell below 20 per year for most of the 2010s and 2020s.
  • The escalation runs parallel to the deployment of federal agents and troops in US cities and the surveillance of political organizations.

6. Colombia earthquake toll passes 265 as government invites US military onto its soil

President Abelardo de la Espriella said the confirmed death toll from the magnitude 7.4 earthquake that struck Colombia’s coffee region and Pacific coast on August 10 has risen above 265, with more than 2,700 people reported missing and at least 1,500 buildings damaged. The epicentre was at San José del Palmar, roughly 150 miles west of Bogotá, in one of the country’s poorest and least-developed regions; rescue teams have still not reached parts of it.

De la Espriella — sworn in only three days before the quake — declared a state of emergency, imposed overnight curfews in Cali and Pereira, and deployed more than 1,000 soldiers to Cali citing looting. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced Wednesday that the new government had formally requested joint military operations with US forces on Colombian soil.

Key facts:

  • Curfews ran from 8pm to 6am in the two largest affected cities, restricting movement of the population rather than accelerating aid.
  • The US military request marks a sharp reversal from the previous Colombian government’s posture and follows Washington’s escalating military operations across Latin America and the Caribbean.
  • Andrea Lobo writes on the WSWS that the disaster is being used as the pretext for a militarization that predates it politically.

7. RSF closes on El Obeid as Sudan’s health system collapses

The World Health Organization reported that 37 percent of health facilities in Sudan were out of service in 2026, as heavy fighting erupted Friday between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces in El Obeid, the capital of North Kordofan. Approximately 14 people were killed in an RSF attack on the nearby town of Umm Arda.

The RSF has effectively encircled El Obeid and is threatening a full ground assault. Around 500,000 civilians, including more than 100,000 internally displaced people, are trapped inside the city under drone strikes and shelling, cut off from food, water and fuel. UN experts have warned of a repeat of el-Fasher, where the RSF slaughtered some 6,000 civilians after its siege broke last year.

Key facts:

  • Food prices in besieged areas have risen by up to 300 percent; water prices have doubled.
  • Funding shortfalls have forced the closure of three health facilities in Central Darfur, cutting off some 75,000 displaced people from care.
  • Pockets of IPC Phase 5 famine have persisted in Sudan since August 2024, with no serious international relief effort mounted.

8. Indiana declares statewide emergency after 1-in-1,000-year rainfall

Governor Mike Braun declared a statewide disaster emergency and mobilized the Indiana National Guard on Thursday, roughly 36 hours after eastern Indiana absorbed a rainfall event the National Weather Service classified in the 1-in-1,000-year range. Parts of the state received 6 to nearly 12 inches of rain in a week onto ground already saturated.

By Friday morning the White River had surged past 24 feet, breaking the 23-foot crest recorded during Indiana’s historic 1913 flood. Flash flooding continued across Indiana and Illinois for a sixth consecutive day, with roughly 36 million people under flood alerts across the Midwest. The flooding followed a deadly derecho — an unusually long-lived windstorm — that tore through the region a day earlier.

Key facts:

  • National Guard units were directed to Gary, Munster and Hobart in the state’s industrial northwest, among the poorest urban areas in the Midwest.
  • The event is the latest in a pattern of “once-in-a-millennium” precipitation events now occurring with routine frequency, against a backdrop of federal disaster-response funding cuts.

France’s Constitutional Council strikes down under-15 social media ban

France’s Conseil Constitutionnel on Friday struck down Article 1 of the law banning social media access for children under 15, ruling that it “constitutes an infringement that is neither appropriate, necessary, nor proportionate” to freedom of expression and communication. France had become the first EU member state to legislate a blanket ban only last month.

The council’s central objection was that enforcement would require “every person, even an adult, to prove their age before accessing” social media, without adequate legal safeguards — a mass identification regime. President Macron has tasked Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu with redrafting the legislation.

Putin makes first visit to disputed Kuril Islands; Japan protests

Vladimir Putin on Thursday made his first visit to the Kuril Islands, touring a fish plant, a hospital and a school on Iturup and meeting Sakhalin governor Valery Limarenko. Japan claims the four southernmost islands — which it calls the Northern Territories — seized by the Soviet Union in the final days of the Second World War, a dispute that has prevented the signing of a peace treaty for eight decades.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi called the visit “incompatible with the Japanese position” and “absolutely unacceptable,” saying it had “further hardened Japanese public sentiment toward Russia.” The protest comes as Takaichi’s government pursues the sharpest rearmament drive in postwar Japanese history.

Separately, Poland announced the arrest of a Russian national allegedly preparing to assassinate a Ukrainian American in Warsaw — reported as the first attempted Russian attack of its kind inside a NATO country. In Ukraine itself, July was the deadliest month since the full-scale invasion began, with 437 civilians killed and more than 2,600 injured. Kyiv has proposed a mutual halt to attacks on civilian shipping to reopen Black Sea grain corridors.

Simultaneous blackouts hit four Central Asian states

Power failed simultaneously across parts of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan on Friday afternoon after a “sudden change in the power flow” in the interconnected Central Asian grid, according to Kazakhstan’s Energy Ministry. Almaty, Bishkek, Dushanbe and Khujand all lost power; Uzbekistan reported outages in southern regions near the Tajik border.

Alatau Zharyq Company said the cascade began at 2:38pm when three 500 kV overhead transmission lines operated by national grid operator KEGOC shut down unexpectedly. Supply has since been fully restored. The incident is the second major failure of the shared Soviet-era grid in recent years, exposing decades of deferred infrastructure investment across four post-Soviet republics whose economies are dominated by extractive industries and remittance flows.

Australian whistleblower David McBride released on parole after 27 months

David McBride, the former Australian Defence Force lawyer who provided the ABC with classified material documenting alleged war crimes by Australian special forces in Afghanistan, was released from a Canberra prison on parole Thursday after serving the 27-month non-parole component of a five-year, eight-month sentence handed down in May 2024.

He will serve the remaining three years in the community under supervision and strict conditions. His convictions — for theft and unlawful disclosure of Commonwealth material — stand, leaving intact the legal precedent that a soldier who exposes war crimes may be imprisoned while those who committed them face no charges. McBride disclosed that he is being treated for early-stage lung cancer and requires surgery.

IG Metall signs off on destruction of 7,000 Duisburg steel jobs

At least 7,000 jobs are being destroyed in the Duisburg steel industry under the collective restructuring agreement Thyssenkrupp Steel concluded with IG Metall, which runs to September 30, 2030 and provides for cutting or outsourcing roughly 11,000 jobs company-wide while reducing shipping capacity to 8.7–9 million metric tons. Chilean regulator finds Codelco ignored seismic warnings before El Teniente deaths

A 122-page investigation by Chile’s National Geology and Mining Service (Sernageomin) into the fatal rock burst at Codelco’s El Teniente mine concluded that the deaths resulted not from an unforeseeable natural event but from systematic management failures, ignored warnings, and a deliberate decision by the chain of command to keep workers in ground known to be dangerously unstable. The report was finalized in May and obtained by Chilean media in June.

Codelco — the world’s largest copper producer and a Chilean state enterprise — has since suspended development of the Andes Norte section after six months of technical study identified an emerging seismic phenomenon associated with the greater depth of the project. The suspension pushes the expansion back roughly two years and has deepened concerns about global copper supply.

CEO pay hits record as median pay ratio reaches 198-to-1

Kennedy Center board votes a second time to close the complex — and restore Trump’s name

The Trump-appointed board of the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts voted Thursday for the second time to close the Washington complex’s main campus for two years for a $250 million renovation, three months after a federal judge temporarily blocked an identical decision. The board simultaneously voted to reinscribe the president’s name at the entrance, designating the building “Restored and Renovated by President Donald J. Trump.”

The main building would reopen in summer 2028; the REACH pavilion, a 2019 addition, remains open for limited programming and as a memorial to the assassinated president. The project includes structural repairs, new marble floors, nearly $12 million in “acoustical improvements,” and the temporary removal of the eight-foot bronze bust of Kennedy that stands in the main foyer.

The scale of the political intervention is unusual even by the standards of this administration. Representative Joyce Beatty, an ex-officio board member, sued in December to reverse the renaming and halt the renovation; eight architecture and cultural preservation organizations filed a separate suit in March. Shutting the country’s flagship performing arts institution for two years destroys the livelihoods of hundreds of musicians, stagehands, technicians and box-office workers, and eliminates the programming — the National Symphony Orchestra, the Washington National Opera, touring theatre — that constitutes the institution’s actual social purpose. The renaming converts a national cultural institution into a monument to the sitting president, in the manner of a personalist regime.

“The Brink of War”: Reykjavik 1986 as historical costume drama

Among Friday’s US theatrical releases is The Brink of War, a dramatization of the October 1986 Reykjavik summit between Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev, at which discussions on human rights, the Strategic Defense Initiative and the elimination of nuclear arsenals collapsed over SDI. The subject matter is genuinely significant: Reykjavik was one of the closest approaches to nuclear disarmament in history, and its failure prefigured the Stalinist bureaucracy’s capitulation and the dissolution of the USSR.

Early critical response has been unfavourable — one reviewer called it “a stage play adaptation gone horribly wrong” — and the film reportedly reduces the summit to a chamber drama of personal negotiation. The historical problem is that Gorbachev arrived at Reykjavik not as a peace envoy but as the representative of a privileged bureaucratic caste preparing the restoration of capitalism. A film that treats the summit as a contest of two statesmen’s characters cannot address why the promise of disarmament evaporated — a question with obvious contemporary weight as the nuclear powers rearm and Washington wages open war on Iran.

Also released Friday: The End of Oak Street, written and directed by David Robert Mitchell (It Follows), with Anne Hathaway and Ewan McGregor as a couple in an early-1980s suburb torn out of space and deposited in a landscape of predatory dinosaurs — a genre hybrid whose premise of a middle-class neighbourhood stripped of its securities has evident allegorical potential. The Rivals of Amziah King stars Matthew McConaughey as a rural Oklahoma beekeeper reunited with an estranged foster daughter under threat from competitors.
