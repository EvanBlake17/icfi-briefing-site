1. Bessent vows “toughest sanctions in history” as US moves to collapse Iran’s economy

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced that Washington will impose what he called “the toughest sanctions in history” on Iran, explicitly framing the measures as regime change by economic strangulation. “We have the blockade, and we are going to have the toughest sanctions in history,” Bessent said. “It is going to work in Iran, and we are going to collapse this regime.” He warned other governments they are “either with us or against us” and scheduled a press conference for Monday to detail the measures, which administration officials described as “the greatest coordinated economic isolation in the history of the world.”

The announcement comes on roughly day 175 of the US-Israeli war against Iran. Trump separately threatened “tremendous economic consequences” for any nation doing business with Iran, and in a Fox News interview said of the mediating Gulf state, “If Oman gets in the way, we’ll bomb the shit out of them.”

2. US federal debt passes $40 trillion as Treasury intervenes in bond market

The US national debt crossed $40 trillion this week, months earlier than forecasters had projected, after the 30-year Treasury yield reached 5.3 percent — its highest level since 2007. The Treasury announced it will at least double its purchases of long-dated bonds, to a minimum of $4 billion per operation, beginning September 9 and running through early November. The intervention only briefly slowed the rise in yields.

The WSWS Perspective by Tom Hall argues the episode signals “the approaching limits of a financial order that has rested for decades on the unending expansion of US government debt,” and that the ruling class response — austerity, higher borrowing costs, or currency debasement — amounts to “three different routes for imposing the costs of the crisis on the working class.”

Key facts: - Federal debt has risen from $5.7 trillion in 2000 and from $38 trillion as recently as last October - Interest payments reached $963 billion over the first ten months of the fiscal year — roughly $200 billion more than military spending and slightly more than Medicare - The dollar index hit a three-month low this week while gold rose 4.7 percent; Citigroup analysts warned of returning “dollar debasement” fears, cautioning that suppressing yields carries “the main price to pay” of “a weaker currency” - A Wall Street Journal editorial named Social Security, Medicare and Medicaid as targets for “bigger reforms” beyond last year’s cuts, which already stripped over $1 trillion from Medicaid/CHIP and $187 billion from food stamps - SNAP enrollment has fallen 11 percent, the steepest drop since 1997 (Center on Budget and Policy Priorities); average ACA marketplace premiums rose from $113 to $178 after subsidy expiration - America’s 977 billionaires gained $2.2 trillion over the past year, reaching $9.24 trillion (Americans for Tax Fairness)

3. First deportation flight lands in Haiti after TPS termination; 20 sent to Liberia

The first US deportation flight to Haiti since the Trump administration terminated Temporary Protected Status for more than 300,000 Haitian nationals landed in Cap-Haïtien on Thursday carrying 161 people. The flight originated in Alexandria, Louisiana. It could not land at Port-au-Prince’s international airport, which is considered too dangerous — gangs control roughly 70 percent of the capital and more than 3,100 people were killed in gang violence in the first six months of this year. DHS has signaled twice-weekly flights.

On the same day, the administration deported 20 immigrants to Liberia under a new third-country agreement under which the West African state will accept up to 1,200 people with no ties to the country whatsoever. The first group arrived at Roberts International Airport outside Monrovia.

Key facts: - This was the eighth deportation flight to Haiti in 2026 but the first since TPS lapsed - The UN has warned Haiti cannot safely absorb mass returns - The WSWS notes the near-total silence of Democratic Party figures, citing Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Bernie Sanders, and points to New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s firing of Haitian city workers - Separately, ICE detained Oswaldo Pirela, a Venezuelan asylum seeker and San Diego Padres minor league coach with valid work authorization, at an El Paso airport

4. Congo Ebola outbreak becomes deadliest in country’s history

The Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo has now killed more people than any previous outbreak in the country’s history, surpassing the 2,299 deaths of the 2018-2020 epidemic. As of August 20, WHO’s Africa office recorded 5,290 confirmed cases and 2,516 deaths — an increase of 81 cases and 40 deaths in a single day. It is the second-deadliest Ebola outbreak on record after the 2014-16 West Africa epidemic, and is progressing faster than any prior outbreak.

The Bundibugyo species has no licensed vaccine or specific treatment; candidate therapeutics remain in testing. Case fatality stands at 47.5 percent overall and reaches 70 percent in areas with weaker response capacity.

Key facts: - Ituri province accounts for 4,447 cases and 1,984 deaths across 28 of 36 health zones; the day’s new cases came from Ituri (56), North Kivu (18) and Haut-Uele (7) - 155 health workers have been infected, 45 of whom have died - The WSWS characterizes the dismantling of public health infrastructure amid militarized spending priorities as “social murder”

5. Yemen slides back toward full-scale war

Houthi forces and the internationally recognized Yemeni government fought their largest battles since the 2022 truce, with clashes now reported across Marib, al-Jawf, Hadramout, Hodeidah, Taiz, al-Dhale and Lahj. Government forces reported carrying out 81 attacks in a single 24-hour period; the Houthis claimed strikes on two targets inside Saudi Arabia, including drone attacks on Saudi infrastructure. The UN has warned that Yemen faces its highest risk of returning to full-scale war since the truce was signed.

Attacks on government military camps in Hadramout and Marib over the past two weeks mark the fiercest escalation since 2022. The collapse of the truce is inseparable from the wider regional war: the Houthis’ Red Sea operations, the US-Israeli campaign against Iran and the effective disruption of the Strait of Hormuz have destroyed whatever framework held the ceasefire together.

6. Disney and ABC sue FCC over retaliatory license review

Disney and ABC have sued the Federal Communications Commission in federal court, alleging that FCC Chairman Brendan Carr is conducting a retaliatory campaign against the network for editorial decisions the Trump administration disliked. In late April the FCC ordered Disney’s eight owned-and-operated television stations to file broadcast license renewals ahead of schedule — a directive that followed Trump’s public pressure campaign over Jimmy Kimmel’s on-air commentary about Melania Trump, for which ABC suspended the host. Carr called the suit “a meritless case” and told CNBC the agency’s focus was on Disney’s diversity practices, denying any First Amendment dimension. The FCC probe also encompasses ABC’s The View.

Key facts: - The complaint alleges the early license review was triggered by “a public-pressure campaign by President Trump” - The suit is the first significant legal pushback from a major broadcaster after months of media capitulation — Paramount, CBS and others having settled or self-censored - Barry Grey on the WSWS frames the case as a fascistic assault on press freedom compounded by billionaire media owners’ willingness to accommodate it

7. Wahab wins California special election despite millions in AIPAC spending

She becomes the first Afghan American member of Congress, filling the remainder of Eric Swalwell’s term. Wahab has described Israel’s conduct in Gaza as genocide.

The result registers the deep unpopularity of the Gaza genocide and the Iran war among the Democratic base — a sentiment the Democratic Party leadership continues to suppress even as it presides over the war itself.

France’s under-15 social media ban takes effect September 1

Beginning September 1, children under 15 in France will be barred from opening social media accounts; from January 1, 2027, platforms must identify and suspend all existing underage accounts. Because there is no way to verify a child’s age without verifying everyone’s, the law effectively imposes universal identity checks — government IDs, biometric scans or behavioral data — on the entire French population’s internet use. Parliament approved the measure by 243-2 in the Senate and 279-81 in the National Assembly on July 21, then stripped out the specific verification mechanism, leaving enforcement to the EU Digital Services Act.

Gary, Indiana blackouts enter second week

US measles cases top 2,777, worst year since elimination

The CDC has confirmed 2,777 measles cases nationwide in 2026 as of August 20 — the highest annual total since the United States declared measles eliminated in 2000. Pennsylvania remains the hotspot with 379 cases for the year, 181 in Lancaster County alone; Ohio added 73 cases this week to reach 142. Some 94 percent of patients are unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status. Kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen from 95.2 percent in 2019-2020 to 92.4 percent in 2025-2026, dropping well below the roughly 95 percent threshold required for herd immunity and leaving approximately 280,000 kindergartners unprotected. The decline is the direct product of the anti-vaccine policies promoted at the highest levels of the federal public health apparatus.

Summer COVID wave builds as hospitalizations climb

CDC ensemble forecasts project roughly 2,400 new COVID-19 hospital admissions for the week ending August 22, up from 1,800 the prior week. National test positivity crossed 3 percent for the week ending August 8 — the first time since March — and is rising in every state, while influenza and RSV activity remain low. The broad-based, early rise indicates a distinct summer wave rather than seasonal drift, and will collide with an autumn respiratory season already complicated by restricted vaccine access.

Newly identified immune organ in skull bone marrow

Researchers at Washington University report in Nature the identification of lymph-node-like structures within skull bone marrow that function as first responders to brain disease. These structures use channels linking the skull to the dura mater to detect brain-derived signals before distant lymph nodes can respond. Disrupting them accelerated glioblastoma growth and shortened survival in mice, while local immune stimulation improved tumor control. Analogous structures were identified in human skull marrow. The finding effectively describes a previously unrecognized immune organ dedicated to the central nervous system, with implications for glioblastoma, Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease.

H5N1 spreads through Australian bird populations

Australia has recorded 290 confirmed H5N1 infections across all six states since late June, killing more than 1,000 greater crested terns — including a mass mortality event of 84 birds in early August — and threatening endangered species. The WSWS reports that the federal government allocated $113 million for biosecurity against a recommended $200 million. In the US, the CDC reported as of August 7 no indicators of unusual human influenza activity; 71 human H5N1 cases have been confirmed in the US since 2024, all in dairy or poultry workers with no person-to-person spread. Twelve human cases were reported outside the US between August 2025 and June 2026 (Bangladesh, Cambodia, India), including three deaths.

EU AI Act enforcement begins as AI agents used in state-linked hacking

The European Commission published compliance guidelines for the EU AI Act’s transparency obligations — labeling, user notices, logging and technical documentation — which took effect August 2, and expanded its AI Office by 38 staff on July 31. The move from legislation to enforcement coincides with the first well-documented case of AI agents deployed in state-linked cyber operations: Israeli cybersecurity firm Dream reported that suspected Chinese state-linked operatives used open-source AI agents to compromise 85 Taiwanese government accounts and exfiltrate more than 2,500 personnel records over four days in July.

Venice opens with a film about Rupert Murdoch

The 83rd Venice International Film Festival, running September 2-12, will open with Danny Boyle’s Ink, a dramatization of Rupert Murdoch’s early career and his acquisition of The Sun, with Guy Pearce as Murdoch and Jack O’Connell as editor Larry Lamb. That the festival should open with a study of the man who did more than almost any other individual to degrade mass journalism into an instrument of reaction is not without significance — though whether Boyle treats Murdoch as a social phenomenon or as a picaresque anti-hero remains to be seen. The competition includes new work from Martin McDonagh, Werner Herzog and Florian Zeller, Casey Affleck’s directorial effort Company, and — most notably — South Korean director Lee Chang-dong’s Possible Love, his first feature since Burning (2018). Lee remains one of the few working filmmakers whose work seriously examines the psychic damage inflicted by South Korean capitalism on ordinary people.

Maggie Gyllenhaal serves as jury president. George Clooney will receive the festival’s first Golden Lion for Lifetime Achievement — a decision that says more about the industry’s need to honor a reliable liberal establishment figure than about any body of work commensurate with the award.

Museums under financial and political siege

The Philadelphia Museum of Art is confronting a roughly $10 million operating deficit as new leadership attempts a turnaround — a crisis emblematic of institutions squeezed between collapsing public subsidy and dependence on donor whim. The broader context is the systematic defunding and political policing of American cultural institutions documented in a recent timeline of federal actions against the arts: the gutting of the NEA and NEH, purges at the Kennedy Center and Smithsonian, and content directives imposed on federally supported exhibitions.

Roundup: - Stolen works by Cézanne, Renoir and Matisse valued at $10 million were recovered in a cardboard box, months after a three-minute museum heist - The Metropolitan Museum of Art has placed on view one of the oldest surviving copies of the Odyssey — a papyrus fragment containing three lines absent from standard modern translations, a reminder that the Homeric text is a living historical document rather than a fixed monument - The Speed Art Museum repatriated 24 artifacts to the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes - Five Philadelphia-area cultural organizations each received $100,000 in unrestricted funding through Etsy and the Center for Craft — private philanthropy filling, minimally, the void left by the destruction of public arts funding - Telfair Museums named Caitlín Doherty as director following a leadership shakeup

Deaths

Ileana Cotrubaș, 87, the Romanian soprano, died August 20. Trained in Bucharest under the Ceaușescu regime and later a fixture at Covent Garden, La Scala and the Vienna State Opera, Cotrubaș was among the great lyric sopranos of the postwar era. Her Violetta, Mimì and Gilda were distinguished not by vocal display but by an unusual dramatic intelligence and an ability to convey the vulnerability of women crushed by social circumstance — precisely the element in Verdi and Puccini that a great deal of contemporary opera production works to obscure.

Michael Wright, 70, the American actor, died August 21. Wright emerged from David Rabe’s Streamers — both on stage and in Robert Altman’s 1983 film — and gave his most memorable performance as Eddie King in The Five Heartbeats (1991), a portrait of talent destroyed by the music industry. He later appeared in HBO’s Oz. His career, marked by long stretches without work, illustrated the narrow range of parts made available to Black actors of his generation.

Also reported: Romanian-born opera figures aside, the Sri Lankan singer Nanda Malini, whose protest songs were banned during periods of state repression, died August 20; Indian film actress Sowcar Janaki, 94, died August 21.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “The US bond market crisis and the coming eruption of class struggle” by Tom Hall — read in full on wsws.org

Beyond the fiscal data reported above, the Perspective makes several analytical points worth flagging for editorial discussion. Hall quotes David North’s report to the SEP’s Ninth National Congress: “the traditional remedies, fiscal expansion and monetary easing, are constrained by the mountain of debt and by the fragility of the dollar itself.” He connects the fiscal breakdown directly to the Iran war, which was launched to seize oil wealth and regional dominance but has become “a military and political debacle” that deepened rather than resolved the economic crisis. He indicts the Democratic Party at every stage — deregulation, bailouts, militarism, austerity — and argues the crisis “demolishes” the DSA’s claim that social progress is achievable without confronting the capitalist oligarchy, citing Trotsky’s Whither France?: governmental “intervention on the foundations of a capitalist régime can be of assistance only in shifting the unprofitable expenditures of the decline from one class to another.” The trade union bureaucracy is identified as an active participant, with UAW President Shawn Fain named for invoking “competitiveness” and “national interests” to discipline workers. Hall concludes that the international working class — some 3.5 billion people operating the most integrated productive apparatus in history — is “being assembled by history… for the world socialist revolution,” and calls for rank-and-file committees in every workplace linked through the IWA-RFC to expropriate the banks and major corporations.

Students and workers in Sri Lanka denounce Ukrainian court’s imprisonment of socialist Bogdan Syrotiuk — Our correspondents

Sri Lankan workers, students and academics have issued statements condemning the 15-year sentence imposed on 27-year-old Ukrainian Trotskyist Bogdan Syrotiuk for his anti-war writings. The statements form part of the international defense campaign led by the ICFI. Participants at Sri Lankan universities drew explicit parallels to the persecution of socialists under the Rajapakse governments and to the use of “national security” pretexts to criminalize opposition to war. The article is useful for the concrete quotations from workers and students identifying Syrotiuk’s imprisonment as a warning of what awaits the working class internationally.

Global outpouring of support for imprisoned Ukrainian socialist Bogdan Syrotiuk — Andre Damon

The petition for Syrotiuk’s release has now reached over 6,300 signatures from 58 countries, with growing participation from international academics and rising social media engagement. Damon contrasts this with what he terms the “guilty silence” of the Western media, which has reported nothing on the case — a silence that is itself political, given the same outlets’ incessant invocation of human rights against Russia. The article is a call to escalate the campaign ahead of appeal proceedings, and should be read alongside the report of the new SBU-linked defense minister: the same apparatus that jailed Syrotiuk now formally commands the war ministry.

How Chile’s “progressive” government and union bureaucracy covered up the El Teniente disaster — By our reporter

An original WSWS investigation into the July 2025 rock burst at Codelco’s El Teniente mine that killed six contract miners. Management had identified the zone as dangerous and concealed the assessment, removing safety warnings from the area. The article documents the two-tier wage structure that made the deaths possible: contract workers earned CLP 1.1-1.7 million per month against CLP 5.99 million for direct Codelco employees — a differential of up to five to one for identical work in identical conditions. The Boric government and the mining union officialdom suppressed the findings of internal reviews to protect production schedules at the state copper company, whose revenues finance the national budget. This is the clearest available illustration of how “progressive” governments in Latin America function as direct enforcers for capital, and of the role of the union apparatus in the industrial killing of workers. Not covered anywhere in the Chilean or international bourgeois press.

Family and coworkers to hold memorial service for James Brasher, third Stellantis worker killed under UAW 2023 sellout contract — Jerry White

James Brasher, 57, died August 1 after falling 25 feet at Stellantis’s Center Line, Michigan distribution facility. He is the third Stellantis worker killed since the ratification of the 2023 UAW contract. White ties the deaths to the facility closures, forced overtime and speed-up that the contract enabled, and to the UAW bureaucracy’s suppression of health and safety grievances. Family and coworkers will hold a memorial service; the article calls for a rank-and-file investigation independent of both management and the union apparatus, on the model of the committee formed after the death of Ronald Adams Sr. at Dundee Engine.

After canceling strike vote, union officials ram through Michigan Medicine nurses’ contract — Kevin Reed

The University of Michigan Professional Nurses Council ratified a three-year agreement covering 7,200 nurses after cancelling a scheduled strike-authorization vote. Wage increases of 4 percent, 4.5 percent and 4.75 percent fail to recover ground lost to inflation, and staffing ratios — the central issue for members — remain unchanged. Michigan Medicine projects a $181.4 million operating margin. Reed documents the sequence by which the leadership demobilized members: cancelling the strike vote removed the only leverage available, after which the contract was presented as the best obtainable. The article calls for the formation of a rank-and-file committee at Michigan Medicine to take contract enforcement out of the hands of officials who function as an arm of hospital management.

Australian government’s phony posturing over Israeli murder of aid worker — Oscar Grenfell

Following the IDF’s self-exoneration over the April 2024 drone strike that killed seven World Central Kitchen workers, including Australian citizen Zomi Frankcom, Grenfell argues that Canberra’s expressions of concern are cynical cover. Australia conducted its own whitewash through the Binskin inquiry, which accepted Israeli findings without independent investigation, and has continued military and diplomatic support for Israel throughout the genocide — including the export of F-35 components. The Labor government’s posture of “concern” is designed to manage domestic anger while changing nothing.

Workers Struggles: Asia, Australia and the Pacific

The regular labor digest reports: protests by app-based drivers and garment workers in India; a work-to-rule action by Sri Lankan veterinary officers; the first strike by Victorian public hospital doctors in 20 years, demanding a 30 percent pay rise; rejection by more than 90 percent of an Arnott’s biscuit factory pay offer; and disputes at Brisbane ferries and Pacific National rail. The pattern across the region is the same: rank-and-file militancy running well ahead of union leaderships that are working to contain it.

Pseudo-left press review

  • Jacobin / DSA — a day devoted almost entirely to messaging, municipal electoralism and philosophy, with no coverage of the Iran sanctions escalation or the bond market crisis
  • Left Voice — orients to union resolutions and “self-organization” abroad while remaining inside the framework of pressure politics
  • Liberation News / PSL — anti-war and anti-ICE mobilization presented as an end in itself, with the general strike invoked as a protest tactic rather than a political struggle for power
  • Socialist Alternative — promoting “left independent” electoral campaigns as the alternative to the two-party system

Jacobin / DSA

“If Woke 1 Was Crazy, MAGA Woke Is Absolutely Insane” (Branko Marcetic, Aug 21) frames the Trump administration’s censorship drive as the mirror image of “progressive overreach” — a formulation that places state repression and campus identity disputes on the same analytical plane. The framing accepts the reactionary premise that there was a symmetrical “woke” problem requiring correction, disarming readers before an authoritarian offensive that is qualitatively different in character and class content.

“It’s Prime Time for Socialists. How We Make Our Case Matters.” (Ben Burgis, Aug 21) argues that DSA’s task is to refine its rhetoric to broaden appeal. The entire problem of socialism is here reduced to communications strategy — program, party and class independence do not enter the discussion. This is the theoretical expression of a tendency whose practice consists of running candidates on the Democratic Party ballot line.

“Canada’s Socialists Are Coming for City Hall” (James Adair, Aug 21) documents socialist municipal campaigns across Canadian cities, explicitly invoking the Mamdani model. The perspective is that city halls can be captured and used to deliver reforms — advanced in the same week that the mayor held up as the model has been implicated in the firing of Haitian city workers facing deportation.

Assessment: Jacobin published nothing on the Iran sanctions escalation, the $40 trillion debt milestone or the Haiti deportations. The day’s output was inward-facing — messaging, municipal electoralism, and a review essay on utopian thought — which is itself a political statement about where this milieu’s attention lies as the war and social counterrevolution accelerate.

Left Voice

Left Voice’s recent output centers on the cancellation of Temporary Protected Status, which it correctly identifies as placing some 300,000 additional immigrant workers at risk, and highlights the resolution passed by NYC Teamsters Local 804 to “stop deportation of Haitian and Syrian” workers. The article treats the passage of a resolution by a local union body as a strategic breakthrough. Absent is any accounting of why the Teamsters International, which endorsed and courted Trump, has done nothing — and any call for organization independent of the apparatus that produced that endorsement.

“Self Organization in Bolivia Shows How to Fight the Far Right Internationally” presents the Bolivian uprising as a model of “self-organization” against “Trump and the international Far Right.” The category “far right” replaces class analysis: the enemy is a political tendency rather than the capitalist state, and the conclusion is broad anti-right unity rather than the construction of a revolutionary party.

Assessment: Left Voice retains a formal orientation to the working class but consistently substitutes militant-sounding activity within existing structures for the fight to break workers from them.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL’s recent statements on immigration — “Defend our Haitian neighbors! We’ve stopped ICE before and can do it again!” — point to the 100,000-strong Minneapolis mobilization against ICE and to the general strike as proof that “the power of a general strike showed millions of people what is possible.” The general strike is presented as a demonstration of moral force capable of compelling the state to retreat, not as a political confrontation posing the question of which class rules. The PSL’s Iran statements (“Why Trump failed in his criminal war against Iran”) likewise treat the war as the criminal project of a particular administration rather than of American imperialism, leaving open the perspective of pressuring a future Democratic government.

Assessment: Genuine mobilizing energy directed into protest politics with no independent political program, and an orientation to bourgeois nationalist regimes as anti-imperialist actors.

Socialist Alternative

“Support Left Independent Candidates—Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties” promotes Kshama Sawant’s campaign on a platform of ending military aid to Israel, Medicare for All and national rent control. The formulation “left independent candidates” is deliberately elastic: it permits support for any figure positioned to the left of the Democratic leadership without requiring a break with the Democratic Party or a socialist program. Sawant’s own record on Seattle City Council — years of pressuring Democratic majorities — is the model.

Assessment: Electoral pressure politics dressed in the language of independence.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP’s August 20 output includes “Labour’s health secretary has no answers,” criticizing the Starmer government’s NHS policy from the left while advancing no perspective beyond union-led pressure; a piece hailing a “socialist” primary victory in a Republican-dominated US state, which uncritically presents a Democratic Party primary result as a socialist advance; and coverage of Unite union members’ disputes framed entirely around the demands of the existing leadership. A further article responds to the racist right’s seizure on a controversial pamphlet.

Assessment: The SWP continues to combine sharp trade-union agitation with an electoral orientation that treats Democratic and Labour left figures as vehicles for socialist advance.

Communist / RCP (IMT)

“Uncertainty and instability fuel capitalism’s ever-deepening crisis” (Nelson Wan, Aug 21) surveys geopolitical disruption and de-globalization as sources of systemic instability. The analysis is descriptive and fatalistic: crisis is presented as automatically generating revolutionary consciousness, with no examination of the political obstacles — above all the union bureaucracies and the pseudo-left parties themselves. “The Mau Mau Uprising – how the British mutilated Kenya” (Aug 20) is a competent historical account of British colonial atrocities, though it treats national liberation movements uncritically and omits the Stalinist and bourgeois-nationalist leaderships’ role in strangling them.

Assessment: Verbal orthodoxy combined with organizational adaptation; the RCP’s rapid growth rests on presenting communism as a subcultural identity rather than a fight for the political independence of the working class.

Note: None of the tendencies reviewed made any reference to the imprisonment of Bogdan Syrotiuk, now approaching 6,300 signatures on the ICFI’s defense petition. The silence of organizations that claim the mantle of socialism on the jailing of a Trotskyist for anti-war writings is a political fact of the first order, and converges precisely with the silence of the bourgeois press.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Bessent’s sanctions ultimatum and the drive toward confrontation with China

The Treasury Secretary has announced that Washington will use economic warfare to “collapse this regime” in Iran, telling other governments they are “either with us or against us.” Since China purchases more than 80 percent of Iran’s exported oil, the sanctions regime announced for Monday is in substance an ultimatum to Beijing, and marks a qualitative escalation from a regional war toward a confrontation between nuclear powers. The WSWS should analyze the announcement as the economic front of the war and situate it within the disintegration of the dollar order examined in today’s Perspective.

2. Yemen returns to open war as the 2022 truce collapses

Fighting across seven provinces, 81 government attacks in a single day, and Houthi strikes on Saudi territory mark the effective end of the four-year truce. A renewed Yemeni civil war — the outcome of a Saudi-UAE war that killed hundreds of thousands and was armed by both US parties — is being subsumed into the wider regional conflagration. The WSWS has followed Yemen closely; the collapse of the truce warrants a substantial analysis of the class forces involved and the role of US imperialism in reigniting it.

3. Third-country deportations to Liberia: the export of human beings

Twenty people with no connection whatsoever to Liberia have been deported there under an agreement covering up to 1,200 individuals. The practice — paying impoverished states to warehouse deportees — establishes a precedent with no basis in any body of law and converts human beings into an item of foreign trade. This deserves treatment alongside the Haiti flights as a single policy of state violence against immigrant workers.

4. Half a billion dollars: crypto, gambling and AI capital buy the US midterms

Corporate spending on congressional races has already passed $500 million, exceeding the entire 2024 cycle, driven by cryptocurrency, gambling and artificial intelligence firms. Combined with the AIPAC operation in California’s 14th District, the figures document the transformation of the electoral process into a mechanism of direct oligarchic rule. This is essential material for the SEP’s midterm campaign.

5. Tunisia: mass protests against Saied as water and power collapse

For a second month, Tunisians have marched demanding the removal of Kais Saied, with protests triggered by heat-driven water and electricity cuts and sustained by IMF-dictated austerity — frozen public hiring, import restrictions, 5.2 percent inflation. The revival of the 2011 slogan “the people want the fall of the regime” points to the unresolved character of the Arab Spring and the absence of a revolutionary leadership. The WSWS has not covered the Tunisian movement in this cycle.

6. Sudan’s forgotten war: 200,000 newly displaced in Kordofan

The IOM reports more than 200,000 people newly displaced across Kordofan, with RSF drone strikes on el-Obeid’s power infrastructure cutting water and hospital electricity. A war that has killed 59,000 people and displaced 14 million receives almost no sustained coverage in the international press, in part because both belligerents are backed by rival regional powers armed by the United States and its allies.

7. A summer COVID wave meets a dismantled public health system

CDC forecasts project a one-third week-on-week jump in COVID hospitalizations while test positivity climbs in every state — and this is occurring alongside the worst measles year since elimination and a collapse in kindergarten vaccination rates. The WSWS should tie these together as the measurable result of the deliberate destruction of public health infrastructure and the promotion of anti-scientific policy from the top of the federal government.
