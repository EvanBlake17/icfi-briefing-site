1. UN Commission finds Israel is committing genocide in Gaza

A 94-page report by the UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry concluded that Israel has committed genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes against Palestinians, singling out the deliberate targeting of children. The findings arrive as the October 2025 ceasefire has become a fiction on the ground: at least 992 Palestinians have been killed and 3,138 injured since it took effect, against 3,269 documented Israeli violations.

The Commission identified four categories of genocidal conduct: killing with precision weapons under “clear visual confirmation of the target”; causing severe bodily and psychological harm; imposing conditions of life calculated to destroy the population through total siege; and dismantling reproductive and neonatal healthcare to prevent births. Israel refused to cooperate with all 13 of the Commission’s information requests. As Kevin Reed noted on the WSWS, the report provides documentation for prosecutions that the major powers — which continue arming and shielding Israel — will ensure are never enforced.

Key facts: - At least 20,179 Palestinian children killed and 44,143 injured since October 7, 2023 — 27% of all casualties; Gaza now holds the world’s highest concentration of child amputees. - 1.8 million Palestinians — essentially the entire population — remain displaced and dependent on aid. - No hospital is fully operational; 1.1 million children face daily uncertainty over water access (UNICEF). - Israel closed the Zikim Crossing on May 24; Kerem Shalom is the sole cargo entry point, with “dual-use” rules blocking medical supplies. - UN Security Council briefers called Gaza “the most dangerous place on earth to deliver aid.”

2. Trump and Iran clash over nuclear inspections as ceasefire is finalized

President Trump insisted that Iran had agreed to “the highest level Nuclear inspections” in perpetuity, but Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baghaei flatly stated there is “no plan” for IAEA visits to the enrichment sites bombed during the US-Israeli assault. IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi countered Wednesday that inspections are “going to happen,” calling them central to the interim deal. The dispute is the principal obstacle to finalizing the 14-point memorandum of understanding signed June 17.

The MOU includes a 60-day ceasefire extension and requires Iran’s highly enriched uranium stockpile — believed sufficient for up to 10 weapons since Israel’s 2025 strikes — to be “downblended.” Vice President JD Vance said inspectors could return “as soon as this week”; Trump then said there was “no rush.” Iran’s top negotiator separately announced a deal to release $12 billion in frozen funds following talks in Switzerland, and Washington has eased oil sanctions for 60 days. The WSWS has characterized the entire war as an imperialist offensive whose terms have been dictated by Washington’s strategic defeat at the negotiating table.

3. US Senate passes Iran war powers resolution 50–48

The Senate voted 50–48 on June 23 to direct Trump to withdraw US forces from hostilities with Iran unless Congress formally authorizes military action — the first time a war powers resolution has passed both chambers, the House having approved a parallel measure 215–208 on June 3. The resolution is non-binding and will not be transmitted to the White House for signature.

Four Republicans crossed party lines — Bill Cassidy (LA), Lisa Murkowski (AK), Susan Collins (ME) and Rand Paul (KY) — while Democrat John Fetterman (PA) voted against. A Reuters/Ipsos poll found only 24% of Americans support the conflict.

Key facts: - Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer: “The American people have paid the price for Trump’s historic blunder in Iran. It’ll go down in the history books.” - Sen. James Risch warned the vote would lead Iran to “simply stand up and walk away from negotiations.” - The measure’s symbolic character underscores the bankruptcy of the two-party system as any vehicle of antiwar opposition: a war backed by only a quarter of the public proceeds because neither party will act to stop it.

4. Ebola passes 1,000 cases in the Democratic Republic of Congo

The Democratic Republic of Congo confirmed more than 1,000 Ebola cases on June 22, with 254 deaths since the outbreak — centered in northeastern Ituri province — was declared on May 15. It is now the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record and, in its first month, was the worst on record at that stage. The WHO declared the epidemic a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on May 17.

The outbreak is caused by the rare Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine or specific treatment. As Benjamin Mateus documented on the WSWS, the absence of a vaccine reflects the lack of market incentive to develop one — a “social crime” rooted in the subordination of public health to profit.

Key facts: - Ituri accounts for 954 confirmed cases across 22 health zones; North Kivu has 91 and South Kivu three. - Contact tracing has reached only about 55% of potentially exposed persons, leaving major gaps amid violence and displacement. - Of $910 million pledged internationally, less than 10% has been delivered; the African Union’s $518 million plan is entirely unfunded.

5. UN launches evacuation of 11,000 seafarers stranded at the Strait of Hormuz

The International Maritime Organization announced June 23 a mass evacuation of more than 11,000 seafarers stranded in the Persian Gulf since Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz on February 28. At the peak, over 20,000 mariners were trapped; 14 died during the conflict, IMO Secretary-General Arsenio Dominguez confirmed. Two temporary maritime corridors have been established, and Oman’s navy issued navigation guidance for a phased exit. On June 23, more than 36 vessels transited the strait — the highest single-day count since the war began.

The crews, drawn largely from the Global South, were left for months without pay, safe egress or protection — an indictment of an industry and a state system that treat maritime labor as expendable. The operation involves cooperation among Iran, Oman, the US, regional states and the shipping industry.

6. Ukraine strikes Gazprom’s Moscow refinery as Russia escalates bombing

Ukraine launched a drone strike on Gazprom’s Moscow Refinery, triggering a large explosion and sending black smoke over the Russian capital. In response to stalled battlefield advances, Russia intensified aerial bombardment of Ukrainian cities; at least two people were killed in Kyiv in one of the largest overnight assaults since the invasion began. Fuel distribution in Russian-occupied Crimea was halted amid Ukraine’s ongoing isolation campaign against the peninsula.

The war’s economic toll on Russia is mounting: official inflation stands at 5.6% year-over-year, but Swedish intelligence alleges the true figure may approach 15%, while Ukrainian strikes have reportedly knocked out roughly 40% of Russian oil export infrastructure. US weapons supply under the Trump administration remains a contested issue. The WSWS continues to analyze the conflict as a NATO-Russia proxy war whose costs are borne by workers on both sides.

7. Alan Greenspan, longest-serving modern Fed chair, dies at 100

Alan Greenspan died Monday, June 22, at his home from complications of Parkinson’s disease; his wife, journalist Andrea Mitchell, confirmed his death. He served five terms as Federal Reserve chairman (1987–2006) under Presidents Reagan, Bush Sr., Clinton and George W. Bush, presiding over the longest US economic expansion in history (1991–2001).

Greenspan was lionized as “the Maestro,” but his legacy is one of class war waged through central banking. His relentless deregulation, his cult of the financial markets, and his refusal to rein in the housing and derivatives bubbles created the conditions for the 2007–08 global financial crisis. His career embodied the financialization of American capitalism — the diversion of social wealth upward through asset inflation while wages stagnated.

Sudan: 19.5 million face acute food insecurity as civil war enters fourth year

UNICEF reports that 19.5 million Sudanese — two in five — face crisis-level food insecurity or worse, with 135,000 already in catastrophic conditions, as the civil war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces enters its fourth year. An estimated 825,000 children under five are expected to suffer severe acute malnutrition in 2026, 25% above pre-conflict levels. Twenty areas across Darfur and Kordofan are at imminent famine risk.

Key facts: - 40% of health facilities are no longer functioning; 17 million lack safe drinking water. - The humanitarian response is funded at only 16% of need (UNDP); the Hormuz closure further disrupted aid shipments. - Sudan is the world’s largest displacement crisis, with nearly 10 million internally displaced — yet it receives a fraction of the attention given the Iran war.

ASEAN and Russia hold first leaders’ summit since 2018

The June 17–18 ASEAN-Russia summit in Kazan — the first in-person leaders’ meeting since Singapore in 2018 — convened simultaneously with the G7 in Évian, France, against the backdrop of a global energy crisis triggered by the US assault on Iran. Physical Brent crude reached $141 per barrel, and the global shortfall approached 13 million barrels daily (~13% of world supply), which the IEA director called “more serious than the ones in 1973, 1979 and 2022 together.”

Southeast Asian states moved to lock in Russian crude: the Philippines’ Petron purchased 2.48 million barrels (its first in five years), Indonesia committed to 150 million barrels after a three-hour Putin-Prabowo meeting, and Vietnam and Thailand secured additional flows. As John Malvar detailed on the WSWS, Indonesia constructed an elaborate legal fiction — designating the research institute Lemigas as import operator to shield Pertamina from bond-covenant violations — which the EU promptly countered by sanctioning the Karimun terminal in its 20th Russia sanctions package.

Far-right lawyer Abelardo de la Espriella, nicknamed “The Tiger,” won Colombia’s June 21 presidential runoff, narrowly defeating leftist Iván Cepeda 49.66% to 48.70% — a gap of roughly 250,000 votes on 63.6% turnout — extending the rightward swing across Latin America. In the first major electoral test of Mayor Zohran Mamdani’s movement, all three Democratic Socialists of America congressional candidates he endorsed won their June 23 New York primaries. The biggest upset came in New York’s 13th district, where 32-year-old community organizer Darializa Avila Chevalier narrowly unseated five-term incumbent Adriano Espaillat; state Rep. Claire Valdez won the open race to succeed retiring Rep. Nydia Velázquez in the 7th. The sweep represents the further integration of the DSA into the Democratic Party machine. Far from an independent challenge to the war or to capitalism, the DSA’s project is to channel mass antiwar and anti-inequality sentiment back behind a party of imperialism — providing a “left” cover for the Democrats precisely as their establishment wing collapses in credibility.

Supreme Court issues a wave of reactionary rulings before recess

The Supreme Court released a cluster of decisions near its summer recess that together form a unified corporate-aligned, reactionary agenda. In a 6–3 ruling split along ideological lines, Justice Brett Kavanaugh held that state-owned firms cannot invoke sovereign immunity to block litigation over assets nationalized by Cuba, allowing Exxon Mobil to sue over more than $1 billion in property — gas stations, a refinery, depots — seized in the 1960s, a decision that boosts US financial pressure on Havana. As John Burton details on the WSWS, the court also unanimously upheld the forced sale of a $200,000 home to satisfy a $2,241.93 tax debt (Pung v. Isabella County); blocked Falun Gong practitioners from suing Cisco over surveillance infrastructure allegedly used in their arrest and torture in China (Cisco Systems v. Doe, 6–3); and ruled that prison guards cannot be sued individually under federal religious-protection law after a guard forcibly shaved a Rastafarian inmate’s dreadlocks (Landor v. Louisiana, 6–3). Eleven cases remain pending, including Trump v. Barbara on birthright citizenship.

Science, technology, and public health

  • US measles cases reach 2,104 in 41 states — three deaths, the first since 2015; elimination status faces WHO review in November
  • H5N5 human case published in NEJM — Australia records its first-ever H5N1 detection as bird flu spreads into new territory
  • Oracle cuts 21,000 jobs citing AI — capex simultaneously surged 162% to $55.7 billion for AI infrastructure

Measles. As of June 18, the CDC confirmed 2,104 measles cases across 41 states and 30 active outbreaks, with 92% of cases in unvaccinated or unknown-status individuals. Three deaths have been confirmed — two unvaccinated children in Texas and one adult in New Mexico — the first US measles deaths since 2015. The country is on track to lose the elimination status it has held since 2000, with a formal WHO review expected in November.

Bird flu. The New England Journal of Medicine published a confirmed case of the rare H5N5 strain in a 75-year-old immunocompromised woman. Separately, Australia recorded its first-ever H5N1 detection (in a brown skua in Western Australia), and the WHO confirmed a fatal pediatric H5N1 case from Bangladesh in Q1 data. The spread into a new southern-hemisphere country and a novel human subtype sharpens pandemic-preparedness concerns.

Roundup: - COVID-19: Declining or likely declining in 36 US states; the CDC ensemble forecasts ~870 new hospital admissions for the week ending June 20. UK hospital PCR positivity fell to 0.6% from 0.9%. It is one of the most explicit corporate acknowledgments yet that AI adoption is replacing headcount at scale. - EU sovereign AI: The European Commission named the EUROPA Consortium winner of its Frontier AI Grand Challenge, awarding a 6,000-chip NVIDIA Blackwell cluster to train an open-source model exceeding 400 billion parameters in all 24 official EU languages — its most concrete counterweight to US and Chinese AI dominance.

Arts and culture

The day’s most politically revealing cultural item is HBO’s release of Josh Fox’s documentary The Welcome Table, profiled by Jacobin. Fox — best known for Gasland — argues that the climate crisis cannot be separated from empire and capitalism, advancing a case for universal freedom of movement for the hundreds of millions who will be displaced this century. The film’s strength lies in connecting ecological catastrophe to the social order that produces it; its weakness, characteristic of the milieu around it, is a tendency to substitute moral appeal for any analysis of the working class as the agency capable of reorganizing production. Still, in a cultural landscape saturated with disaster spectacle that treats climate change as fate, a work insisting on its man-made, profit-driven character deserves serious engagement.

Literature offers a quieter but significant moment in the Canadian launch of Maggie O’Farrell’s new novel Land, among the most anticipated books of 2026. O’Farrell, whose Hamnet — adapted into the TIFF People’s Choice-winning film she co-wrote — demonstrated a rare capacity to render historical grief and the texture of vanished lives, continues a body of work concerned with memory, loss and the persistence of ordinary people beneath the official record. At its best, such historical fiction restores the inner lives that “great man” history erases.

Roundup: - Repertory and restoration: TIFF Lightbox screened a new 4K restoration of James Bidgood’s Pink Narcissus (1971) for its 55th anniversary — a reminder of the value of preserving independent and avant-garde cinema against the streaming era’s disposability — alongside a 20th-anniversary screening of She’s the Man (2006). - The Greenspan obituaries as culture war: Jacobin’s appraisal of the late Fed chair as “a faithful servant of the ruling class” stands against the hagiographic obituaries dominating the mainstream press, underscoring how the framing of a public figure’s death is itself a political battleground. - Cultural deaths of the month: The wider toll of June 2026 included actor Anthony Head (Buffy the Vampire Slayer, Ted Lasso) at 72 and longtime Today film critic Gene Shalit at 100 — figures whose careers spanned the postwar consolidation and subsequent corporate hollowing-out of broadcast culture.

The throughline across these items is the contest over historical memory and the social meaning of art — whether culture serves to illuminate the forces shaping human life or to anaesthetize against them. The genuine artistic achievements worth defending are those that, regardless of their makers’ politics, insist on the reality of the social world.

Ebola passes 1,000 cases in the Congo — Benjamin Mateus

Beyond the case figures noted above, Mateus advances the explicit causal analysis the bourgeois coverage omits. NALC union prepares to deny letter carriers right to contract vote — Erik Schreiber, Tom Hall

The National Association of Letter Carriers is moving to bypass ratification by sending unresolved bargaining issues to binding arbitration after the May 22 expiration of the 2023–2026 National Agreement — effectively stripping members of the right to vote. The precedent is established: in January 2025, carriers rejected the NALC tentative agreement by roughly 70% (63,680 to 26,304), yet arbitration imposed it anyway. This unfolds amid a sweeping USPS restructuring under Postmaster General David Steiner, who has declared the universal service model “unsustainable” and is moving to end six-day delivery, close rural offices, and integrate USPS’s last-mile network with Amazon, DHL and UPS. The article documents a deadly safety crisis — carrier Eugene Gates Jr. disciplined via GPS surveillance (TIAREAP) before dying of heat-related causes, and four deaths over two years at Georgia’s Palmetto processing center — and calls for organization modeled on the 1970 postal wildcat strike, when nearly 200,000 workers defied the strike ban.

Greens deal aids Australian Labor government’s austerity budget — Oscar Grenfell

The Australian Greens struck a deal to pass Labor’s budget, providing political cover for what Grenfell characterizes as “$63.8 billion in cuts to essential social spending,” with particular impact on disability support. The sole Greens win was an eight-week extension of a parliamentary inquiry into disability cuts — purely performative. The article argues the Greens function structurally as a capitalist party maintaining an opposition facade while enabling Labor’s agenda.

Sri Lanka: Thousands of farmers protest over rising costs and low rice prices — W.A. Sunil

Thousands of Sri Lankan farmers protested to demand a guaranteed minimum paddy price of 140 rupees per kilogram, against conditions where private traders buy at only 90 rupees while production costs run roughly 120 rupees — and one acre now costs about 130,000 rupees, more than double recent levels. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake had promised subsidized fertilizer at 10,500 rupees per 50-kg bag, but insufficient supply forces farmers to private dealers charging 17,000–18,500 rupees. Agriculture Minister K.D. Lalkantha dismissed the protests as geographically limited. The article places the JVP/NPP government — which won power by exploiting discontent over the previous crisis — within a pattern of intensifying the very austerity it campaigned against, and calls for nationalizing major plantations, agribusiness and banks under workers’ control.

Ontario’s Tory government unveils plans to massively boost military production — Penny Smith

Ontario Premier Doug Ford announced at the CANSEC trade show a 10-year Ontario Defence Industrial Strategy to position the province as a hub in North American and European military supply chains. The plan spans roughly 300 firms across aerospace, advanced manufacturing, critical minerals and nuclear technology, and projects tripling Ontario’s defence workforce from 13,000 to 43,000 by 2035 while adding $6 billion to GDP — with the Ring of Fire’s critical minerals explicitly targeted for weapons inputs. This aligns with PM Mark Carney’s pledge to raise Canada’s military budget to 5% of GDP by 2035 (~$150 billion annually). The article highlights labor-bureaucracy collaboration: Unifor president Lana Payne sits on Carney’s advisory council championing military production. It characterizes the strategy as “economic conscription” — presenting military jobs as the only path to decent wages after years of cost-of-living crisis and AI-driven unemployment.

Pseudo-left press review

  • Jacobin / DSA — celebrating the New York primary sweep as proof that the road to socialism runs through the Democratic Party
  • Left Voice — correct denunciations of the Latin American right paired with a centrist orientation toward the DSA milieu
  • Liberation News / PSL — anti-imperialist framing of the Iran war that obscures the reactionary character of the Tehran bourgeoisie
  • Socialist Alternative — hailing Labor Notes and union “reform” as the way forward, shielding the bureaucracy from accountability
  • Socialist Worker / SWP (UK) — declaring the MOU a “defeat” for Trump while subordinating Iran’s workers to bourgeois nationalism
  • The Communist / RCP (IMT) — echoing the “humiliation of imperialism” line and building a party around proclamation rather than program

Jacobin / DSA

Fresh off Mamdani’s mayoralty, Jacobin’s “Claire Valdez on Taking the Mamdani Coalition to Congress” promotes the DSA’s congressional candidates as the leading edge of American socialism. The political line is explicit: the path forward runs through Democratic primaries, capturing the party’s apparatus from within. This is the Sanders-DSA strategy in its purest form — the subordination of working-class politics to the oldest party of American imperialism, dressed in socialist phraseology. Jacobin’s “Donald Trump Helped Colombia’s Far Right Win” offers useful reporting on US interference, but treats the defense of bourgeois-democratic electoralism, not independent working-class mobilization, as the answer to the far right. Its appraisal of Greenspan as a “faithful servant of the ruling class” is among its sharper pieces. Overall orientation: an intellectual journal for a layer seeking to refurbish the Democratic Party as a vehicle for “socialism.”

Left Voice

Left Voice’s “Elections in Colombia Signal Another Victory for the Latin American Right” correctly identifies the rightward continental drift and the bankruptcy of the Pink Tide governments that demobilized the working class. Its republication of the Workers Resist statement on the arrest of anti-ICE activists in Minnesota reflects genuine attention to state repression. But the PTS-aligned group’s persistent orientation toward the DSA and “broad left” milieu — seeking to pressure and regroup with forces tied to the Democratic Party rather than building an independent revolutionary party on the program of the Fourth International — expresses its centrist character. Overall orientation: militant in rhetoric, but oriented toward the very pseudo-left formations whose role is to contain the class struggle.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL’s Iran coverage (“47 years of hybrid war against Iran”) provides a serviceable historical account of imperialist subversion — from the 1953 CIA coup against Mossadegh to the sanctions regime strangling Iran today. The defect is consistent with the PSL’s Stalinist lineage: anti-imperialism is conflated with political support for the bourgeois nationalist regime in Tehran, whose suppression of the Iranian working class and youth — including the recent student protests — goes unmentioned. Opposition to imperialist war is correct; the substitution of the Iranian capitalist state for the Iranian proletariat as the agent of liberation is a betrayal of internationalism. Overall orientation: campist anti-imperialism that lines up behind one bourgeois camp against another.

Socialist Alternative

“4,700 Union Members Meet in Chicago to Discuss the Way Forward for Labor” celebrates the Labor Notes conference as evidence of labor’s revival, foregrounding the Federal Unionist Network and Amazon organizing. The political line presents the existing unions, reformed through “rank-and-file” pressure on the bureaucracy, as the vehicle of working-class advance. Absent is any reckoning with the role of the union apparatus in suppressing struggles — precisely the question posed by the BP Whiting lockout and the UAW Nexteer “shotgun vote.” By promoting Labor Notes’ milieu of staffers and reform officials, Socialist Alternative shields the bureaucracy from the rank-and-file rebellion building beneath it. Overall orientation: left cover for the trade-union apparatus.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP declares the 14-point MOU “a defeat for Trump” and frames the war as having threatened to “tip the world economy into disaster,” while reporting on reignited student protests in Iran. Its “Peace talks change nothing, says Lebanese socialist” usefully punctures the diplomatic theater around Lebanon. But the SWP’s triumphalist reading of imperialism’s “defeat” obscures the catastrophe inflicted on the region’s workers and the continued danger of escalation, and its perspective remains tied to building protest pressure rather than the independent political mobilization of the working class. Overall orientation: movementist agitation lacking any strategy to break workers from bourgeois and Stalinist leaderships.

The Communist / RCP (IMT)

RCP political secretary Rob Sewell’s June 22 piece characterizes the MOU as “a humiliation for Trump and a strategic defeat for US imperialism,” while Ben Curry covers the resignations of Labour ministers John Healey and Al Carns as blows to Starmer. The IMT’s recently proclaimed “Revolutionary Communist International” continues to substitute organizational self-proclamation and recruitment drives for the patient construction of a cadre on the basis of the historical struggle against Stalinism and centrism. Its Iran analysis shares the broader pseudo-left tendency to read events as automatic vindications of “the masses” without confronting the crisis of revolutionary leadership. Overall orientation: declamatory optimism untethered from the strategic lessons of the Fourth International.
