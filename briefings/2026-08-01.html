US widens air war on Iran as IRGC strikes Kuwait base

The United States struck dozens of Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps targets across Iran on July 30 and 31, hitting sites on Qeshm and Kish islands and in Fars, Bushehr, Khuzestan and Hormozgan provinces. Three people were killed when a residential building on Qeshm island was hit. On July 31 the IRGC retaliated with a drone attack on the Ahmad Al-Jaber airbase in Kuwait, targeting hangars, satellite communications equipment and warehouses; one worker employed by a Chinese contractor was killed. The war is now in its sixth month, and weeks past the collapse of the June ceasefire.

Trump told a Cabinet meeting the administration would continue escalating: “We’ll be hitting them very hard and, you know, at some point, they’re going to say, ‘We just can’t take it anymore.’” US officials told Axios that strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure — power plants, refineries, bridges — were under active consideration for the coming days, though they cautioned the plans could be called off. The Washington Post reported that the infrastructure campaign has already left Iran’s economy “battered,” with widespread blackouts and shortages of basic goods.

Writing on the WSWS, Jordan Shilton situated the renewed bombardment within a single, integrated global conflict linking the Middle East to Ukraine, arguing that the imperialist powers are proceeding from the premise that “the redivision of the world” can only be settled by force.

Key facts:

  • Targets struck July 30–31: Qeshm island, Kish island, Fars, Bushehr, Khuzestan, Hormozgan provinces
  • Casualties reported: 3 civilians killed on Qeshm; 1 worker killed at Ahmad Al-Jaber airbase, Kuwait; 5 IRGC members killed in joint US–Saudi strikes on Iraq, bodies repatriated to Iran
  • Attacks have now extended to Jordan, Egypt, Iraq and maritime zones in the Red Sea and Caspian Sea; Iran denied involvement in the Egypt incident and warned of “Israeli plots and false-flag operations”
  • Brent crude ended July more than 20 percent higher on the month, its advance driven almost entirely by war risk

Trump announces Hamas disarmament deal amid deep uncertainty

Trump announced on July 30–31 that Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups had agreed to fully disarm in Gaza in exchange for a phased Israeli withdrawal. Under the framework, a “precise disarmament road map” is to be prepared within 14 days, Israeli forces are to pull back in stages as disarmament proceeds, and an International Stabilization Force is to operate alongside a newly constituted Palestinian police force, under the supervision of Trump’s “Board of Peace.”

Neither Hamas nor the Israeli government gave immediate confirmation that they had agreed to the terms as Trump described them. Israel says it will withdraw only after disarmament is verified as genuine and complete; Hamas says disarmament is contingent on full Israeli withdrawal and the start of reconstruction, and that surrender of heavy weapons — a later phase — depends on the creation of a Palestinian state, which the Netanyahu government rejects. The sequencing dispute is precisely the one that collapsed every previous framework.

Key facts:

  • 14-day deadline for a finalized disarmament road map
  • International Stabilization Force plus a new Palestinian police force to assume security functions
  • Hamas conditions heavy-weapons decommissioning on Palestinian statehood; Israel rules it out
  • Trump simultaneously touted the Gaza framework while escalating the air war on Iran, underscoring that the “peace effort” is subordinate to the wider regional offensive

Medicaid work requirements take legal effect

The federal Medicaid work-requirement rule took legal effect on July 31, the most significant restructuring of the program since its creation in 1965. The rule flows from the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act” signed July 4, 2025, which cuts federal Medicaid spending by an estimated $911 billion over ten years. Enrollees aged 19 to 64 in expansion states must document 80 hours per month of work, community service or schooling, or show at least $580 in monthly earnings, or qualify for a narrow exemption. Medical exemptions require proof that a condition “significantly impairs” work capacity — a cancer diagnosis alone does not qualify.

State Medicaid agencies are required to conduct member outreach between June 30 and August 31, with the compliance deadline for most states set at January 1, 2027. Nebraska became the first state to implement the requirements on May 1, 2026. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities has warned that states lack the administrative capacity to process verification at scale, echoing Arkansas’s 2018 pilot, in which more than 18,000 people lost coverage within six months overwhelmingly because of paperwork failures rather than failure to work.

The WSWS Perspective by Kate Randall counterposes the cuts to the FY2027 Pentagon request of $1.5 trillion — a $445 billion single-year increase, the largest inflation-adjusted jump since World War II — and quotes Trump directly: “It’s not possible for us to take care of day care, Medicaid, Medicare, all these individual things. We have to take care of one thing: military protection.”

Key facts:

  • CBO projects 7.5 million additional uninsured Americans by 2034; 2.3 million expected to lose coverage in the first year of work requirements alone
  • OBBBA also cuts $270 billion from student loans and $187 billion from SNAP, with expanded food-aid work requirements projected to remove 2.4 million people monthly
  • A proposed rule would reverse 2024 Social Security disability expansions, cutting roughly $330 a month from nearly 400,000 disabled recipients
  • Randall notes “Medicaid” was absent from major newspaper front pages on July 31 and that no Democratic leader raised the coverage losses on Sunday talk shows

Russia launches 255-drone barrage on Ukraine, hits US-owned drone plant

Russian forces launched 255 Shahed-type attack drones against Ukraine overnight into July 31, along with dozens of ballistic and cruise missiles. At least eight people were killed and more than 50 injured, with damage reported in Kyiv, Lviv and other cities. A Russian ballistic missile destroyed a drone manufacturing facility in Kyiv owned by Terminal Autonomy, a Delaware-registered company — the first time in the war that Moscow is known to have struck a US-registered firm’s production site. No casualties were reported at the plant.

Ukrainian forces struck a warehouse belonging to Wildberries, Russia’s largest retailer, and an oil refinery overnight; Russia’s defence ministry said it had hit a vessel carrying military cargo in the Black Sea. Ukraine’s General Staff logged 207 combat clashes across the front over the day.

Kazakhstan’s President Kassym-Jomart Tokayev publicly called for the war to be “frozen” and for a return to negotiations on the basis of a revised version of the 2022 Istanbul framework — the most explicit such call yet from a Central Asian state closely tied to Moscow.

117 arrested outside London court over Palestine Action support

British police arrested 117 people outside Westminster Magistrates’ Court on July 30, the overwhelming majority for “expressing support for a proscribed organisation.” The rally, organised by Defend Our Juries, was called to picket the opening plea hearing for 25 defendants — the first of thousands charged since Palestine Action was banned as a terrorist organisation a year ago. Membership or support carries up to 14 years’ imprisonment. Protesters held signs and chanted “I oppose genocide, I support Palestine Action” as they were led away; onlookers shouted “Shame!”

The arrests came in the same week that Palestine Action was granted permission to take its challenge to the proscription order to the UK Supreme Court. The prosecution of thousands of people for speech — under counter-terrorism law, in a country with no written constitutional free-speech protection — represents one of the most sweeping peacetime attacks on political expression in modern British history.

Chris Marsden on the WSWS tied the arrests to the wider criminalisation of anti-war opposition, arguing the proscription functions as a template for suppressing domestic dissent as Britain integrates itself into the war drive against Iran and Russia.

Key facts:

  • 117 arrested July 30; 25 defendants entering pleas in the first tranche of prosecutions
  • Up to 14 years’ imprisonment for membership or expressions of support
  • Supreme Court has granted permission for a challenge to the year-old ban
  • Thousands more charges pending nationally

Merz government in open crisis as CDU discusses replacing chancellor

Discontent inside Friedrich Merz’s Christian Democratic Union has mounted for two weeks, and by the end of July private discussion of replacing the chancellor was no longer taboo within the parliamentary group. An RTL/n-tv Trendbarometer poll published in late July found 14 percent of respondents satisfied with Merz’s performance against 85 percent dissatisfied — among the worst ratings recorded for a sitting German chancellor.

The immediate political test comes in September: state elections in Saxony-Anhalt on September 6, followed by Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern two weeks later. The AfD is polling to win or come close to winning in the eastern states. A July cabinet reshuffle intended to stabilise the coalition succeeded mainly in angering Merz’s own allies. The coalition’s July 2 “reform package,” including roughly €10 billion in annual income-tax relief aimed at lower and middle earners, has done nothing to arrest the slide.

Peter Schwarz on the WSWS links the government’s paralysis to the contradiction between the demands of German rearmament — the largest since 1945 — and an economy in its third consecutive year of stagnation, arguing the ruling class is preparing to govern with or through the far right rather than concede to the working class.

Key facts:

  • Three state elections in September: Saxony-Anhalt (Sept. 6), Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern (Sept. 20)
  • €10 billion annual income-tax relief package agreed July 2 has not shifted polling

Venezuela earthquake death toll passes 5,500 as sanctions remain

The confirmed death toll from the twin earthquakes that struck northwestern and central Venezuela on June 24 — a magnitude 7.2 shock followed 30 seconds later by a 7.5 mainshock — has now surpassed 5,500, up from 3,535 reported on July 7. The USGS PAGER system’s modelling has consistently indicated the true figure may exceed 10,000. In La Guaira state, roughly 80 percent of buildings collapsed; damage in Caracas is extensive and reconstruction has barely begun.

Andrea Lobo’s WSWS report argues that the disaster is being compounded by US policy: sanctions and financial restrictions continue to block Venezuela’s access to international credit, reconstruction materials and reinsurance markets, while Washington escalates pressure on the Maduro government. Lobo characterises the arrangement as the continued “looting” of the country under conditions of mass death.

Key facts:

  • Mw 7.2 followed by Mw 7.5 mainshock, 30 seconds apart, June 24, 2026
  • Death toll: 3,535 on July 7; more than 5,500 confirmed by late July
  • La Guaira state: approximately 80 percent of buildings collapsed
  • USGS PAGER modelling has flagged the possibility of a toll above 10,000

Keiko Fujimori inaugurated as Peru’s president

Keiko Fujimori was sworn in on July 28 after winning the June runoff by fewer than 50,000 votes, becoming Peru’s ninth president in a decade. The 51-year-old leader of Fuerza Popular — four times a presidential candidate, and the daughter of Alberto Fujimori, imprisoned for human rights abuses committed during his 1990s dictatorship — has pledged to reduce crime, restore investment and stabilise the country’s chronically unstable political system.

Francis Portocarrero’s WSWS analysis of the inaugural address reads it as a programmatic statement of class war: militarised policing, states of emergency, deregulation of mining and labour, and closer alignment with Washington’s regional offensive, including against Venezuela and Brazil. The narrowness of her victory and the fragmentation of Congress make a governing majority dependent on the same forces that removed her predecessors.

Burnham government links nuclear submarine programme to welfare cuts

Andy Burnham took office as British prime minister on July 20, replacing Keir Starmer, who stepped down after a run of electoral defeats, policy reversals and scandals. Burnham, 56, is the seventh prime minister since the 2016 Brexit referendum, and entered Downing Street promising to “end instability” through a programme of devolution and reindustrialisation branded “Manchesterism.”

Within his first two weeks, Burnham has committed to funding the next generation of nuclear-armed submarines. Robert Stevens reports on the WSWS that the government intends to finance the programme through reductions in welfare spending — the same trade-off being made in Washington and Berlin. The pattern is now uniform across the imperialist centres: rearmament budgets expanding at historic rates, social provision treated as the reservoir from which they are drawn.

Gulf states drawn deeper into the Iran war

Saudi Arabia has moved from tacit support to direct participation, joining US air strikes on IRGC-linked targets inside Iraq — the operation in which five IRGC members were killed. Riyadh simultaneously announced the formation of a multinational Red Sea defence coalition, framed as protecting commercial shipping but functioning as a US-aligned military bloc against Iran and the Houthis.

The geographic spread of the conflict is now unmistakable: incidents have been recorded in Jordan, Egypt, Iraq, Kuwait, the Red Sea and the Caspian. Kuwait’s hosting of the struck Ahmad Al-Jaber base makes clear that the Gulf monarchies’ basing arrangements have converted them into belligerents whose largely migrant workforces bear the immediate physical risk — the sole fatality at Ahmad Al-Jaber was a contract worker.

India’s youth movement forces resignation of education minister

The protest movement initiated by the newly founded Cockroach Janata Party has drawn tens of thousands of students, unemployed youth and young professionals into central Delhi’s tightly controlled government district, chanting “Modi must resign” and “Remove Modi, save the country.” The movement’s trigger was alleged irregularities in the NEET medical entrance examination, but its content is economic: graduate unemployment stood above 15 percent in March 2026, and the belief that education secures mobility has collapsed.

Modi’s government conceded a central demand when Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan resigned. Activist Sonam Wangchuk ended a 26-day hunger strike as organisers vowed to continue. The WSWS covered the movement’s emergence on July 24, warning that the absence of an independent orientation to the working class leaves it exposed to absorption by Congress and the regional bourgeois parties.

144 dead or missing on the Atlantic migration route

The UN refugee agency recorded 144 refugees and migrants dead or missing between July 14 and 18 in a series of maritime incidents off Nouadhibou, Mauritania. Three rescue operations brought 387 survivors ashore. The deadliest vessel had departed from Bufuloto, Gambia, and drifted for nearly 25 days before rescue; only 38 of the roughly 181 people aboard survived.

UNHCR classifies the West Africa–Canary Islands crossing as one of the deadliest in the world: distances range from 800 kilometres from Mauritania to 2,400 kilometres from Guinea, in overloaded and unseaworthy boats with minimal prospect of rescue. Katerina Selin’s WSWS report attributes the toll directly to EU border militarisation and the externalisation of enforcement to Mauritania, Senegal and Morocco, which has pushed departure points ever further south and lengthened the crossings.

Europe braces for a “warmer than normal” August as wildfires spread

European forecasters have warned of a warmer-than-normal August following back-to-back heatwaves, including a record-breaking June that is estimated to have caused thousands of excess deaths. Wildfires are burning across southern France, Spain, Portugal and Greece. In France alone more than 12,000 acres have burned and close to 12,000 people have been evacuated.

The class character of heat mortality is well documented and consistently under-reported: deaths concentrate among outdoor workers, the elderly poor in unairconditioned housing, and residents of dense urban heat islands. No European government has introduced binding heat-exposure limits for construction, agricultural or delivery workers.

Trump’s Section 338 tariffs on Canada take effect August 19

Trump invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — a provision never previously used to impose duties — to levy 50 percent tariffs on roughly $20 billion in annual Canadian imports, including USMCA-compliant goods, effective August 19. Affected products range from wine and dairy to cement and hockey sticks. Prime Minister Mark Carney has said Canada will retaliate. The administration separately announced phased tariffs on imported generic drugs, reaching 100 percent in August 2028 and 200 percent a year later, ostensibly to force onshoring of production — a measure that will raise costs for the same Medicaid population being cut from coverage.

Divided Fed holds rates as three regional presidents dissent for hikes

The FOMC held the federal funds target at 3.50–3.75 percent on July 29 for a fifth consecutive meeting, on a 9–3 vote. Presidents Hammack, Kashkari and Logan dissented in favour of a hike, citing persistent inflation — a hawkish dissent bloc unusual in size. Tariff pass-through is now a central variable in the inflation outlook.

Gordie Howe International Bridge opens as tariff war escalates

The Gordie Howe International Bridge opened to traffic at about 10:30 a.m. on July 27, connecting Detroit and Windsor across the Detroit River after eight years of construction and two decades of political conflict. The six-lane, 1.5-mile span — the largest crossing between the two countries, with twin towers rising 722 feet — was financed by the Canadian government. Tolls are $5.75 for passenger vehicles. The first Dodge Charger across was driven by Gordie Howe, grandson of the hockey player.

Kevin Reed’s WSWS report notes the contradiction: the bridge was built to serve a deeply integrated North American auto supply chain that the White House is now attacking with 50 percent tariffs, while Canadian and American autoworkers are pitted against each other by their respective governments and union apparatuses.

Fourth Los Angeles water main rupture in two weeks

A 36-inch riveted steel water main installed in 1916 ruptured beneath West Hollywood at 3:55 a.m. on July 16, closing Sunset Boulevard for days. LADWP crews drained a 0.7-mile section and removed roughly 195,000 gallons of water from the excavation before cutting out a 25-foot length of failed pipe. A 6-inch main failed at Santa Monica Boulevard and Laurel Avenue on July 23; another break in the 2800 block of South Bronson Avenue in Jefferson Park on July 26 knocked out 37 service connections and three fire hydrants.

Marc Wells’s WSWS report treats the cluster as a symptom of systemic disinvestment: a distribution network with pipe more than a century old serving a metropolitan area where reservoir levels and fire risk are simultaneously at crisis levels.

US measles cases hit a 33-year high

The CDC reported 2,318 confirmed measles cases for 2026 as of July 23, and by July 30 the year’s total had surpassed the 2,289 recorded for all of 2025 — the highest annual count since 1991, with five months of the year remaining. Cases have been reported by 45 jurisdictions, plus 16 among international visitors. There have been 35 new outbreaks this year, and 93 percent of confirmed cases (2,153 of 2,318) are outbreak-associated. Utah has recorded more than 500 cases; Virginia has gone from six cases in 2025 to 176 in 2026. Some 138 people — 6 percent of cases — have been hospitalised; no deaths have been recorded.

The overwhelming majority of those infected are under 20 and either unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status. National MMR coverage among kindergarteners has fallen from 95.2 percent in 2019–20 to 92.5 percent in 2024–25, below the roughly 95 percent threshold required for community immunity. The United States’ measles elimination status, certified in 2000, is now openly in question.

H5N1 spreads to a sixth continent

Highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b is now circulating in wild birds, poultry and an expanding list of mammal species across six continents, with Australia confirming its first-ever detections in June. More than 180 million poultry birds have been affected since the outbreak began, and over 1,000 dairy herds across multiple US states have reported infections — a host jump virologists regard as unprecedented. Mass die-offs have struck South American sea lions, and on the Antarctic coast researchers estimate that close to half the world’s breeding female southern elephant seal population may already have been killed. From 2024 to May 2026, 71 human cases were identified in the United States, all linked to direct animal exposure, with no confirmed person-to-person transmission; CDC continues to assess general population risk as low.

Study: greenhouse warming intensifies El Niño’s grip on the Indian monsoon

Research published July 31 finds that greenhouse warming has already strengthened the influence of El Niño on the Indian summer monsoon in the historical record, and projects further intensification — raising the risk of severe rainfall deficits and consequent food insecurity across South Asia. The finding matters because monsoon variability in India is not a meteorological abstraction: it determines the incomes of hundreds of millions of agricultural labourers and small cultivators, the same social layers whose children are now protesting unemployment in Delhi.

Record heat dome bakes the US West

A record-challenging heat dome settled over the western United States at the end of July, delivering the summer’s highest temperatures so far. Meteorologists warn the heat will accelerate soil desiccation and wildfire risk, and increase evaporative loss from reservoirs. The combined water level of Lake Mead and Lake Powell stands at a historic low.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural news of the day is the WSWS’s obituary for Sam Neill, who died on July 13 in Sydney at the age of 78. Neill had been diagnosed in 2023 with angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma, a rare non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and had announced in April that he was clear of the disease; his death came suddenly.

Richard Phillips’s appreciation places Neill where he belongs — not primarily as Dr. Alan Grant in Jurassic Park, the role by which most obituaries identified him, but as a formative figure in the Australian and New Zealand new wave of the late 1970s and 1980s. Neill’s first major role, in Roger Donaldson’s Sleeping Dogs (1977), was in a film about an apolitical man swept into a fictional New Zealand under martial law — a work made in the immediate aftermath of the 1975 dismissal crisis in Australia and the intensification of state powers across the Antipodes. Gillian Armstrong’s My Brilliant Career (1979) and later Jane Campion’s The Piano (1993) situated him in a cinema attempting to work through questions of colonial settlement, class and sexual repression with genuine seriousness. That this cohort of filmmakers largely dispersed into international commercial production by the 1990s is itself a comment on what happened to national film cultures under the pressures of globalised entertainment capital. Neill’s own range — from Campion and Armstrong to Spielberg franchise work — traces that arc.

That a film about the anti-fascist photographic record of 1937 opens a major festival in 2026 is not incidental.
  • Two documentaries on the Gaza war: Amos Gitai’s The Road to Jericho and Ahmed Hassouna’s Citizen Osama.
  • Shinya Tsukamoto’s Mr. Nelson, Did You Kill People?, drawn from the experiences of an African American veteran of the Vietnam War — a continuation of the anti-war line Tsukamoto pursued in Fires on the Plain.
  • More conventional star vehicles fill the competition, including a Robert Pattinson feature, Primetime, and an Oasis documentary — the festival’s now-routine accommodation to streaming-era publicity requirements.

Elsewhere:

  • Spider-Man: Brand New Day opened wide over the weekend of July 31, the franchise’s latest continuity reset. The recourse to narrative erasure as a commercial mechanism — wiping a character’s accumulated history so the property can be sold again — is the studio system’s answer to creative exhaustion.
  • The touring production of Come From Away, the Tony-winning musical about the Newfoundland town that hosted stranded air passengers after September 11, continues its national run. Its enduring popularity rests on a sentimental humanism deliberately detached from what the September 11 attacks were used to justify — a quarter-century of war whose latest phase is being fought over Iran this week.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “Medicaid work requirements take effect: No money for healthcare, $1.5 trillion for war” by Kate Randall — read in full on wsws.org

Beyond the material integrated above, the Perspective documents the parallel dismantling of public health infrastructure under HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.: roughly 20,000 HHS employees terminated, all 17 members of the CDC’s Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices replaced, $500 million in mRNA research cancelled, and universal hepatitis B birth-dose vaccination discontinued. It records that the OBBBA allocated $150 billion in new military spending and $170 billion for border enforcement, including capacity for 100,000 migrant detention beds and 10,000 additional ICE agents, and that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is pressing for a further $67 billion on top of the $1.5 trillion request, with over $200 billion earmarked for operations against Iran. Randall’s political conclusion: the bipartisan silence is not an oversight but confirmation that both parties are instruments of the financial oligarchy, and that the defence of social programmes requires the independent mobilisation of the working class through rank-and-file committees and, ultimately, the expropriation of oligarchic wealth.

American workers and youth are turning towards socialism — Patrick Martin

Yesterday’s Perspective examines new opinion polling showing a pronounced leftward shift in American attitudes, particularly among workers and young people, in response to war, inflation and the assault on democratic rights. Martin’s argument is that the growth of favourable views of socialism is not a polling curiosity but a measure of accumulated objective experience: a generation that has lived through 2008, the pandemic, and now open dictatorship-building has drawn conclusions the political establishment cannot address. The programmatic point is that this sentiment does not spontaneously produce a revolutionary party — it must be given conscious political form, which is the task the SEP and IYSSE have set themselves. The article is useful for editorial board discussion in that it explicitly counterposes the growth of socialist sentiment to its capture by the Democratic Party’s “democratic socialist” wing.

UAW presidential debate reveals class divide between socialist autoworker Will Lehman and pro-apparatus candidates — Shannon Jones

Jones covers the UAW presidential candidates’ debate, in which Will Lehman stood alone in calling for the abolition of the union bureaucracy’s control over contract ratification and the transfer of power to rank-and-file committees. The other candidates, whatever their factional differences, defended the apparatus’s structures and its partnership with the auto companies and the government. Lehman raised the international unification of autoworkers against a bureaucracy that has enforced tariff nationalism, and pointed to the collapse of real wages under successive Administration Caucus and “reform” leaderships alike. The debate is significant because it takes place under conditions in which the apparatus has openly resorted to intimidation.

New Zealand students condemn death threat against US socialist auto worker Will Lehman — IYSSE (New Zealand)

The IYSSE in New Zealand has issued a statement condemning a death threat reportedly made against Lehman by a union official. The statement situates the threat within a pattern of bureaucratic violence and intimidation against oppositional workers internationally, and calls on students and workers to demand a full investigation and the removal of those responsible. It links the episode to the broader question of the union apparatus’s function as a policing agency within the workplace.

Union bureaucracy, DSA rally behind Troy Jackson after collapse of Graham Platner campaign — Jacob Crosse

Following the collapse of Graham Platner’s campaign, the union officialdom and the Democratic Socialists of America have consolidated behind Troy Jackson in Maine. Crosse argues the swiftness of the transfer demonstrates that what is involved is not a programmatic commitment to any candidate but an institutional commitment to the Democratic Party as such. The DSA’s role, he writes, is to supply a left cover for the apparatus’s integration into the state, recruiting radicalised workers and youth into a machine whose function is to prevent independent political action. The article is a useful companion to Martin’s Perspective on socialist sentiment: it identifies the mechanism by which that sentiment is diverted.

Detroit legal aid strikers call for unity: “We should all be out together” — Lawrence Porter

Striking Detroit legal aid workers, in conflict over pay and benefits, are calling for a common walkout with public defenders in other cities. Porter reports strikers explicitly rejecting the isolation imposed by their own union’s city-by-city bargaining structure. The quotation the article takes as its title — “We should all be out together” — expresses the elementary instinct toward class-wide action that the apparatus exists to suppress. The strike concerns workers who provide legal representation to the poorest layers of the population, and whose own wages have fallen far behind the cost of living in Detroit.

Who died and why: COVID-19, class and mass death in the United States — Benjamin Mateus

Mateus continues his series analysing US COVID-19 mortality by class and social position. The article’s core contribution is methodological: it insists that mortality data be disaggregated not merely by race and age — the categories favoured by official public health — but by occupation, income and housing conditions, which reveal the pandemic as a class event. The series documents how excess mortality concentrated among workers in production, transport, food service and health care who could not withdraw from exposure, and argues that the normalisation of ongoing mass death represents a permanent lowering of the value placed on working-class life. Mateus connects the abandonment of mitigation to the same fiscal logic driving the Medicaid cuts.

The pro-business record of departing Australian union chiefs McManus and O’Neil — Martin Scott

A balance sheet of the tenures of outgoing ACTU secretary Sally McManus and president Michele O’Neil. Scott documents that under their leadership real wages fell substantially, strike levels reached historic lows, and the ACTU functioned as an arm of successive Labor governments — most consequentially through the Secure Jobs, Better Pay framework, which entrenched compulsory arbitration and suppressed industrial action. The conclusion is that the leadership transition changes personnel and not policy, because the bureaucracy’s role is structurally determined by its integration into the corporate and state apparatus.

Australian Labor Party’s national conference hails savage assault on disability services as a “reform” — Owen Howell

The ALP national conference endorsed cuts to the National Disability Insurance Scheme under the language of “reform” and “sustainability.” Howell notes the absence of any organised opposition from the party’s nominal left or from the affiliated unions, and situates the cuts within Labor’s broader budget consolidation to fund AUKUS and military expansion — the Australian expression of the same guns-versus-welfare equation being imposed in Washington, London and Berlin.

Australian pseudo-left seeking to head off rebellion against union bureaucracy by Victorian teachers — Committee for Public Education

Victorian teachers’ anger at their union’s handling of workloads and pay is being channelled by pseudo-left groups into demands for a change of leadership within the existing apparatus. The CFPE argues that such “reform” campaigns function to keep opposition inside the structures that produced the betrayals, and calls for rank-and-file committees independent of the Australian Education Union.

Australia: Significant opposition to union sellout deal at Western Sydney University — A WSU Rank-and-File Committee member

A first-person report on the large “no” vote against the enterprise agreement at Western Sydney University, driven by anger over pay outcomes below inflation and the entrenchment of casualisation. The account documents the union’s conduct of the ballot and the difficulty members faced obtaining the full text of the agreement before voting.

Royal Mail’s DM26 rollout exposes CWU’s partnership with management — Tony Robson

Robson analyses the Communication Workers Union’s collaboration with Royal Mail management on the DM26 restructuring programme, which reorganises delivery routes and working time to the detriment of postal workers. He argues the “partnership” framework agreed by the CWU leadership has made the union an instrument for implementing the company’s plans, and calls for rank-and-file committees to organise the fight independently.

Lula and Morenoite-led union promote arms industry amid US offensive against Brazil — Guilherme Ferreira

Ferreira examines the promotion of domestic arms manufacturing by the Lula government and a union led by the Morenoite pseudo-left, presented as a patriotic response to US economic and political pressure on Brazil. He argues this represents the subordination of Brazilian workers to the rearmament programme of their own bourgeoisie, and that the involvement of a nominally Trotskyist tendency in promoting military production marks a definite class realignment. The article is directly relevant to the assessment of the Morenoite currents internationally, including their US affiliate.

Berlin court declares the city’s transport company must continue an advertising campaign for the far-right portal “Nius” — Andy Niklaus

A Berlin court has ordered the city transport authority BVG to continue running advertising for the far-right online outlet Nius, which the BVG had sought to withdraw. Niklaus treats the ruling as part of the judicial normalisation of the far right in Germany — the same courts that have upheld bans on Palestine solidarity demonstrations compelling a public body to carry far-right propaganda. The article connects the ruling to the broader rehabilitation of extreme-right politics under conditions of rearmament and government crisis.

University of Washington names new Middle East Center director 3 months after political firing of Aria Fani — Diana Green

The University of Washington has appointed a new director of its Middle East Center three months after the politically motivated dismissal of Aria Fani over Palestine-related speech. Green reports that the appointment has been made without any review of the circumstances of Fani’s removal, and situates it within the systematic purge of academics who have opposed the Gaza war — a process now institutionalised across American universities through donor pressure, federal investigations and administrative discipline.

Widespread protests in Bangladesh against poverty wages, mass layoffs — Wimal Perera

Perera reports protests across Bangladesh by garment and other workers against poverty wages and mass layoffs, including highway blockades. The article documents that the minimum wage remains far below subsistence amid double-digit food inflation, and that the interim government has responded with police deployments and arrests. Perera situates the unrest within the wider regional wave of struggles from India to Sri Lanka, and argues for the formation of independent rank-and-file committees linked internationally.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output over July 30–31 was dominated by the consolidation of the Mamdani administration in New York as the tendency’s strategic reference point.

  • “Mamdani-Style Mayors Should Study Peru’s Democracy Experiment” (Jared Abbott and Stephanie McNulty, July 31) proposes Peru’s participatory budgeting experiments as a model for American progressive mayors. The article appears the same week Keiko Fujimori was inaugurated on a programme of militarised austerity — an irony the authors do not confront. Participatory budgeting in Peru functioned historically to legitimise the neoliberal decentralisation imposed after Alberto Fujimori’s dictatorship, allowing municipalities to administer poverty rather than abolish it. Presenting it as a democratic model is a defence of the management of capitalist scarcity at the local level.
  • “Zohran Mamdani’s Public Grocery Plan Is a Serious One” (Errol Schweizer, July 30) argues from within the grocery industry that municipal subsidy can address food-price consolidation. The framing accepts the permanence of the food monopolies and confines socialism to a subsidised retail outlet.
  • “Take a Moment to Feel the Pain of the Rich” (Danny Dorling, July 31) attacks The Economist’s defence of billionaires. The rhetoric is sharp but purely moral: inequality appears as a policy failure to be corrected by taxation, not as a relation of production.
  • “US Mail Carriers Are Poised to Elect New Union Leadership” (Alexandra Bradbury, July 30) reports on reform slates in the 200,000-member NALC. This is standard Labor Notes coverage in which the problem of the union bureaucracy is reduced to which officials hold office.

Assessment: The day’s output presents municipal administration and union office as the terrain of socialist politics. There is no reference to the Iran war, to the Medicaid cuts taking effect, or to the international character of the crisis.

Socialist Worker (SWP, UK)

  • “Saudi Arabia joins US air strikes in Iraq as war escalates” (July 30) is accurate reporting and correctly identifies “a new and dangerous phase.” It does not, however, draw any conclusion about the Labour government’s role in the war.
  • “What Burnham should do to solve the crisis in social care” (July 29) advises the new prime minister on policy to prevent “private equity companies raking it in.” This is the essential political position: the SWP addresses itself to a Labour government committed to funding nuclear submarines through welfare cuts as though it were a potential instrument of reform.

Liberation News / PSL

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Kuwait strike exposes Gulf migrant workers as the war’s front line

The sole fatality in Iran’s retaliatory drone strike on the Ahmad Al-Jaber airbase was a worker employed by a Chinese contractor. The Gulf states’ vast migrant workforces — Bangladeshi, Indian, Nepali, Filipino, Egyptian — staff the logistics, construction and services on which US basing depends, without citizenship, union rights or the ability to leave. Their position in a widening regional war is a question no other publication is posing.

2. Trump’s Section 338 tariffs and the collapse of the postwar trade order

The invocation of a 1930 statute never before used to impose duties — covering USMCA-compliant goods and taking effect August 19 — represents the formal abandonment of the treaty framework governing North American trade. Carney’s pledge of retaliation sets up an escalating conflict between two economies whose auto, aerospace and agricultural sectors are physically integrated. The consequences will fall on workers on both sides of the border.

Recommended reading Wiley President Trump imposes new 50% tariffs on certain Canadian imports

3. Measles reaches a 33-year high as vaccination coverage collapses

With 2026 cases now exceeding the entire 2025 total and five months remaining, and kindergarten MMR coverage down to 92.5 percent nationally, US measles elimination status is in immediate jeopardy. The connection to the dismantling of the CDC’s vaccine advisory apparatus and the termination of 20,000 HHS staff is direct and demonstrable.

Recommended reading Axios These states are hot spots for 2026’s record measles outbreak

4. Heat deaths and the absence of workplace protection in Europe and the US

Simultaneous record heat in the American West and southern Europe, with wildfires forcing 12,000 evacuations in France alone, is producing excess mortality concentrated among outdoor workers and the elderly poor. No major government has enacted enforceable heat-exposure standards for construction, agricultural, warehouse or delivery workers.

Recommended reading Euronews “Warmer-than-normal August” looms: Why is Europe so hot this summer?

5. NALC leadership election and the crisis of the postal unions

Some 200,000 letter carriers are voting for new national leadership amid the ongoing dismemberment of the US Postal Service through automation, contracting-out and de facto privatisation. Jacobin and Labor Notes are presenting the reform slates as the solution; the WSWS is uniquely positioned to explain why a change of officials will not alter the apparatus’s function, and to link postal workers to the DM26 fight at Royal Mail.

Recommended reading Jacobin US mail carriers are poised to elect new union leadership

6. The Fed’s hawkish dissent bloc and the coming attack on wages

Three regional Fed presidents dissented in favour of rate hikes on July 29 — an unusually large hawkish minority — as tariff-driven price increases work through the economy. The policy question being decided is whether the working class will be made to absorb tariff inflation through unemployment. The July payroll report on August 7 will be the next marker.
