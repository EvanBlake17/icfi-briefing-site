1. US-Iran war escalates as Hegseth demands billions for tenth day of strikes

The US-Iran war entered its tenth consecutive night of strikes as Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Joint Chiefs Chairman General Dan Caine testified before the Senate Appropriations Committee, requesting a $67 billion supplemental to fund the escalating conflict. Hegseth put the war’s cost so far at $37.5 billion, while reporting placed the administration’s total ask over time as high as $200 billion. US Central Command said its latest strikes were aimed at degrading Iranian capabilities used against commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz.

Casualties are mounting. Nearly 100 US service members have been assessed with some degree of injury since July 7, and the Pentagon has identified a fourth service member killed in recent days — the latest during the controlled detonation of a downed Iranian drone in northern Iraq. Remains were also located in Jordan, where an earlier Iranian attack left two US troops dead and one missing.

Senators — mostly Democrats — pressed Hegseth on the request’s size and the absence of any strategy or exit. As the WSWS Perspective by Andre Damon stresses, this was not antiwar opposition but a demand to prosecute the war more effectively. Michigan Senator Gary Peters, whom Damon profiles as a 23-year Merrill Lynch/Paine Webber finance executive worth up to $6.3 million and a 2017 co-sponsor of anti-boycott (BDS) legislation, told Hegseth: “You don’t have a strategy. You don’t have a long-term plan to actually win this war. Win the war!… When will you show leadership?” Senator Dick Durbin complained the Iran war was diverting resources from Ukraine: “We’re in a moment in history where we can win the war in Ukraine… but instead we’re putting more money into this exchange with Iran.” Senator Chris Coons affirmed near-total agreement with Hegseth on core war aims, and Senator Patty Murray — while claiming she would not “rubber-stamp” the request and calling Trump’s conduct threats of “war crimes” — pledged Democrats would do “absolutely everything” to fund the military. Not a single senator of either party proposed cutting off funds.

Damon situates the escalation within a broader drive for territorial conquest: former Trump Ukraine envoy Keith Kellogg has publicly called for US ground troops to seize Iranian territory, and the July 15 Wall Street Journal report described plans to take Kharg Island. Internal administration discussion, per the WSWS, extends to deploying ground troops and even using nuclear weapons.

Key facts: - The $67 billion supplemental includes ~$21 billion for fuel and equipment replacement and ~$46 billion for expanded weapons production (rocket motors, guided bombs, hypersonic missiles, counter-drone systems), plus roughly $1 billion for the DC National Guard deployment and $900 million for Caribbean boat strikes. - Hegseth denounced critics: “Shame on you and others who call this a quagmire and a failure!” - The request is a bridge to a proposed $1.5 trillion military budget next year — roughly a 50% increase over this year’s record $1 trillion. - A July 15 Wall Street Journal report described White House plans to seize Kharg Island and other Iranian territory; only Senator Jack Reed asked about a ground invasion, a question Caine refused to answer.

2. Andy Burnham becomes UK prime minister as Starmer resigns

Andy Burnham was invited by King Charles III to form a new government on July 20 after Keir Starmer traveled to Buckingham Palace to tender his resignation, becoming Britain’s seventh prime minister in a decade. Burnham, until recently mayor of Greater Manchester, takes power without a general election, elevated through an internal Labour Party leadership crisis rather than a vote of the electorate.

Starmer led Labour to a landslide two years ago, but his approval ratings collapsed amid a widespread sense that he failed to deliver change, and he was damaged by his appointment (and later firing) of Peter Mandelson — a friend of the late Jeffrey Epstein — as ambassador to Washington. The turnover underscores the acute instability of British bourgeois politics: a government elected with a massive parliamentary majority has disintegrated in barely two years, and its replacement is installed through backroom manoeuvres within the Labour apparatus. Burnham inherits a Britain gripped by austerity, a cost-of-living crisis, the escalating war drive against Russia and Iran, and the funneling of higher-education funding into military-university partnerships — with no popular mandate for any of it.

Key facts: - Burnham is the UK’s seventh PM since the 2016 Brexit referendum. - The transition follows a Labour leadership crisis in which Burnham had earlier been blocked from seeking a return to Parliament. - The change is being closely watched by the pseudo-left; Alex Callinicos of the SWP has already published a piece titled “Burnham will need more than words.”

3. Zelensky fires military chief Syrskyi after mass protests

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky fired General Oleksandr Syrskyi as commander-in-chief of the armed forces following days of mass protests in Kyiv and other cities demanding his removal, naming General Mykhailo Drapatyi, 43, as his replacement. The firing is the culmination of a broader wartime government purge that began the previous Thursday, when Zelensky replaced his prime minister and dismissed Defense Minister Mykhailo Fedorov after just six months.

Demonstrators demanded Fedorov’s reinstatement, viewing him as a driver of military modernization, and denounced Syrskyi as the embodiment of the army’s “Soviet-style” command culture. Drapatyi, 43 — often described as representing a younger generation of commanders forged in the current war — has publicly praised Fedorov and called for a “systematic overhaul” of the army. The turmoil exposes deep fractures within the Ukrainian state and military at a moment when Washington is diverting resources toward Iran and war-weariness is mounting after more than four years of catastrophic losses. That mass protests could erupt in wartime Kyiv, and force the removal of the top general within days, testifies to the growing social and political crisis behind the front lines — even as the demonstrations are being channeled by pro-NATO factions favoring more aggressive “modernization” of the war effort.

Key facts: - The crisis erupted amid the largest wartime protests Ukraine has seen, spreading beyond Kyiv nationwide. - Zelensky’s overhaul removed the prime minister, the defense minister and now the top general within days. - Durbin’s Senate complaint that the Iran war is starving Ukraine of air-defense funding (see top story 1) sharpens the backdrop.

4. Trump imposes additional 50% tariffs on Canada

On July 20, President Trump signed proclamations imposing an additional 50% tariff on a wide range of Canadian imports — including autos, alcohol, wine, cheese, cement and hockey sticks — under Section 338 of the Tariff Act. Energy, potash, fish and critical minerals are excluded. The tariffs take effect in 30 days, sharply escalating the trade conflict with one of the United States’ largest trading partners and a nominal NATO ally.

The move continues Trump’s use of tariffs as an instrument of economic warfare against allies and rivals alike, threatening integrated North American supply chains, particularly in autos.

Key facts: - The action was taken under Section 338 of the Tariff Act, a rarely used provision. - Exclusions cover energy, potash, fish and critical minerals — inputs US industry cannot readily replace. - Rising tariffs are cited by market analysts as a factor weighing on equity futures and feeding inflation risk. - The action deepens a trade conflict that has already strained the USMCA framework and threatens tens of thousands of cross-border auto-sector jobs.

6. US measles cases surpass all of 2025 in worst year since 1992

Confirmed US measles cases have surpassed 2025’s full-year total of 2,289, reaching 2,260 as of the CDC’s July 16 update across 44 states and jurisdictions and 34 outbreaks, with the figure continuing to climb. About 92–93% of cases are outbreak-associated and involve unvaccinated or unknown-status individuals. 2026 is now on pace to be the worst US measles year since 1992.

The surge places the country’s WHO measles-elimination status — unbroken since 2000 — at risk ahead of a PAHO review in November. It is a direct consequence of collapsing vaccination rates and the anti-science orientation of the federal public health apparatus.

Key facts: - 2,260+ confirmed cases in 44 jurisdictions; 34 active outbreaks. - ~92–93% of cases in unvaccinated or unknown-status people. - WHO elimination status (held since 2000) faces a PAHO review in November.

LAUSD faces insolvency, thousands of layoffs

On July 2, Los Angeles County Superintendent Debra Duardo issued the Los Angeles Unified School District — the nation’s second-largest — a “Lack of Going Concern” determination, a formal finding that it cannot meet its financial obligations. The district must rewrite its budget by early August under a Fiscal Stabilization Plan projected to cut roughly $3.6 billion over three years through layoffs, furlough days, healthcare cuts and school consolidations. The WSWS reports the plan targets 5,690 jobs amid a long enrollment decline (from 746,831 students in 2002–03 to 392,654 in 2025–26).

As the WSWS notes, the crisis unfolds while California’s 246 billionaires added $487.2 billion in wealth over 12 months, and after union leadership canceled a strike authorized by nearly 70,000 workers in April.

Cyclosporiasis outbreak tied to Taylor Farms lettuce spreads across 27 states

A cyclosporiasis outbreak linked to Taylor Farms iceberg lettuce sourced from central Mexico has spread across 27 states, with the CDC tracing cases to shredded lettuce served at Taco Bell locations. National figures cited in bourgeois coverage put confirmed cases above 1,644 with 94 hospitalizations and no deaths; the WSWS, citing state data, reports far higher totals (6,571 Michigan cases, 102 hospitalizations). Notably, the FDA reviewed a Taylor Farms de Mexico sample that had tested positive and, within 24 hours, declared “no confirmed positive sample results,” effectively withdrawing its own finding.

As Evan Blake writes on the WSWS, the $7 billion company — which has donated $3.8 million to Republican committees — effectively dictates the government’s response, an object lesson in corporate capture of food-safety regulation.

Arts and culture

Danny Boyle’s new film “Ink” was announced as the opening film in competition of the 83rd Venice International Film Festival (September 2–12), its world premiere set for opening night in the Sala Grande. Written by playwright James Graham and starring Jack O’Connell, Guy Pearce and Claire Foy, the bio-drama dramatizes the early days of Rupert Murdoch’s acquisition of The Sun and his creation of “a new kind of news.” The subject matter is significant: it is a portrait of the birth of the modern right-wing tabloid, an institution central to the degradation of public consciousness and the rise of reactionary populism across the English-speaking world. That such a film opens Venice — even as Murdoch’s empire continues to shape politics on both sides of the Atlantic — reflects a belated cultural reckoning with a media form built to manipulate rather than inform. It is Boyle’s first film to screen at Venice.

The festival also announced lifetime-achievement Golden Lions for Ellen Burstyn and George Clooney. Burstyn, whose career runs from The Last Picture Show and The Exorcist to Requiem for a Dream, represents a generation of American screen acting rooted in psychological realism and the social ferment of the 1970s — an artistic seriousness increasingly rare in an industry dominated by franchise product.

Roundup: - Pace Gallery announced it will close its Hong Kong exhibition space this month as the city’s art market faces slowing growth and falling demand — a barometer of the broader contraction of the speculative art economy and the shifting geography of finance capital. - Victoria & Albert Museum: Sarvy Geranpayeh, head of the V&A’s academic programmes, resigned citing “racist” and “culturally insensitive” behaviours. The episode reflects the ongoing turmoil within cultural institutions, where genuine grievances are frequently channeled into identity-based recrimination rather than any confrontation with the class basis of institutional decay and underfunding. - Spain’s World Cup victory drew an estimated two million people into the streets of Madrid to greet the returning squad, who were received by the royals and Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez. Such mass celebrations reflect real popular enthusiasm, but are also actively cultivated by the state as vehicles for national unity amid deep social discontent.

The confluence of these developments — a film exhuming the origins of tabloid manipulation, a gallery retreating from a financial capital, and a state harnessing football to nationalism — underscores the WSWS understanding of culture as a battleground of social forces, not a realm of neutral “content.”

Germany’s armed forces doubles size of homeland security division — Johannes Stern

Germany’s Homeland Security Division, established in April 2025, is expanding from roughly 4,400–6,500 personnel to over 10,000 by 2035 across six regiments and 42 locations. Federal judge bars strike, orders SMART-TD to police CSX workers — Jesse Thomas, Justin Smith

A Florida federal judge issued a TRO blocking a near-unanimous strike authorization by CSX rail workers over a new attendance policy that narrows medical-absence protections — eliminating ER visits as qualifying “hospitalization” and excluding telehealth. Under the century-old Railway Labor Act’s “minor dispute” classification, the court ordered the SMART-TD union itself to discipline any member who strikes “upon pain of fine, suspension or other sanction.” The article’s key political point: the state has formally deputized the union apparatus as a company police force against its own members — a concrete illustration of the integration of the union bureaucracy into corporate management and the state, and of why the WSWS builds rank-and-file committees independent of these structures.

The ruling is a landmark in the state’s suppression of the right to strike, arriving amid a wave of rail-worker anger over punitive attendance regimes imposed since the 2022 federal strike ban. Thomas and Smith connect it to the broader assault on railroaders’ conditions and the complicity of the rail unions, which have repeatedly enforced management demands rather than mobilizing their members.
