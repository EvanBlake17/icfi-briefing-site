1. US and Iran sign 14-point peace deal as Swiss nuclear talks collapse

Trump and the Iranian president signed a 14-point memorandum of understanding at the Palace of Versailles on June 17, the first formal peace instrument to emerge from the four-month US-Iran war that began with US-Israeli airstrikes on February 28, 2026. The framework extends the existing ceasefire for 60 days toward a permanent settlement. One day later, on June 18, Vice President JD Vance’s planned June 20 trip to Switzerland for follow-up nuclear talks was postponed; the Swiss foreign ministry confirmed June 19 that the Bürgenstock signing ceremony “will not proceed as planned.” The White House cited “logistical complexities,” while Iran’s delegation pointed to ongoing Israeli military operations in Lebanon.

The collapse of the Swiss leg reintroduced uncertainty into a deal markets had already priced in, sending oil modestly higher after a week of sharp declines. The agreement remains the central fact of the post-war landscape — and a humiliating retreat for a war that killed between 7,468 and 10,018 people across the region.

As Andre Damon noted in the WSWS Perspective today, the deal has provoked “a chorus of bipartisan condemnation framed around military failure, not principled anti-war opposition” — with Democrats attacking Trump from the right for an insufficiently aggressive outcome.

Key facts: - The framework extends the ceasefire 60 days; the US commits to lifting sanctions on “an agreed-upon schedule,” a $300 billion reconstruction fund, and immediate permission for Iranian oil sales. - Iran reaffirms it will not develop nuclear weapons; its 440kg stockpile of 60%-enriched uranium is to be managed under a “mutually-agreed mechanism” with IAEA supervision and likely down-blending — a retreat from earlier US demands for full transfer. - Iran pledges “best efforts” for safe commercial passage through the Strait of Hormuz for 60 days, but its lead negotiator said the strait “will not return to pre-war conditions” and Tehran will “receive a fee for services” — a claim in conflict with international maritime law. - The war’s toll: Iran 3,468–6,000+ killed, Lebanon 3,756 (including ~1,000 Hezbollah fighters), Israel 66, the United States 15 (540+ injured). Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed; Ali Larijani was assassinated March 17. - On Day 1 of the war, a school in Minab was struck, killing 156 civilians including 120 students.

2. Gaza death toll passes 73,000 as Israel pushes territorial control toward 70 percent

The Gaza Health Ministry reported June 18 that 73,018 Palestinians have been killed since October 7, 2023, with more than 173,230 injured. At least 1,007 of those deaths have occurred since the US-brokered ceasefire took effect in October 2025. Despite the ceasefire, Israeli forces now control roughly 64% of Gaza — well beyond the 53% outlined in the original agreement — and Prime Minister Netanyahu has reportedly ordered the military to expand to 70%. Rights groups have raised concerns of ethnic cleansing as territorial control extends systematically beyond ceasefire terms.

On June 18 alone, an Israeli drone struck a vehicle near the Abu Khadra Mosque in Gaza City’s Rimal neighborhood. Al Jazeera correspondent Hind Khoudary reported “locals said the target was a jeep driven by civilians”; one victim, Abdul Jawad Abu Lebn, had wedding invitations in his car. A fisherman, a man near Khan Younis, and a girl in Beit Lahiya were killed in separate incidents the same day.

The continued killing — more than a thousand Palestinians dead in the eight months since the ceasefire — proceeds even as Washington’s diplomatic energies have shifted to the Iran settlement. The incremental expansion of Israeli control, from the agreed 53% to 64% and toward 70%, constitutes a methodical redrawing of Gaza’s map through the destruction of residential infrastructure and the permanent displacement of its population. The WSWS has covered the Gaza war continuously since October 2023 as a genocide enabled by both US parties.

Key facts: - 73,018 killed, 173,230+ injured since October 2023; 1,007+ killed since the October 2025 ceasefire. - Israeli control: ~64% of Gaza now, vs. 53% agreed; Netanyahu’s reported order targets 70%. - June 18 strikes killed at least four civilians in separate incidents across the strip.

3. Hegseth declares “NATO 3.0,” orders six-month review of US forces in Europe

Speaking in Brussels on June 18, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a six-month Pentagon review of US force posture, basing rights, access, and overflight arrangements across Europe, framed under the slogan “NATO 3.0.” He described it as “a post-Cold War recognition that NATO needs to be a real hard-line military alliance” with Europe taking “primary responsibility for the defense of Europe,” and tied future US financial contributions to whether allies meet his 5% of GDP defense-spending target.

Hegseth called it “shameful” that European NATO allies refused to provide US forces access to bases for launching attacks on Iran. NATO’s supreme allied commander has been developing backup contingency plans since June 3, when Washington signaled it would withdraw an aircraft carrier, aerial refueling aircraft, and dozens of fighter jets from European commitments.

The “NATO 3.0” framing accelerates a transatlantic realignment in which the European powers — above all Germany, which is rebuilding the Bundeswehr toward “war capability” by 2029 — are pressed to assume the costs of continental militarization while the US redirects forces toward the Pacific and the Middle East. The demand that allies hit 5% of GDP would compel a historic transfer of social spending into armaments across the continent.

Key facts: - Six-month review covers posture, basing, access, and overflight across the European theater. - US contributions conditioned on allies hitting 5% of GDP defense spending. - The drawdown signal from June 3 has already triggered European contingency planning.

4. Trump announces “lethal kinetic strike” on Tren de Aragua leader as Venezuela bombs gold miners

Trump announced a US “lethal kinetic strike” killing Héctor Guerrero Flores, the alleged leader of the Tren de Aragua gang — a man indicted in New York but never arrested or tried. The operation was coordinated with Venezuelan military helicopters that simultaneously bombed gold-mining regions in Bolívar state, forcing hundreds of informal miners to flee. “Bombs and gunfire could be heard in the jungle,” Reuters reported.

Guerrero Flores, 43, known by the alias “Niño Guerrero,” was credited with transforming a Venezuelan prison gang into a transnational operation spanning Latin America, the United States, and Spain. Venezuela’s communications ministry confirmed he was killed in a “combined operation” between US forces and Venezuelan security services in Bolívar state on June 12.

As Andrea Lobo argued on the WSWS, the action fits a pattern of at least 210 extrajudicial killings of fishermen and others in Caribbean and Venezuelan strikes, and serves US and Canadian mining corporations seeking control of Venezuela’s gold and oil. The Chavista government cooperated militarily while remaining economically subordinated to Washington — a striking confirmation of the Maduro government’s integration into the US “war on cartels” even as Washington threatens it with regime change.

Key facts: - Guerrero Flores killed by US strike without arrest or trial despite an existing US indictment. - Venezuelan helicopters bombed Bolívar state gold-mining zones in tandem with the US operation. - The killing extends a documented pattern of 210+ extrajudicial deaths in the region’s strike campaign.

5. Bolivia’s crisis deepens: two ministers resign, 100-plus roadblocks paralyze the country

Bolivia’s defense and education ministers resigned amid weeks of mass protests demanding the resignation of President Rodrigo Paz. More than 100 road blockades nationwide — nearly double the number of two weeks ago — have triggered shortages of food, medicine, and fuel in La Paz, El Alto, and surrounding cities. Between May 1 and June 2, the unrest left 10 dead, 37 injured, and produced 365 arrests.

The protest base is made up of miners, teachers, farmers, and other workers. The immediate trigger was a law permitting land mortgages, but the deeper cause is Bolivia’s worst economic crisis in 40 years, driven by collapsing energy production and a critical shortage of US dollars — the most severe contraction in at least a generation.

Key facts: - Defense and education ministers resigned; protesters demand Paz’s resignation. - 100+ roadblocks have produced food, fuel, and medicine shortages in major cities. - May 1–June 2: 10 deaths, 37 injuries, 365 arrests.

6. Brigham nurses vote 99.6% to authorize strike in largest such vote in Massachusetts history

Nurses at Brigham and Women’s Hospital voted 2,798 to 12 — a 99.6% authorization rate — on June 16 to authorize a one-day strike, which the Massachusetts Nurses Association called the largest nurse strike authorization vote in state history. After seven months of negotiations, Mass General Brigham executives offered 0% wage increases for nurses below the top pay step and proposed raising nurses’ health insurance costs. Core demands center on safe staffing ratios and a cost-of-living adjustment.

A 10-day notice is legally required before any strike; the next bargaining session was scheduled for June 18. Brigham and Women’s, one of the nation’s most prominent academic medical centers, has not seen a nurses strike in decades.

Key facts: - 2,798 to 12 in favor; 99.6% authorization — a state record. - Management offered 0% raises below the top step plus higher health costs after seven months of talks. - A one-day action requires 10 days’ notice; bargaining continued June 18.

Mississippi officer kills 1-year-old, firing into vehicle during shoplifting probe

Summer COVID wave underway as “Cicada” and KP variants spread

The CDC reports that COVID-19 cases are most likely rising in 39 states and declining nowhere, evidence that the anticipated summer wave has arrived. Hospitalizations climbed 25% from May 26 to June 1, the latest available data, and national wastewater activity has risen to “moderate,” up from “low” the prior week, with the highest levels in the Western US — Alaska, California, Colorado, Nevada, and Utah. The KP.2, KP.3, and KP.1.1 variants together account for roughly 63% of US infections, while the highly mutated “Cicada” subvariant (BA.3.2) has now been detected in at least 33 countries among travelers, patients, and wastewater. The renewed surge comes as the Trump administration and RFK Jr.’s public-health apparatus continue to scale back federal surveillance and vaccine access.

Measles cases pass 2,100 for 2026 as H5N1 persists in dairy and poultry

As of June 18, 2026, the US has recorded 2,104 confirmed measles cases this year — among the largest annual totals in decades and a direct consequence of collapsing childhood vaccination rates and the dismantling of public-health infrastructure. Meanwhile, A(H5) avian influenza remains widespread in wild birds and continues to cause outbreaks in poultry and US dairy cows, with sporadic human cases among dairy and poultry workers. The CDC characterizes the public-health risk as low but continues to monitor people with animal exposures.

Arts and culture

The day’s most substantial cultural intervention came from the WSWS itself: David Walsh’s review of Steven Soderbergh’s new film The Christophers. Walsh argues that the film — centered on a fictional artist, Julian Sklar, who declares he has produced “nothing but shit in 30 years. Nothing at all in 20” — is stylish but fundamentally shallow, itself guilty of the same fashionable cleverness it gestures at. Drawing on the 19th-century Russian critic N.A. Dobrolyubov’s argument that subject matter matters more than raw talent, Walsh contends that Soderbergh’s generation abandoned its radical instincts under the reaction of the 1980s and 1990s and retreated into irony, producing work that cannot grip mass audiences because it lacks genuine commitment to meaningful social themes. Critics praise it, Walsh writes, because “they are not difficult to impress.” The review is a useful entry point for the broader question of why a technically accomplished American cinema remains so politically and emotionally evasive in a period of war and social crisis.

The art world continues to absorb the loss of David Hockney, who died June 11 at age 88. One of the most important British painters of the postwar era, Hockney moved across pop art, the California swimming-pool canvases, photo-collage, opera design, and late-period iPad works such as the 90-metre digital frieze A Year in Normandie. The Tate has confirmed two posthumous projects for next year: a major Tate Britain retrospective spanning seven decades and a Turbine Hall multimedia installation of his opera designs. Hockney’s restless reinvention and his insistence on the direct observation of life and landscape stand as a rebuke to the ironic detachment Walsh criticizes in Soderbergh — a genuine artistic achievement rooted in sustained engagement with the visible world.

Other developments: - Gene Shalit, the Today show’s longtime, mustachioed movie and book critic, died June 12 at age 100. A mainstay of American morning television from 1970 until his 2010 retirement, his career traced the transformation of cultural criticism into mass-media personality. - Anthony Head, the actor known for Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Ted Lasso, died at 72. - Hockney tribute exhibitions and retrospectives across London — at the Tate and Serpentine — point to the enduring public hunger for serious visual art even as arts institutions face funding pressure and commercialization.

Sri Lanka: residents in Colombo housing complex protest dilapidated conditions — WSWS reporters

Residents of the Sahaspura housing complex — 671 units and over 20,000 inhabitants across 14 floors — protested June 3 after only one of six elevators remained operational, forcing the elderly and children to climb up to 14 flights. What the WSWS should cover today

1. Who captures Iran’s $300 billion reconstruction fund?

The US-Iran MOU establishes a $300 billion reconstruction fund and immediately permits Iranian oil sales, but no reporting has examined which US defense contractors, oil multinationals, and Gulf investors stand to capture it, or how Iranian workers — many of whom protested the regime in 2025–2026 — figure into reconstruction planning. A socialist account would trace the flow of reconstruction capital and oil revenue as the material content of the “peace.”

Recommended reading Newsweek What’s in Donald Trump’s 14-point US-Iran peace deal?

2. The Minab school massacre: 120 students killed on Day 1 of the Iran war

On February 28, 2026, the opening day of US-Israeli strikes, a school in Minab was struck, killing 156 civilians including 120 students. This mass killing of children has largely disappeared from Western coverage as diplomatic framing took over. The accountability gap for this and other opening-phase civilian strikes deserves sustained coverage as the war is “settled” diplomatically.

Recommended reading Wikipedia Casualties of the 2026 Iran war

3. The class composition of Bolivia’s revolt

Bolivia’s protests are reported as a crisis of presidential legitimacy, but the movement’s base is miners, teachers, and agricultural workers responding to a 40-year economic low. Coverage has not examined the workers’ specific demands on wages and land reform, or why a government with progressive credentials now faces a revolt from its own social base — a question central to the perspective of permanent revolution.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Bolivia in crisis: Social unrest, demands for president to resign escalate

4. Congo’s mineral wealth and the real stakes of the DRC war

Reporting treats the Rwanda/M23 forced-recruitment campaign and the stalled Doha talks as a regional security problem. Missing is the economic core: eastern Congo holds some of the world’s largest coltan, cobalt, and gold deposits, and M23 control maps onto these zones. A socialist account would name the corporations profiting from instability and the Western governments protecting Rwanda’s M23 patronage relationship.

Recommended reading Human Rights Watch DR Congo: Rwanda, M23 forcibly recruit, detain thousands

5. The summer COVID wave and the dismantling of public health

With cases rising in 39 states, hospitalizations up 25% in a week, and the “Cicada” variant spreading, the renewed COVID surge coincides with the gutting of federal surveillance and vaccine access under RFK Jr. The WSWS is well positioned to connect the “let it rip” policy to the broader assault on social infrastructure and the interests of capital in keeping workers on the job.

Recommended reading Vaccine Advisor Summer COVID-19 surge continues as wastewater levels rise

6. The Gulf Coast disaster and working-class vulnerability

Tropical Storm Arthur’s three deaths — including a worker killed during cleanup — and the transfer of detainees out of “Alligator Alcatraz” expose how disaster preparedness and infrastructure failure fall hardest on the working class and the most vulnerable. The intersection of climate-driven extreme weather, deportation infrastructure, and austerity-starved emergency services warrants sustained coverage as hurricane season begins.
