1. Trump pledges no Iran strike before the midterms as the Pentagon readies a three-day campaign

On Thursday Trump said the United States will not attack Iran before the November 3 midterm elections. He called the talks with Tehran “productive,” though Al Jazeera notes that they appear to be at an impasse. The statement came hours after a series of reports in the Atlantic, Axios, the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal, summarized by Andre Damon on the WSWS, describing detailed Pentagon preparations to resume bombing. These include a roughly three-day air campaign against Iranian drone, missile and energy targets, and a Camp David strategy meeting chaired by Vice President Vance on October 2. As Damon put it, the question being discussed in Washington is timing, not whether to attack. A pledge not to strike before November 3 implies a decision to strike afterward.

Russia moved openly to Tehran’s side. Putin met Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian in Turkmenistan on Thursday before a Commonwealth of Independent States summit. “On our part, we are ready to do everything we can to help you resolve this situation,” he said, according to Interfax, and he described the war as having begun through no fault of Iran’s. A day earlier, by phone, Putin had offered to mediate. Washington accuses Moscow of passing targeting data to Iran for its strikes on US assets.

Key facts:

  • Hormuz: A political deputy of the IRGC navy said Iranian forces conduct operations “every night” in the Strait of Hormuz against vessels Tehran considers to be breaking its rules. Britain’s UKMTO is investigating reports of multiple casualties aboard the tanker struck north of Qatar this week.
  • Saudi Arabia: Saudi Arabia’s Civil Aviation Authority said King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh was “subjected to two attacks on Thursday,” killing three Saudi citizens. These deaths come in addition to the three foreign workers killed at Abha and Riyadh earlier in the week.
  • US losses: Al Jazeera reports at least 81 US aircraft, worth up to $3.3 billion, lost or damaged in the war. Damon cites AP figures of 19 US military dead and more than 800 wounded, and Al Jazeera’s count of 3,527 Iranians killed as of August 28.
  • Public opinion: 69% of Americans call the war not worth fighting (AP-NORC).
  • Carrier: The USS Abraham Lincoln docked at Naval Air Station North Island in San Diego on Thursday after a record 265 days at sea.

2. ICE agent shoots Bronx man in a car with a five-year-old as DHS builds an election-fraud apparatus

At about 4 p.m. Thursday, during what DHS called a “targeted enforcement operation” in the Bronx, a plainclothes federal immigration officer shot Oscar Belgal, a 28-year-old Dominican national. Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch said Belgal was pulling into a parking space when an officer approached with his gun drawn, followed by a second officer also out of uniform. When Belgal moved the car forward, at least one officer fired seven rounds and hit him below the left ear. A five-year-old child was in the car. The child was unhurt, and Belgal was conscious in hospital. Mayor Zohran Mamdani called the shooting “unconscionable,” said ICE “must be abolished,” and said he had called Trump to demand an end to ICE enforcement in the city. Governor Kathy Hochul asked Trump and DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin to halt operations in New York and release any bodycam footage. Protesters marched from the scene to the hospital Thursday night, and there were scuffles with police.

The shooting comes as the administration turns the immigration apparatus toward the midterms. CNN reports that a DHS “election fraud” operation has drawn on tens of millions of records, scrutinized dozens of nonprofit voter-registration groups, and considered deploying undercover agents. Mamdani’s and Hochul’s response, appeals to Trump himself, shows that the Democratic Party has no answer to the use of federal police powers against the population beyond petitioning the man directing them.

Key facts:

  • Arrest quota: ICE leaders ordered agents to raise daily arrests by 1,000, to a goal of 3,000 a day, before the election.
  • Prosecutions: A Reuters review found only 129 federal prosecutions in total under the 1996 “voting by aliens” statute.
  • Scale and funding: ICE doubled in size in Trump’s first year back in office, to more than 22,000 agents. It now has about $75 billion over four years and is the best-funded federal law enforcement agency.
  • Precedent: In January, federal agents in Minneapolis shot dead two US citizens, Renee Nicole Good and Alex Pretti. Washington then removed local authorities from the investigation and surged 3,000 agents into the state.

3. Pentagon will livestream the firing-squad execution of Nidal Hasan

On Thursday, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth told Jack Posobiec of Real America’s Voice that the military execution of former Army Major Nidal Hasan will be public. “We’ll make sure that people are able to watch it, that it’s public,” he said, “because people need to understand that there’s serious consequences for these types of things.” He called the decision a “no-brainer.” A Defense Department official told Al Jazeera the execution will be “livestreamed.” It is set for December 3 at Fort Hood, at 1 p.m. Central, the same hour as the 2009 shooting in which Hasan killed 13 people. Trump authorized the firing squad on Monday.

No state or federal execution in US history has been televised. The last public execution took place in Owensboro, Kentucky, on August 14, 1936, and the last US military firing-squad execution was in 1945. In April the administration made the firing squad, gas asphyxiation and electrocution acceptable methods for federal executions. The broadcast is meant to bring state killing back as public spectacle, and it is being promoted through far-right media.

Key facts:

  • Witnesses: Army regulation requires at least two media outlets as witnesses. Hegseth did not say whether troops or the public would attend in person.
  • Lethal injection: Al Jazeera’s analysis of the Christa Pike case says botched lethal injections are common.
  • 2026 total: Texas’s execution of Jamaal Howard was the 30th US execution this year. Florida accounts for 16 of them.

4. French youth march again as minister pledges no more school job cuts

On Thursday more than 100,000 high school students, university students and workers marched across France, in a national day of action called by high school student unions. Two days earlier, nearly half a million had marched. In Paris, police fired tear gas and turned water cannons on students demanding more funding and more teachers. The education minister met student leaders and promised no further job cuts in schools. It is the government’s first concession on substance after three weeks of repression and a suspension of high school classes that officials presented as a lesson in democracy.

As V. Gnana and Alexandre Lantier wrote on the WSWS, the youth “cannot defeat police repression or end austerity, militarism and war on their own.” The union federations have called only one-day actions, and neither they nor La France insoumise have called a general strike or demanded Macron’s removal. Placards read “October 2026 is the new May 68” and “Gen Z international revolt.”

Key facts:

  • Grievances: Staff shortages, overcrowded classrooms and decaying buildings.
  • Spreading movement: Similar youth movements are under way in Belgium (high school strikes) and Germany (protests against conscription), alongside the Spanish housing protests.
  • What comes next: The minister’s job pledge will be tested against a budget written under bond-market pressure.

5. Spain mourns Maricarmen as the Popular Party refuses a minute of silence

On Thursday, thousands marched from the Puerta del Sol encampment to the former home of María del Carmen Abascal, the 87-year-old who died on Wednesday before she could return to the flat she had been evicted from. Mourners left flowers, candles, notes and children’s drawings at the building. Protesters chanted that these were murders, not deaths. “They killed her through heartbreak,” said one demonstrator, Diana.

Her death has deepened the political divide before the November 29 snap election. The Popular Party holds absolute majorities in the Madrid regional and municipal assemblies, and it refused a minute of silence. Its deputies stayed seated with folded arms while left deputies gave a 60-second standing ovation. PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo accused the Socialists of exploiting the crisis: “We will not allow this attempt to manipulate the emotions of the Spanish people.” Sánchez had called the election after parliament rejected his emergency housing measures.

Key facts:

  • CIS survey: In a June survey published Thursday by the state pollster CIS, 76.3% of Spaniards backed market intervention to guarantee access to housing.
  • Housing as a right: In the same survey, 79% said the right to housing should take precedence over housing as a commodity.
  • Ombudsman: Ángel Gabilondo backs more public housing, limits on tourist rentals and stronger eviction protections, and is preparing a housing report.
  • Tenants’ warning: A tenants’ union, quoted by Jacobin: “Either rentierism ends, or the government falls.”

South Africa: anti-migrant riots in Soweto and Durban

On Thursday, anti-migrant protests turned violent in Johannesburg’s Soweto township and in Durban. Acting National Police Commissioner Lt. Gen. Puleng Dimpane said 10 vehicles were torched in Johannesburg and 14 in Durban. Three people were arrested in Durban, where AFP reported police firing rubber bullets. Reuters saw residents in Soweto loot a shop belonging to a Somali national and chase him away. The protesters are targeting a July Constitutional Court ruling that removed procedural barriers to claiming asylum. They demand that refugee centers stop processing applications. Government spokesperson William Baloyi blamed “deliberately spread” misinformation, noting that the ruling grants no automatic status. The Inter-Ministerial Committee on Migration says asylum offices are overwhelmed.

  • Populist groups have campaigned for months for undocumented foreigners to leave, and tens of thousands have fled.
  • About a third of South Africans are unemployed three decades after apartheid. The ANC-led government of national unity has offered workers nothing but scapegoats.

Gaza ceasefire: a Palestinian killed or wounded every 90 minutes

According to a year-end report from the UK charity Islamic Relief, Israeli forces have killed or wounded a Palestinian about every 90 minutes since the October 2025 ceasefire. That amounts to at least 1,454 killed, 320 of them children, and more than 5,000 wounded. Israel uses its “Yellow Line” to keep Palestinians out of 65% of the Strip, crowding nearly 2.1 million people into the rest. About 82% of structures are damaged or destroyed, and more than 20,000 severely ill patients are denied permission to leave for treatment. “There is no ceasefire in reality,” an Islamic Relief aid worker said. On Thursday an Israeli strike killed a Palestinian near Deir el-Balah, and in the West Bank a 13-year-old was shot during a raid on Yamoun.

UN Security Council extends Darfur arms embargo but blocks Sudan-wide sanctions

On Thursday the Security Council voted unanimously for Resolution 2830, extending the Darfur sanctions regime to December 11 and its expert panel to January 11, 2027. Russia and China blocked a US push to extend the embargo across Sudan. “We will not allow even a hint of broadening the restrictions,” said Russia’s deputy ambassador, Anna Evstigneeva. Since April 2023 the war has killed at least 59,000 people and displaced about 13 million. The vote turned on great-power alignment, not on Sudanese lives: Egypt backs the army, and the UAE is accused of arming the RSF.

Ebola: Kenya’s suspected second case negative as DRC epidemic passes 8,700 cases

On Thursday, preliminary tests on a 20-year-old man in Wajir County, Kenya, came back negative for Ebola. He had traveled from the Democratic Republic of Congo through Uganda. Kenya’s first-ever case, a citizen who fell ill in the DRC, died in Nairobi earlier this week. He had taken the same route and passed several airport temperature screenings, which exposed gaps in border surveillance. Africanews reports that 28 of his contacts have been traced.

The DRC outbreak, declared in May, is now the second largest on record: more than 8,700 reported cases and at least 4,205 deaths. It is caused by the Bundibugyo virus, for which no licensed vaccine exists. The Ervebo vaccine, used against the Zaire strain since 2019, does not protect against it. Kenya is the fourth country to record a case linked to the epidemic. Uganda had about 20 cases before it was declared Ebola-free in July. A death rate near 50% in a strain without a vaccine makes this a serious international threat. It is spreading while the US has cut USAID and withdrawn from the WHO.

The WHO has asked Russia for further information on the lab tests conducted on the Irkutsk Anti-Plague Research Institute worker who died on October 2, and to verify reports of a second employee with pneumonia. “WHO has requested further information on the lab tests conducted on the individual who died,” Tedros said. Surveillance roundup: measles, H5N1, COVID

  • Measles: The CDC count is unchanged since the last update: 3,887 confirmed cases in 47 jurisdictions as of October 1, about 70% above all of 2025, with about 95% outbreak-linked. Kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.4% from 95.2% in 2019–20, and the CDC estimates about 280,000 kindergartners are unprotected. The next update is due this week.
  • H5N1: Bird detections in Europe hit a seasonal low over the summer, at 110 across 12 countries from June to August (ECDC). The CDC counted 12 human infections outside the US between August 2025 and June 2026, in Bangladesh, Cambodia and India, with 3 deaths. Autumn migration usually brings a rise. The Netherlands will require poultry to be kept indoors from October 16.
  • COVID-19: The CDC’s wastewater variant page has not shown new data since May, when XFG.1.1 predominated. Under current HHS leadership, the US has no timely public picture of variant circulation as respiratory season begins. That gap is itself a public health failure.

Arts and culture

Adam Wingard’s Onslaught and the return of Operation Paperclip. Wingard (You’re Next, The Guest) has made an action-horror film about fascist elements in the US high command who release surgically enhanced “super soldiers” on the domestic population. It opens with a montage of Border Patrol raids, Abu Ghraib, Iraq and Vietnam, set against the anti-war platitudes of presidents of both parties. Its villain, Hans Kammler (Dan Stevens), is a “third-generation Paperclip” scientist. The name comes from the SS general who oversaw the V-2 program and helped build the concentration camps. The program itself recalls Truman’s 1945 recruitment of some 1,600 Nazi scientists and officials. Wingard told Variety that a photograph of masked ICE agents beside a vehicle “looked like a shot from” his film. Asked to name the most dangerous super-villain, he said, “The president of the United States.”

Reviewing it for the WSWS, Carlos Delgado credits the film with an instinctive hostility to the military-intelligence apparatus. He finds its most disturbing sequence, a massacre at a rural truck stop, effective in showing working-class people treated as disposable, the way US imperialism treats its victims abroad. The weakness is the film’s premise that fascism is a foreign import, which, Delgado argues, gets the history backwards. Washington took in the Nazis because the US and German ruling classes shared the aim of crushing the working class and defeating the Soviet Union. The film’s second half falls back into genre clichés and Illuminati-style conspiracy.

Leon Botstein resigns from Bard after Epstein email surfaces. Botstein was Bard College’s president for 51 years and is the longtime music director of the American Symphony Orchestra. On Wednesday he resigned from all his Bard positions. Congressional investigators found an email in which he asked Jeffrey Epstein whether a young woman was “for me, or are you keeping her for yourself.” Representatives Jamie Raskin and Madeleine Dean asked Attorney General Todd Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel to open a criminal investigation. They say the Justice Department has held the evidence for at least 18 months. Botstein’s tenure tied a liberal arts institution and a leading orchestra to Epstein’s money and social circle.
