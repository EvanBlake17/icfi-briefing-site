1. Brazil votes in a dead-heat runoff between Lula and Flávio Bolsonaro

Brazilians vote today in the second round between President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (PT, Broad Front) and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro (PL). Both closed their campaigns on Saturday with rallies: Lula, 80, on Copacabana Beach and Avenida Paulista, and Bolsonaro in Minas Gerais, with hundreds of motorcyclists in yellow national team shirts, and in Rio’s Barra da Tijuca. Voting is electronic and results usually come within hours of polls closing. Governors in five states and the Federal District are also on the runoff ballot.

The final CNT/MDA survey, released Saturday, has Bolsonaro at 46.3 percent and Lula at 45 percent. A week earlier the same pollster had it 46.8 to 45.9. Lula attacked his opponent’s ties to Washington on the final day: “The people must choose either someone who kisses the national flag or someone who kisses the American flag.” One organizer at a rally told AFP voters were “stuck choosing between the worst, the horrible, and the lesser evil.” As Tomas Castanheira wrote on the WSWS this week, Lula is offering “essentially the same fiscal agenda” as Bolsonaro, “in a form designed to avoid social upheaval.”

Key facts: - Electorate: almost 160 million are eligible to vote and voting is compulsory. More than 33 million people (21 percent) did not vote in the first round on October 4, when Bolsonaro led 47 to 45 percent, a margin of 2.2 million votes. - Lula’s record: his approval fell as low as 24 percent this term over crime and the cost of living. He raised Bolsa Família by 15 percent, cut fuel taxes, and backed legislation the Senate approved cutting the maximum workweek from 44 to 40 hours. - Bolsonaro’s program: El Salvador-style megaprisons, designating transnational gangs as terrorist organizations, and trying 16-year-olds as adults. He admitted taking millions of dollars from banker Daniel Vorcaro to fund a film about his father and is under investigation for corruption, money laundering and tax evasion. - Washington’s role: Trump designated Brazil’s largest drug-trafficking groups as terrorist organizations shortly after receiving Flávio at the White House. The Lula government called it a pretext for intervention. Trump imposed 50 percent tariffs last year, citing the prosecution of Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving 27 years under house detention for the coup attempt. A new round of tariffs followed this year. - Stakes for the region: the Seattle Times/AP headline frames the vote as one with “US influence over Latin America at stake,” and ABC (Australia) writes that Trump “could soon be one step closer” to “restoring” US power in the hemisphere.

2. Trump says a US attack on Iran could come as early as Sunday

Trump said on Saturday that Washington is weighing an attack on Iran as soon as Sunday, while also floating a “major deal.” He said the US might have to “finish the job.” This reverses the line the White House gave on October 23, that the US “will not be attacking Iran at any time prior to the midterm elections.” At a Florida rally on Friday, Trump said the military “may do another little Epic Fury,” the codename for the bombing campaign that opened the war on February 28.

Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian blamed Washington for the collapse of talks on an interim agreement, calling the Americans “cowards” who “messed everything up,” but said both sides should return to the deal and “conclude it.” Government spokeswoman Fatemeh Mohajerani said Tehran would not “wait for permission to defend itself.” The IRGC warned through Fars that even limited strikes would bring a response “several times greater.”

Key facts: - Evacuations: Washington has reportedly ordered many diplomatic staff to leave Saudi Arabia, and departures from other countries in the region are expected (Al Jazeera). - Intelligence memo: an October 23 memo from Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton to Rubio says Iranian lethal plotting against US citizens has “reached advanced stages in both the Middle East and certain other countries” (ABC News, first reported by Axios). - Shipping: the IRGC said seven tankers were attacked or hit mines overnight into Thursday, and two supertankers struck mines on Friday. UKMTO separately reported a tanker hit by an unknown projectile in Hormuz. - Iranian deterrent claims: Iranian media say Tehran has activated a “dead hand” doctrine allowing attacks from all 31 provinces if more senior figures are assassinated. The IRGC has threatened seabed internet and communications cables if civilian infrastructure is hit again. - Istanbul meeting: foreign ministers of Türkiye, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Egypt, joined by the UK, France, Germany and Italy, met without the US or Iran. The four regional states called Israeli occupation “the main source of instability in the region and beyond.” Turkish FM Hakan Fidan said military intervention “is by no means a solution.” - Leadership: Mojtaba Khamenei, who succeeded his assassinated father in March, has not appeared in public since. Judiciary chief Mohseni Ejei and speaker and chief negotiator Ghalibaf have reportedly met him this week. - Iranian commentary: analyst Reza Nasri, who has ties to the Foreign Ministry, wrote that Iran has reached a verdict of “enough is enough” and will not negotiate under bombardment.

3. Second weekend of mass protests sweeps France, Italy, Spain and Albania

Demonstrations against the cost of living, school conditions and governments took place across southern and western Europe on Saturday, a week after the October 17 riots. The scale in France was close to the previous weekend’s even though routes were banned and the security deployment was the largest yet. The youth-led movement that began with high school blockades in mid-September now openly calls for Macron’s resignation and denounces the billionaires.

France: the Interior Ministry counted 410,000 marchers nationwide, 110,000 of them in Paris. The CGT says both figures are almost double. Police arrested “nearly 1,000” people and carried out more than 3,000 checks in Paris. Officers fired tear gas and water cannon at nightfall, and the authorities said 500 “rioters” clashed with police. The government allowed only a route on the city’s southeastern rim. It banned protests not organized by unions in Paris and all protests in Montpellier, Rouen and Nice. Police used tear gas on 150 people in Lyon. At least 24 metro stations closed. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez spoke of “an extremely elevated level of violence,” while the government’s line was “the marches went well.” Student representatives said Friday’s meeting with Macron had resolved nothing. He promised more teachers and an overhaul of the Parcoursup admissions system. The movement has called another protest for next Saturday.

The far right: the National Rally’s congress in Orléans re-elected Jordan Bardella as party president with nearly 99 percent. Le Pen and Bardella were due to launch their presidential campaign before about 10,000 supporters on Sunday. Thousands of counter-protesters chanted antifascist slogans in the city.

Italy: protests took place in eight cities against the Meloni government. Milan peaked at about 10,000, mostly young people, with hundreds of soldiers deployed. ANSA reported clashes in Turin and Rome. Police reported nine arrests and 371 people briefly detained nationwide, after about 150 house raids in Lombardy on Saturday morning. The administrator of the Movimento 17 Ottobre Instagram page, a 30-year-old Italian-Moroccan journalist, is under investigation for “inciting crimes.” With most of those arrested under 18, Justice Minister Carlo Nordio said Italy needs to expand youth detention capacity.

Spain: about 25,000 marched from Atocha to Gran Vía in Madrid to defend the state pension system before next month’s general election. They demanded continued inflation indexing and an end to the gender pension gap.

Albania: in Tirana, police used tear gas, water cannon and rubber bullets on thousands of protesters. More than 300 protesters and police were injured, the worst clashes so far, and dozens were arrested. Protesters threw stones and rolled burning bins at the Education Ministry. They also set fire to the red cushions Prime Minister Edi Rama had laid outside government headquarters as a symbol of “dialogue.” Rama blamed “foreign actors,” Russia and Iran. Some placards denounced opposition leader Sali Berisha as well.

4. Israel drives 2,000 Palestinians from their homes in Hebron and seals the West Bank for election day

The Hebron operation that began on Friday turned into a mass expulsion overnight. Soldiers announced evacuation orders over loudspeakers late at night. Hebron Mayor Yousef al-Ja’bari says 2,200 people were forced out, and Defense Minister Israel Katz put the figure at “about 2,000.” Katz said they “will not be allowed to return to their neighbourhood until we’ve finished dealing with all the Hamas issues in Hebron.” The city of about 200,000 is sealed, and no one can enter or leave. The army has turned Palestinian homes into military posts (Anadolu) and is bulldozing buildings. Soldiers fired a stun grenade at an Al Jazeera crew filming demolitions near the Farsh al-Hawa checkpoint.

The army will bar Palestinian workers from most of the West Bank on Sunday and Monday and close all crossings on Tuesday, when Israel votes. Netanyahu would not say whether he would dismantle the Palestinian Authority but promised it a “big surprise.” Al Jazeera’s Nour Odeh says Likud and its coalition, which trail in the polls, are trying to “capitalise” on the attack.

Key facts: - Raids: at least 15 areas were raided on Saturday, including Jenin, Nablus, Ramallah, al-Bireh, Birzeit, East Jerusalem and Bethlehem. The Palestinian Prisoner’s Society counted about 70 arrests. In Halhul, nine people, six of them children, were treated for tear gas inhalation. - Guterres: the closures “severely restrict Palestinians’ freedom of movement, access to basic services and livelihoods.” - UN Human Rights Office (Mayy El Sheikh): “yet another episode in a never-ending stream of suffocating measures.” - B’Tselem: “collective punishment and acts that could constitute war crimes.” - Israeli election: thousands marched from Rabin Square to Hostages Square in Tel Aviv on Saturday at the October Council’s call for a state commission of inquiry into October 7. The government offers only a “political” commission appointed half by the coalition and half by the opposition. The last polls before the gag period show neither Netanyahu’s bloc nor Gadi Eisenkot’s with a majority.

5. BC Conservatives win their first majority in nearly a century as Eby resigns

The British Columbia Conservatives, led by interim leader Lorne Doerkson, were projected to win a majority in Saturday’s snap provincial election. CBC calls it the party’s first majority “in nearly a century.” NDP Premier David Eby, whose decision to call the vote two years early the Vancouver Sun says “backfired,” resigned as party leader on election night. Doerkson promised to get BC “back on track.” Some Vancouver Island seats were still close as of the latest reports, and no final seat counts were available.

Eby called the election to exploit opposition to Trump’s tariffs and annexation threats, and the Conservatives’ split after they fell three seats short in 2024. As Penny Smith wrote on the WSWS before the vote, the NDP campaigned on a 2026 budget that cuts 15,000 full-time public-sector jobs by 2028–29 and withdraws $1.4 billion from social housing. That left the field to a party campaigning on tax cuts, more police and doubling gas production.

Key facts: - Conservative platform: tripling the basic personal exemption to $32,000, a “never raise taxes” pledge, more police, and an “Energy Superpower Plan” to double gas production by 2032. - NDP legacy: Eby’s “Balanced Measures Mandate” set a flat 3 percent annual raise for more than 450,000 public employees. His “Look West” strategy targeted $200 billion in major-project investment and a 35 percent share of federal defence vessel contracts. - Splinter parties: CentreBC and Sturko remained “positive” despite the night’s results (Maple Ridge News). The Greens under Emily Lowan had campaigned to “hold the balance of power.”

6. Serbia and Bulgaria go to the polls

Serbia: polls are open from 7 a.m. to 8 p.m. in the snap parliamentary election Aleksandar Vučić called in September, after almost two years of student-led protests that began when the Novi Sad station canopy collapsed in November 2024, killing 16. Eighteen lists are competing for 250 seats. Vučić resigned the presidency to run for prime minister and is promising higher pensions and public-sector wages, more health spending and subsidies for young couples. The Student List is led by cardiologist Ilija Srdanović and fields about 250 mostly unknown candidates, among them professors, actors and farmers. Its program centers on corruption and education. An Ipsos poll published Thursday gave Vučić’s SNS 47 percent and the students 40 percent. An October 20 poll had the students ahead, 44.5 to 42.7. Verisk Maplecroft’s Mario Bikarski calls it “a watershed moment,” and Al Jazeera compares a Vučić defeat to Orbán’s loss in April. Vučić claims the students are financed by “foreign agents” in Croatia and Montenegro.

Bulgaria: 27 candidates are running for the largely ceremonial but directly elected presidency. Acting President Iliana Iotova, backed by Rumen Radev, who won the parliamentary election that analysts called Kremlin-friendly, leads former caretaker PM Andrey Gyurov 41 to 28 percent (Alpha Research). Gyurov signed a security agreement with Kyiv while in office. Iotova’s camp calls for a diplomatic end to the Ukraine war and, according to analysts, an end to arms deliveries. She says her opponents favor “a war of attrition” when “we are all the ones who are most exhausted.” Turnout is expected below the 50 percent threshold, so a November 1 runoff is likely. Drone strikes on merchant ships off the Bulgarian coast, which likely killed about two dozen seafarers, dominated the campaign. Radev has not explicitly blamed Moscow but said the outcome “benefits the Russian Federation.”

Ukraine says Starlink is restored after Musk admits throttling it

Defense Minister Yevhenii Khmara said Saturday that Starlink and Starshield connections are again stable on equipment moving faster than 400 km/h. More than a week of restrictions had hobbled Ukrainian drone operations. Zelenskyy wrote: “The speeds are now where they need to be.” Musk admitted that SpaceX had throttled the service. He claimed Ukraine’s military violated its terms by using it for offensive drone strikes, said that “loophole” was closed last week, and warned the front would collapse if Starlink were switched off. Trump denied responsibility while defending Musk. The episode shows how much a single billionaire’s private company controls the communications of a frontline army.

  • Talks: Kremlin spokesman Peskov confirmed the planned US-Ukraine-Russia meeting in Abu Dhabi. Zelenskyy says technical-level meetings have been agreed.
  • Oleshky: the Melania Trump-brokered local ceasefire takes effect on October 27, with a humanitarian operation planned for October 29.
  • Strikes: Russian attacks killed one person in Lozova, at least three in Dnipropetrovsk region, one in Kramatorsk (six wounded, three of them children) and one in Sloviansk. Ukraine hit the Togliattikauchuk petrochemical plant, a petrochemical plant and Ozon warehouse in Omsk, and the Proton electronics plant in Oryol, where the governor reported three dead. Crimea had power cuts.
  • Casualty count: the Kyiv Independent reports that a media investigation has now identified almost 283,000 Russian soldiers killed in the war by name.

Houthis fire on Aden and Saudi Arabia as Riyadh airport reopens

Yemen’s Transport Ministry says two Houthi ballistic missiles hit Aden airport, the fourth such attack this month, along with the coastguard headquarters. The Houthis claimed strikes on the al-Nasr camp and Aden’s naval base. Saudi civil defense said two missiles were fired toward Mecca and Medina on Saturday, which the Houthis called a “huge lie.” They say their targets were three Saudi airports, oil facilities including the Shaybah field and Dammam airport, and an airbase. King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh resumed flights on Sunday after two weeks closed. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree called it “unsafe” and a “military target,” and the coalition said it downed two drones over Riyadh early Sunday. A Houthi attack earlier this month killed 13 and wounded more than 300, the deadliest in the kingdom in over a decade.

  • Yemen’s army says a malfunctioning Houthi missile killed three people in a Sanaa home on Friday. Houthi Al Masirah TV reports civilian deaths from coalition strikes in Sanaa and Hodeidah.

Jordan church massacre toll rises to nine

One of the wounded from Friday’s attack on a funeral at St George’s Greek Orthodox Church in Hamoud, Karak governorate, died on Saturday, bringing the toll to nine. Forty were wounded. The authorities call it “an act of extremist terrorism,” but the attackers have not been found and no group has claimed it. King Abdullah visited Karak and promised punishment “with an iron fist.” Prime Minister Jafar Hassan blamed “terrorist ideology and blind extremism.” The US embassy says security has been stepped up at malls and embassies. A survivor, the lawyer Nasser al-Burqan, said a gunman fired at him from about two meters away.

Gunmen kill 10 to 40 in Nigeria’s Taraba State

Gunmen on about 50 motorcycles, two to a bike, attacked Binnari village in Taraba State after Friday prayers. They looted food, cash and motorcycles. Police recovered 10 bodies, survivors told AFP at least 20 were killed, and Amnesty International puts the toll at 40, with more bodies found on Saturday. Amnesty said the authorities “continue to leave rural communities in some parts of the north at the mercy of gunmen.” Separately, the military said an air strike in Borno killed 15 “terrorists.”

Justice Department demands California counties set aside suspected noncitizen ballots

Federal prosecutors have sent letters to several California counties demanding that election officials identify and sequester ballots cast by suspected noncitizens. Reuters reports the department wants counties to help identify suspected noncitizen voters. ABC News says officials are “frustrated, rattled” by the demand. The Mercury News reports that Bay Area elections offices received what officials called “threatening” letters, and the Guardian quotes a California leader calling it “weaponizing the law.” The coverage available does not say how many counties were targeted or which lists the DOJ is using. It follows the department’s suit to force California to purge its rolls using DHS lists of alleged noncitizens. Separately, a federal judge has restricted DHS investigations into noncitizen voting, citing flawed data.

DOJ sues Alaska to block certification if Senator Sullivan narrowly loses

On Thursday the Justice Department sued Alaska and local election officials in federal court in Anchorage. It wants to block certification of the Senate race if Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan loses narrowly to Democrat Mary Peltola. The suit argues voters could confuse him with a second Republican candidate, Dan J. Sullivan Jr., a former Forest Service worker and teacher whom the Alaska Supreme Court allowed on the ballot in June without a party label. The suit asks for an investigation into whether votes cast for him were meant for the senator. Control of the Senate could depend on the race.

  • Trump: “I’m the one that said do it.”
  • Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R): the DOJ “should never have filed this lawsuit.”
  • David Becker (Center for Election Innovation and Research): the administration “has absolutely no authority to tell Alaska when or how to certify the results.”
  • Derek Muller (Notre Dame law): “one of the worst ones I’ve ever seen.”

Together with the California letters, this is a federal government laying the legal groundwork, in advance, to contest results it does not like.

Obama campaigns in Ohio and Michigan, attacking the Iran war’s handling

Obama, 65, campaigned in Cleveland for Senate candidate Sherrod Brown and gubernatorial candidate Amy Acton, then in Detroit for Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed and gubernatorial candidate Jocelyn Benson. He heads to Georgia next week with Jon Ossoff and Keisha Lance Bottoms. He called Trump’s war “an unnecessary war that nobody asked for.” But his central charge was that it failed: “Iran now has veto power over the Strait of Hormuz, where oil goes through to get to the world.” He also said, “We have never seen a president excel in straight-up grifting, on your dime.” Obama called the midterms a “circuit breaker.” He defended El-Sayed against attacks on his Muslim faith and denounced Vivek Ramaswamy’s ad featuring Acton’s estranged mother, which Trump defended while voting early in Florida.

The Democrats’ closing message is the one the WSWS has documented all month: opposition to Trump’s management of the war, not to the war.

Ancient proteins put a face on the Denisovans

A team led by Qiaomei Fu at the Chinese Academy of Sciences reports in Nature that it extracted and sequenced 18 ancient proteins from a skull and a jaw fragment excavated at Xujiayao in northern China in 1976–79. The fragments had been assigned to different lineages. The key marker is a collagen variant, COL1A2-996K, found in Denisovans but not in Neanderthals or modern humans, which “classifies Xujiayao” as Denisovan. 3D reconstructions show low, flat skulls with thick bone, heavy brow ridges and flat faces, combining older hominin traits with ones shared with Neanderthals and later humans. The result means the Denisovans, until now known mainly from Siberia and Tibet, lived across East Asia, and other unassigned East Asian fossils may belong to the same lineage. This is one of the most significant advances in paleoanthropology this year, and it comes from protein analysis rather than DNA, which rarely survives at these ages.
