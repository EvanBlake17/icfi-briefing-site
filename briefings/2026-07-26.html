US pauses Iran bombing after 13 nights as White House weighs “major” escalation

For the first time in two weeks, the US military announced no new strikes on Iran on Friday and Saturday, following 13 consecutive nights of bombardment. The pause came after a White House meeting at which Vice President JD Vance and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine reportedly raised concerns about further escalation. Trump told reporters Friday that he thinks Iran would “love to make a deal” but that he does not “think they’re ready to,” while separately telling Axios he was weighing “a massive attack, bigger than ever before.”

Andre Damon reported on the WSWS that over 1,000 sites have been struck in the two-week escalation, on top of roughly 13,000 sites hit during the opening campaign of February 28 to April 8, and that a former Trump envoy is now publicly demanding a ground invasion of Iran.

Key facts:

  • Iran’s health ministry reports 59 killed and 666 wounded since the war resumed in late June. A missile strike on Ahvaz killed four and injured five.
  • The Pentagon revised its official US war death toll down from 18 to 14 and its wounded count from 482 to 462, attributing the change to “a temporary data disruption” (spokesman Sean Parnell). Retired Maj. Gen. Paul Eaton called it “statistical sleight of hand.”
  • Iran retaliated against targets in Bahrain, Kuwait, and a US base in Jordan.
  • Approximately 50,000 US troops are in the region alongside two carriers and nearly 20 warships in the Arabian Sea. B-2 and B-52 crews are on alert; F-16s have been moved from Germany and F-35s from Britain; more than 150 additional medics have been sent to Landstuhl.
  • US gasoline is near $4.10/gallon and diesel is up over 60 percent year-on-year.

Wildfires force more than 250,000 evacuations across France and Spain

Wildfires driven by a prolonged heatwave across the western Mediterranean have produced what French officials describe as the largest peacetime evacuation in the country’s history. Some 57,000 people were moved overnight in the Gironde, bringing that department’s total to 167,000 and the combined Gironde–Landes figure to 197,000. French Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said about 98,000 hectares have burned in France so far in 2026, a national record, with fires still uncontained in the Gironde, the Landes and the Var.

Key facts:

  • At least 15 civilians and three firefighters have been killed across the region as of July 25.
  • Two fires west of Madrid merged on July 24 into what the regional government calls the worst wildfire in the Madrid region’s recorded history.
  • Fires have burned across Spain, Portugal, France, Greece, Italy and Turkey; smoke has disrupted the Tour de France.
  • Scientists attribute the scale to a punishing sequence of heatwaves that has left Mediterranean soils tinder-dry — the same mechanism identified in recent European heat-mortality research.

The disaster is unfolding while European governments direct hundreds of billions into rearmament. Firefighting capacity, forest management and rural emergency services across southern Europe have been degraded by successive rounds of austerity.

ICC member states vote to remove chief prosecutor Karim Khan

The Assembly of States Parties of the International Criminal Court voted on July 24 to remove chief prosecutor Karim Khan over findings of “serious misconduct and serious breach of duty” — the first removal of a sitting ICC prosecutor in the court’s history. Khan, who issued the arrest warrants against Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant, had been on leave since May 2025 over allegations of sexual misconduct with a female aide, which he denies.

Key facts:

  • The vote was 82 in favor of removal, 13 against, 15 abstentions, out of 125 member states.
  • Khan’s chief lawyer said the decision was “unsupported by evidence,” reached through a process in which Khan was “never given a fair opportunity to be heard,” and “in defiance of the conclusions of independent judges.”
  • The Trump administration has sanctioned ICC prosecutors and officials and has stated its intention to “dismantle the ICC altogether.”

Whatever the merits of the misconduct findings, the removal decapitates the office that issued the Netanyahu warrants at precisely the moment when the imperialist powers are demanding the warrants be shelved. The episode underscores the impotence of international juridical bodies against the interests of the major powers — the central point of Joseph Kishore’s WSWS analysis of Mamdani’s retreat on the same question (see WSWS coverage below).

Israel intensifies Gaza strikes and expands barrier as death toll passes 73,000

Israeli forces stepped up strikes across Gaza this week and continued construction of a physical barrier dividing the territory, notwithstanding the US-brokered ceasefire that took effect in October 2025. Strikes Thursday killed at least two Palestinians in the Bureij and Jabalia refugee camps and in Gaza City’s Zeitoun and Tal al-Hawa neighborhoods — all areas for which evacuation warnings had just been issued. On Saturday, an Israeli airstrike reportedly killed a Palestinian medic in the Nuseirat refugee camp in central Gaza, along with Abdel-Nasser Al-Maqadma, head of the Hamas police force in North Gaza governorate.

Key facts:

  • Total Palestinian deaths since October 2023 stand at at least 73,311, including more than 1,185 killed since the ceasefire began.
  • WFP director of emergencies Ross Smith: “It is still a critical, desperate humanitarian situation.” An estimated 1.4 million people — 67 percent of Gaza’s population — face crisis-level food insecurity.
  • Al Jazeera correspondent Ibrahim al-Khalili: “What is particularly alarming is that these warnings have been followed by attacks on the very areas where people have been told to flee.”
  • The UK Foreign Office demanded Israel lift humanitarian restrictions; Egypt pressed for faster deployment of the International Stabilization Force.

Strait of Hormuz crisis strangles global oil and LNG shipping

Traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has collapsed. Roughly 20 percent of the world’s seaborne oil and one-third of its LNG normally transits the 21-mile chokepoint; on recent days as few as four tankers made the passage, against 125–140 before the war. Iranian missiles have struck the tankers Al Bahyah and Mombasa B and the Qatari-flagged LNG carrier Al Rekayyat, and multiple Greek-owned Dynacom tankers were hit on July 19–20.

Key facts:

  • Brent crude broke $100/barrel for the first time since May before easing to around $95–96 on speculation about US-Iran talks.
  • Vessels rerouting around Africa are adding up to two weeks to delivery times, raising freight and insurance costs worldwide.
  • The World Bank has warned global growth could fall to 1.3 percent from 2.9 percent if the disruption persists.
  • The FAO warns the conflict will deepen global hunger through energy and fertilizer prices (see International developments).
  • JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon said investors are “underestimating the risks” from Ukraine, the Middle East and US-China tensions.

US measles cases hit 35-year high

The CDC recorded 2,318 confirmed measles cases in the United States as of July 23, surpassing the 2,289 cases recorded in all of 2025 with more than five months of the year remaining. It is the highest annual count since 1991 and the second consecutive record-breaking year since elimination was declared in 2000.

Key facts:

  • 45 jurisdictions have reported cases. 35 new outbreaks began in 2026, and 93 percent of confirmed cases (2,153 of 2,318) are outbreak-associated — 782 from 2026 outbreaks and 1,371 from outbreaks that began in 2025.
  • 93 percent of those infected are unvaccinated; the overwhelming majority are under 20.
  • No deaths have been recorded so far in 2026.
  • Officials identify summer travel as a high-risk transmission window and warn the count will continue climbing.

The resurgence follows the dismantling of federal vaccine advisory structures, the gutting of local public health budgets, and the promotion of anti-vaccine positions from within the Department of Health and Human Services itself.

Chip selloff hammers Asian markets

Trump’s replacement tariffs take effect on 60 trading partners

Replacement duties of 10 to 12.5 percent took effect Friday on 60 trading partners, invoked under Section 301 “forced labor” authority after the courts struck down the previous tariff mechanism. Canada, Mexico, India and the UK face 10 percent; the EU, Taiwan, Brazil, China, Japan and South Korea 12.5 percent. Brazil’s Lula called the move “arbitrary and unjustified”; most governments signaled continued negotiation rather than retaliation.

Separately, Trump signed three Section 338 proclamations imposing 50 percent tariffs on Canadian goods — including wine, cement and hockey sticks — effective in roughly 30 days, around August 20. Energy products, potash, fish and critical minerals are exempt; goods previously protected under USMCA are not. CNN Business described it as “a warning to the rest of the world.”

Ebola outbreak in DR Congo becomes fastest-spreading on record

The Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has passed 1,000 deaths faster than any previous Ebola epidemic. Ministry of Health figures as of July 21 record 2,536 confirmed cases, 1,033 confirmed deaths and 506 recoveries — a case fatality rate of 40.7 percent among confirmed cases. The 1,000-death threshold was crossed in 67 days; the 2014–16 West Africa epidemic took 142.

  • Ituri province accounts for nearly 90 percent of confirmed cases and more than 80 percent of deaths. Cases have also been reported in North Kivu, South Kivu, Haut-Uele and Tshopo, plus 20 cases and two deaths in Uganda and one case in France.
  • Contact tracing reaches 82 percent of identified contacts, short of the WHO’s 90–95 percent target; Africa CDC reports that fewer than 9 percent of contacts that should be traced are actually monitored. WHO modeling suggests true case numbers are two to four times the confirmed count.
  • The joint WHO–Africa CDC $518 million response plan faces a funding shortfall of over $400 million.
  • Ituri has been in armed conflict for more than 30 years; over 900,000 people there live in displacement camps.
  • Africa CDC director-general Jean Kaseya: “People are dying, because there are no vaccines, no medicine and no funding.”

Benjamin Mateus notes on the WSWS that US biodefense funding has prioritized Zaire ebolavirus countermeasures over Bundibugyo, leaving no licensed vaccine or therapeutic for the strain now circulating — a direct consequence of organizing pharmaceutical research around national-security priorities rather than human need.

Largest summer COVID wave in years as Nimbus variant dominates

CDC wastewater surveillance shows national COVID-19 viral activity at “moderate,” up from “low” the previous week, with viral levels the highest recorded for a summer wave since July 2022. The NB.1.8.1 subvariant, nicknamed “Nimbus,” is now dominant, accounting for an estimated 43 percent of sequenced cases.

  • National test positivity stood at 4.8 percent as of July 12, up 1.1 points week-on-week, with emergency department visits rising across all age groups.
  • Wastewater signals are highest in the South and West; California, Florida, Texas, Alabama, Louisiana and Hawaii show high or very high activity. Cases are growing in 27 states.
  • Wastewater typically leads clinical case counts by one to two weeks, meaning the reported rise understates current transmission.

The surge is proceeding with no mitigation measures whatsoever, minimal public communication, and a federal vaccine apparatus that has been deliberately dismantled — the institutionalization of the “forever COVID” policy the WSWS has documented since 2020.

Roundup

  • H5N1: Highly pathogenic avian influenza clade 2.3.4.4b is circulating in wild birds, poultry and an expanding list of mammal species across six continents, with Australia confirming its first-ever detections in June. More than 180 million US poultry birds have been affected since the outbreak began, alongside over 1,000 dairy herds. 71 human cases were identified in the US from 2024 through May 2026. Between August 2025 and June 2026, Bangladesh, Cambodia and India reported 12 human infections with three deaths. No person-to-person transmission has been identified.
  • Climate: Scientific American reports on new work quantifying how repeated European heatwaves are simultaneously killing thousands and desiccating soils, producing the compound drought-fire conditions now driving the evacuations in France and Spain. Soil moisture deficits are becoming self-reinforcing across the Mediterranean basin.
  • Public health infrastructure: The fourth rural US hospital closure of 2026 — Sturgis Hospital in Michigan — followed 23 closures in 2025 and 25 in 2024. Roughly 700 additional rural hospitals are considered at risk, 300 in immediate danger (details in WSWS coverage).
  • Research access: A 2025 study counted 71 Tyrannosaurus rex specimens in private hands against 61 in public collections — a ratio with direct consequences for what can be studied (see Arts and culture).

Nolan’s The Odyssey opens to record numbers and divided criticism

Christopher Nolan’s adaptation of Homer, released July 17, has taken more than $264 million globally in its opening weekend, surpassing Oppenheimer. Made for $250 million — the most expensive production of Nolan’s career — and shot entirely on IMAX 70mm cameras, it stars Matt Damon as Odysseus and Anne Hathaway as Penelope, with Tom Holland, Robert Pattinson, Lupita Nyong’o, Zendaya and Charlize Theron. Nolan has said his next film is “at least” three years away.

That a studio committed a quarter of a billion dollars to a version of an ancient epic about a soldier’s ruinous decade-long return from a war of conquest is itself significant. The Homeric material is saturated with the human costs of war, the destruction of households, and the moral corrosion of the returning warrior — themes with obvious contemporary resonance. Whether Nolan’s treatment engages them or converts them into spectacle is the substance of the critical division. Writing in Jacobin, Eileen Jones argues the film drains classical mythology of its vitality, reducing the poem to technically overwhelming but emotionally inert set-pieces. The scale of the commercial success — in a period of collapsing theatrical attendance — also indicates a real audience appetite for serious material treated seriously, against the industry’s own franchise-driven assumptions.

A T. rex sells for $50.1 million as fossils become financial assets

A 12.5-foot Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton nicknamed “Gus,” recovered in South Dakota from 2021 by commercial collector Thomas Heitkamp, sold at Sotheby’s New York for $50.1 million to an anonymous buyer — a record for any paleontological specimen. Seven bidders competed. The previous record was $44.6 million for a stegosaurus in 2024; “Stan,” another T. rex, went for $31.8 million in 2020 and now resides in Abu Dhabi. The last time a major specimen went to a public institution was 1997, when the Field Museum acquired “Sue” for $8.36 million.

The Society of Vertebrate Paleontology warned that private ownership forecloses research: “Modern research techniques are still revealing new information from fossils collected more than a century ago.” Philip Guelpa’s WSWS review draws the connection between the transformation of irreplaceable scientific material into a speculative asset class for the ultra-rich and the simultaneous starvation of publicly funded research.

Execution in Wisconsin: Only the working class can stop police-state terror — Jacob Crosse

Corey Ruiz, 38, of Madison, Wisconsin, was shot and killed by police for allegedly looking into parked cars. He is one of five workers killed by police, ICE agents or National Guard troops between July 5 and 13. None of the officers involved has been arrested; most have not been publicly identified.

The Perspective’s central data point: police in the US had killed at least 689 people by July 4, 2026, on pace to exceed 2025’s total of 1,318. “On average, American police kill more than three people every day. The perpetrators are routinely placed on paid leave, shielded from public identification.”

The political argument is directed against the entire framework of police reform advanced by the Democratic Party and its periphery — body cameras, independent investigations, consent decrees — which has coexisted with a steadily rising kill rate. Crosse locates the growth of state violence in the social polarization of American capitalism: billionaire fortunes expanding while tens of millions depend on government assistance. “The police exist to defend capitalist property and the wealth and power of the financial oligarchy against the working class.” The conclusion is that the abolition of police-state violence and of ICE requires the independent political organization of the working class across racial lines on a socialist program — not the election of reform-minded prosecutors or mayors.

US bombards Iran for 13th straight night as White House plans “major” escalation — Andre Damon

Beyond the facts reported above, Damon assembles the evidence that the pause is preparation rather than de-escalation, and treats the Pentagon’s downward revision of its own casualty figures as a deliberate operation to conceal the cost of the war from the American population. The programmatic conclusion is that the war cannot be stopped through Congress or the Democratic Party, both of which have funded every phase of it, but only through the construction of an international anti-war movement based on the working class, directed against the capitalist system that produces war.

Jacobin falsifies Trotsky and historical record to promote popular front in Argentina — Andrea Lobo

Lobo replies to Martín Mosquera’s “How to Defeat Milei” in Jacobin América Latina, which urges Argentine workers into a broad “progressive bloc” with Peronism ahead of the 2027 elections. Mosquera claims Trotsky showed “flexibility” toward anti-fascist alliances and committed “sectarian errors” in opposing the Popular Fronts in France and Spain. Lobo calls this “a grotesque fabrication,” restating the distinction Trotsky insisted upon: the united front of workers’ organizations in struggle — “March separately, but strike together!” — versus the Popular Front, which subordinates the working class politically to bourgeois parties.

On the historical record, Lobo argues the French Popular Front did not radicalize the June 1936 upsurge but “shut that upsurge down,” with Communist Party leaders ordering strikers back into the factories in exchange for concessions “the bourgeoisie clawed back within two years.” Against Mosquera’s premise of an Argentine “social defeat,” she cites four general strikes, mass pension protests and plant occupations. The conclusion identifies the convergence of Jacobin and the PTS in obstructing the independent political organization of workers, and traces it to the social interests of a privileged middle-class layer concerned with managing capitalism rather than overthrowing it.

Mamdani, Netanyahu and the capitalist state — Joseph Kishore

On July 21 New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani released a video calling Netanyahu “a war criminal” and pledging his arrest should he attend the UN General Assembly in September. The video drew more than 13 million views within a day. Mamdani then reversed himself, stating he lacked “independent legal authority” and calling on the federal government to enforce the ICC warrant — an appeal directed at the same administration that has sanctioned ICC prosecutors.

Kishore’s argument turns on the class character of the state. Mamdani, he writes, proceeded as though “the state is not a neutral body but an instrument of class rule” were not the case — appealing to institutions constituted to defend the interests of the ruling class against precisely such measures. He notes that Netanyahu’s security in New York would be the responsibility of Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch, a billionaire’s heir and open partisan of the Israeli state whom Mamdani retained, and that Mamdani endorsed Governor Hochul despite her record against labor. The programmatic conclusion: “The capitalist state, which exists to uphold the interests of the ruling class, must be replaced with a workers’ state.” Individual prosecutions cannot address an imperialist system that produces genocide.

Canadian imperialism complicit in Trump’s regime change operation in Cuba — James Clayton

Clayton documents the silence of Mark Carney’s Liberal government on Trump’s threats to invade Cuba, and roots it in Canadian capital’s own interests. Cuba is Canada’s largest Caribbean trading partner at roughly $1 billion annually. Canadian tourism operators Sunwing and Blue Diamond Resorts have invested billions on the island; the mining company Sherritt holds major nickel and cobalt operations and was owed $344 million (USD) before operations halted. In May 2026 Trump confidant Ray Washburne acquired a majority stake in Sherritt.

Ottawa’s total humanitarian response has been $8 million in food aid channelled through NGOs, followed by silence on the blockade — because Carney hopes “to facilitate the conclusion of an agreement” for US market access under conditions in which Trump is imposing tariffs on Canada. Clayton also notes the involvement of Raúl Guillermo Rodríguez Castro, grandson of Raúl Castro, in negotiations over property restitution for exiled Cubans — evidence of the Cuban bureaucracy’s own preparation for capitalist restoration. The article situates Canada’s “Fortress North America” orientation as subordination to US imperialism in exchange for a share of the spoils.

Pattern bargaining opens for 23,000 US Steel and Cleveland-Cliffs workers — Samuel Davidson

Contracts covering roughly 23,000 USW members expire September 1: about 11,000 at US Steel and 12,000 across 13 Cleveland-Cliffs facilities, with a further 2,000 Cliffs miners bargaining separately. US Steel is offering 4 percent in year one, 3 percent in years two and three and 4 percent in year five — 18.2 percent compounded through 2031, with a $4,000 ratification bonus and no cost-of-living adjustment. On healthcare the company seeks “plan design changes” and “potential increased cost sharing,” with a joint committee to find savings whenever annual costs grow more than 3 percent.

Davidson sets this against the recently concluded four-year oil refinery pattern agreement — 4 percent in years one and four, 3.5 percent in years two and three, again with no COLA — as the template the USW intends to apply. His assessment: “the USW bureaucracy is not preparing a fight based on what workers and their families need,” but bargaining within limits set by management’s unchallenged control over investment, plant closures and production. The article calls for rank-and-file committees to formulate demands independently and to link steelworkers across US Steel, Cliffs and Nippon-owned operations internationally.

Southern California grocery pharmacists authorize strike — Marc Wells and Tom Hall

Nearly 800 pharmacists across seven UFCW locals at Ralphs, Albertsons, Vons and Pavilions in Southern and Central California voted July 15–16 to authorize strike action. No date has been set; negotiations resume August 6–7. Pharmacists are demanding a $9.95 increase over two years retroactive to March 2026, contractual enforcement of California’s pharmacy staffing law, a $2/hour premium for specialty clinical services, and improved pension and vacation provisions. The employers have offered $5 over three years with no retroactivity.

The dispute centres on patient safety as much as pay. Pharmacists now handle prescriptions, immunizations and insurance administration with staffing levels that a UFCW survey found produced medication errors reported by 11.9 percent of respondents. Vons pharmacist Craig Chally: “The current workload is not sustainable. Safe staffing protects patients.” The article notes the UFCW’s practice of authorizing strikes and then allowing deadlines to pass, and the deliberate separation of the pharmacists from the far larger grocery workforce in the same chains.

Sturgis Hospital closes in rural Michigan — Riley Ward

Sturgis Hospital, serving a town of 11,000, closed June 19 after more than a century of operation with less than three days’ notice. Three hundred workers lost their jobs and health insurance simultaneously with the public announcement. A nurse with 38 years’ service: “I honestly thought that I’d wake up and this was a bad dream.”

The financial trajectory: 2024 revenue of $15.4 million against $23.2 million in expenses, an $8 million operating deficit, and negative net assets of $48 million. The hospital had already cut 140 positions over the preceding decade, shuttered birthing and hospice programs in 2018, and closed all 84 inpatient beds in 2023 before terminating emergency services. Residents now face 20–30 minute drives, with the nearest facility out-of-network for most Michigan insurance plans. Ward frames the closure as the local expression of a national policy: rural healthcare, unprofitable by construction, is being liquidated while Medicaid is cut and military spending expands.

5 workers killed in helmet factory fire in Sri Lanka — Wasantha Wijesiri and Dehin Wasantha

Five workers died in a fire at the Nimato helmet factory in the Katuwana Industrial Zone, 25 km southeast of Colombo, on July 21. The blaze began around 9:45 a.m. and engulfed the three-storey building within minutes. Four young workers from rural agricultural districts — two aged 19, two aged 24 — died along with 54-year-old production supervisor Mario Edward. Bodies required DNA identification.

The factory stored PVC and polystyrene foam without containment, had failed to renew its Environmental Protection Licence after 2024, and had no emergency response plan. The Central Environmental Authority had instructed management to remedy violations as recently as July 2; nothing was done. The nearest fire brigade was 15 km away and took nearly an hour to arrive; the industrial zone, employing some 2,000 workers, has no fire station. The authors locate the deaths in the systematic dismantling of inspection: 25 qualified engineers are responsible for nearly 30,000 factories nationwide, under an IMF austerity program that treats regulation as a cost to be eliminated.

Break the collusion between the union and management at Volkswagen! — VW Action Committee

The statement documents the escalation of announced job cuts at Volkswagen from 35,000 to 50,000 and then 100,000 positions. Its argument is directed at IG Metall’s structural position: the union holds majority labour representation on the supervisory board and, under the VW Act’s two-thirds requirement, must approve plant closures — meaning restructuring proceeds only with its consent. “IG Metall does not merely describe itself as a ‘co-manager’ but acts as an integral part of management.” The Committee draws the balance sheet of the 35,000 cuts IG Metall accepted 18 months ago: concessions did not avert further attacks but invited them. It calls for independent action committees at every plant and international coordination with VW workers in Mexico, Brazil, China and the United States.

Workers denounce threat against Will Lehman — WSWS reporters and rank-and-file groups
