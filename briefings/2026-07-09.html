1. Trump tears up Iran truce and reopens war

President Trump declared the 60-day US-Iran truce reached June 17 to be dead, telling reporters alongside NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, “As far as I’m concerned, it’s over,” and warning the US would “very probably” attack Iran “hard again tonight.” US Central Command said American forces struck roughly 90 targets across southern Iran to “degrade” Iran’s ability to threaten navigation in the Strait of Hormuz; Iranian authorities reported eight military personnel killed. Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps answered with drone and missile fire at US targets, and Iran struck US bases in Kuwait and Bahrain, warning other Gulf states against facilitating American aggression.

The immediate pretext was an attack on three commercial tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday, which Washington and Gulf governments blamed on Tehran without direct Iranian claim of responsibility. The deeper trigger, as the WSWS Perspective by Keith Jones details, was the US cancellation of an oil-sanctions waiver Tuesday, followed within hours by air strikes on more than 80 targets. Trump threatened to reimpose a naval blockade and to “take over” Kharg Island, Iran’s main oil export terminal, and boasted of having ordered the strike that killed Supreme Leader Ayatollah Khamenei at the war’s outset, calling Iran’s leaders “scum.”

Key facts: - Trump: “I don’t want to deal with them anymore… as far as I’m concerned, it’s over.” - The truce was signed with Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian after the US failed to achieve regime change or eliminate Iran’s nuclear and missile programs. - Since July 4, millions have joined six days of funeral observances for Khamenei and family members, including his 14-month-old granddaughter killed in the February 28 decapitation strike. - Democratic criticism came from the right: Senator Adam Schiff called the earlier truce “a thorough capitulation”; Senator Chris Murphy termed it “essentially a surrender to Iran.”

As Keith Jones writes on the WSWS, the imperialist powers are “dragging humanity to the abyss,” and only “the revolutionary mobilization of the international working class” offers a way out.

2. NATO summit in Ankara enshrines 5 percent of GDP military target

Heads of state and government of NATO’s 32 members concluded a summit in Ankara, Turkey, dominated by pressure to implement the 5 percent of GDP defense-and-security spending pledge agreed at last year’s Hague summit, to be met by 2035 (3.5 percent core defense, 1.5 percent defense-related). Secretary General Rutte announced that European allies and Canada are already investing around 4 percent of GDP, with some allies set to reach 5 percent in 2026, far ahead of schedule. The summit unfolded even as the US resumed bombing Iran near the Strait of Hormuz.

Key facts: - Rutte’s three stated priorities: increased defense investment, transatlantic defense-industrial production, and support for Ukraine. - As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, the summit mobilized some $217 billion for rearmament while Turkey detained 225 dissidents ahead of the gathering. - On the sidelines, Trump praised Erdoğan, announced plans to lift CAATSA sanctions and reconsider Turkey’s F-35 exclusion, and advanced a $700 million-plus F110 engine sale — over Netanyahu’s objections. - Trump simultaneously demanded Denmark cede Greenland, floated a US-Russia deal over Ukraine at NATO allies’ expense, and threatened to cut trade with Spain.

3. Israel continues Gaza onslaught after Hamas dissolves its government

Israeli strikes killed as many as 16 Palestinians, including a child, in the two days after Hamas announced it had dissolved its government in Gaza to transfer power to a technical committee backed by the United Nations. The Cairo-based committee of technocrats, chaired by Gaza-born engineer and former Palestinian Authority official Ali Shaath, is mandated to restore essential services under UN and “Board of Peace” supervision. The Israeli military said it targeted a Hamas operative in Gaza City and an Islamic Jihad militant in Khan Younis.

Key facts: - Total casualties since the ceasefire signed nine months ago have reached 1,084 killed, per figures cited by the WSWS. - Hamas spokesman Hazem Qassem called the handover “a positive step forward”; Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Saar dismissed it, insisting “any civilian government will of course operate as Hamas dictates.” - Netanyahu vowed Israeli forces would “remain in southern Lebanon, Syria, and Gaza for as long as required.” - Negotiations remain deadlocked over phase-two disarmament of Hamas and reconstruction.

4. Pentagon deploys ~2,000 troops to earthquake-stricken Venezuela

Nearly 2,000 US military personnel have been deployed under a State Department-led relief operation following the June 24 twin earthquakes (magnitudes 7.2 and 7.5) that devastated Venezuela’s Yaracuy state. US Southern Command directed the amphibious transport ship USS Fort Lauderdale, the littoral combat ship USS Billings, and C-17 and C-130 transport aircraft, operating from logistics hubs in Puerto Rico and Curaçao. Washington pledged $150 million in aid — $100 million to a UN humanitarian fund and $50 million to aid organizations.

Key facts: - The official death toll exceeds 3,300, with more than 16,000 injured and roughly 31,000 reported missing as of early July. - As Andrea Lobo argued on the WSWS, polling shows 71.2 percent of Venezuelans believe Trump cares more about the country’s oil than about relief, framing the deployment as regime-change positioning. - The intervention establishes a US military foothold in a country Washington has long targeted for its oil reserves.

5. Keiko Fujimori declared president of Peru

Peru’s electoral authorities declared conservative Keiko Fujimori, eldest daughter of imprisoned former dictator Alberto Fujimori, the winner of the June 7 runoff by a margin of 50.135 percent to 49.865 percent over leftist Senator Roberto Sánchez — a difference of roughly 50,000 votes out of 18 million cast. Fujimori, 51, becomes Peru’s first elected woman president and will be sworn in July 28 for a five-year term. It was her fourth bid for the presidency after defeats in 2011, 2016, and 2021.

Key facts: - Peru has cycled through nine presidents in ten years amid chronic institutional crisis. - As Francis Portocarrero and Andrea Lobo argue on the WSWS, the installation reflects US intervention to counter Chinese influence — China accounts for 36 percent of Peru’s trade versus the US’s 14 percent — centered on the Chinese-backed Chancay megaport. - The WSWS traces Fujimorism to Alberto Fujimori’s dictatorship (Montesinos-era corruption, prison massacres) and the 2022–23 crackdown that killed some 70 protesters after Pedro Castillo’s ouster. - Peru’s poverty rate stands at roughly 30 percent.

6. Largest healthcare strike in Massachusetts history hits Mass General Brigham

Approximately 4,500 nurses and clinicians — more than 4,000 registered nurses at Brigham and Women’s Hospital and about 450 clinicians at Mass General Brigham Home Care — walked off the job July 8 in the largest nurses’ strike in Massachusetts history, following a 2,798–12 (99.6 percent) strike authorization vote. The Massachusetts Nurses Association says the hospital proposed zero-percent cost-of-living raises. Mass General Brigham hired 1,300 traveling nurses and, citing a five-day minimum contract for replacements, locked out the striking nurses through July 13; home-care clinicians began a seven-day strike running to July 15.

Key facts: - As Kate Randall reported on the WSWS, MGB posted $2.4 billion in net income while its CEO drew $8.4 million in pay. - Union demands center on competitive wages, affordable health insurance, permanent-workforce investment, and patient-care protections. - State House talks were convened amid the walkout without resolution.

33,000 Mercedes-Benz workers protest across Germany

More than 33,000 workers demonstrated outside Mercedes-Benz plants across Germany on Friday, according to IG Metall (the company counted almost 16,000 across six sites), opposing a cost-cutting drive that would raise the standard week from 35 to 40 hours with no additional pay — roughly 260 extra unpaid hours per year. Around 90,000 of the company’s ~108,000 German employees will not receive a collectively agreed special payment worth 18.4 percent of a monthly wage in July; it is deferred to next year. The protests are expected to launch a wider wave across Germany’s crisis-hit auto industry.

As K. Nesan noted on the WSWS, profit-sharing has collapsed from €7,300 (2023) to €3,139 (2025) even as Mercedes paid €3.3 billion in dividends and an €8.8 million CEO payout; the works council is complicit in imposing austerity. The company says German “structural costs — particularly labour costs — are not competitive by international standards.”

Sri Lanka Negombo prison riot leaves 27 dead

Clashes at the Negombo prison, about 35 km north of Colombo, left 27 dead — including seven prison officials and 19 inmates — and more than 100 wounded, in unrest that began Sunday and turned deadly Monday when inmates attacked intervening guards and tried to break the main gate. A further 77 people remained hospitalized. Authorities linked the violence to narcotics and gang rivalries, while prison welfare groups pointed to catastrophic overcrowding: the facility, built for roughly 650, held some 2,600 prisoners. As Wasantha Rupasinghe reported on the WSWS, Sri Lanka’s prisons run at 3.6 times capacity nationally, and the riot was reportedly triggered by the exposure of a drug-trafficking network.

Italy’s summer of strikes against Meloni’s austerity

A wave of strikes across Italian aviation, rail, logistics, maritime, and public sectors is demanding a €2,000 monthly minimum wage, the restoration of wage indexing (the scala mobile), and a windfall tax on energy and banking corporations, while opposing surging military spending under the Meloni government. Rank-and-file union USB is concentrating stoppages into July before a legal summer strike ban takes effect. As Marc Wells reported on the WSWS, the strikes coincide with Law No. 80/2025, which imposes up to two years’ imprisonment for group roadblocks — the broadest postwar expansion of anti-strike penalties. The movement’s openly political character underscores the convergence of austerity, militarism, and authoritarian state repression across Europe.

Chile moves to militarize cities after child’s killing

The killing of 12-year-old Alejandro Águila in a carjacking has fueled a drive to lower the age of criminal responsibility, with Chile’s Congress voting 77–40 in favor and cross-party calls — including from Socialist Party figures — for military deployment in Santiago. The push comes amid a documented surge in violence: child homicides in Chile roughly doubled between 2018 and 2024. As Mauricio Saavedra argued on the WSWS, the tragedy is being exploited to militarize the country despite President José Antonio Kast’s own low approval on crime, part of a broader Latin American turn toward “law and order” authoritarianism.

Markets whipsawed by Iran war

Global markets were dominated by the collapse of the US-Iran truce. Oil surged as CENTCOM struck Iranian targets near the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world’s oil transits, while equities sold off on fears of a wider war and a renewed inflationary shock. The class content of the day’s data is stark: oil-and-defense-linked capital stands to profit from the very war that threatens working people with higher fuel and food prices and a fresh cost-of-living squeeze, even as governments across NATO redirect hundreds of billions from social spending toward armaments.

ICE officer kills Mexican construction worker in Houston

An ICE officer fatally shot Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, a Mexican immigrant construction worker, during an attempted vehicle stop in east Houston around 6:50 a.m. on July 7. DHS claims Araujo “weaponized his vehicle in an attempt to run over” the officer; his family says he was driving to pick up coworkers and would have complied had he recognized the unmarked vehicles as law enforcement. The DHS Office of Inspector General is investigating, and civil-rights groups say social-media footage does not support ICE’s account.

As Trévon Austin reported on the WSWS, this is one of at least 10 immigration-agent shootings in 2026 tracked by The Trace, on pace to exceed 2025’s total of 15 — reflecting the violent expansion of the deportation machine under conditions of a broader authoritarian offensive.

H5N1 avian influenza reaches Australia

Australia has confirmed its first detections of H5 high-pathogenicity avian influenza, with seven confirmed or presumed cases in wild birds as of 9 a.m. AEST July 8 — five in Western Australia, one in South Australia, and one in New South Wales. The first was a migratory brown skua found in Cape Le Grand National Park; the NSW case was a giant petrel near Hawks Nest. Officials report no mass mortality, no poultry infection, and low human risk to date. Australia was the last continent without local transmission of the panzootic strain.

As Gary Alvernia reported on the WSWS, more than 280 million birds have been culled worldwide and at least 10 human deaths recorded since 2021. The WSWS criticizes Australia’s $113 million response as underfunded — only $22 million allocated for vaccination and PPE — reflecting the same neglect of public-health infrastructure that has characterized the ruling-class response to COVID-19.

mRNA vaccine and gene-editing advances

  • Nipah vaccine: A phase 1 study in Nature Medicine found that an mRNA vaccine (mRNA-1215) encoding the Nipah virus Malaysian-strain protein was safe and induced elevated immune responses at one year — a notable step against a pathogen whose case-fatality rate can exceed 70 percent.
  • In-vivo base editing: A phase 1 trial in six patients reported that lipid-nanoparticle base editing targeting PCSK9 in liver cells reduced LDL cholesterol without serious adverse events, advancing one-time genetic therapies for cardiovascular disease.
  • Antimicrobial resistance: An evaluation across 193 countries for the 2026 Global Action Plan update found that states with strong multisectoral governance achieved measurable reductions in antimicrobial resistance — underscoring that AMR is fundamentally a question of public investment and coordination.
  • Infrastructure and disease: The Sri Lankan prison catastrophe and Michigan carbon-monoxide deaths (above) are, in the final analysis, public-health failures rooted in overcrowding and grid neglect — reminders that “science” questions are inseparable from the allocation of social resources.

Arts and culture

The most pointed cultural intervention of the past day came from the WSWS, where David Walsh dissected the New York Times’ feature ranking the “definitive” movies about America. Walsh’s critique goes beyond quibbling over a list: he argues the panel’s omission of works like Citizen Kane and The Grapes of Wrath — films that confront class, poverty, and the concentration of wealth and power — in favor of more fashionable choices reflects the Times’ abandonment of any class-based, historical understanding of American life. The exercise, he contends, substitutes identity-inflected and market-driven criteria for a serious reckoning with the social truths great cinema has captured. It is a useful illustration of how a leading organ of upper-middle-class opinion approaches culture: as a curation of taste divorced from the objective realities of American society.

Against this backdrop, the summer’s release calendar leans heavily on nostalgia and the recycling of the pop-cultural past rather than new work grappling with the present crisis:

  • Music documentaries: Sia: Nostalgic for the Present, a 75-minute concert film, gets a limited release July 23; Wham! 10 Days in China, the 1986 record of the duo’s George Michael–led tour, returns to theaters July 28. Both trade on the packaging of earlier decades — a symptom of an industry with diminishing confidence in the contemporary.
  • Theater revival: A new Death Note stage musical is being revived for a 2026 London run, part of the broader mining of established franchises for the West End.
  • Cultural losses of 2026: The year has already claimed Canadian vocalist David Clayton-Thomas of Blood, Sweat & Tears, whose brass-driven, socially conscious late-1960s hits reflected a period of ferment, and Alan Osmond of the Osmonds — figures whose careers spanned the commercialization of postwar popular music.

The WSWS framework insists that cultural production be read as a register of social contradictions. In a period defined by war, mass strikes, and the drive toward authoritarian rule, the retreat into nostalgia and franchise revival — and the Times’ aestheticized, class-blind canon-making — reveal an artistic establishment increasingly detached from the great questions confronting humanity. The genuine artistic achievements of earlier eras, whether the muckraking realism of The Grapes of Wrath or the formal daring of Citizen Kane, remain a standard against which the thinness of much current output is measured.

Assessment: Jacobin functions as the theoretical workshop for chaining the growing radicalization of workers and youth to the Democratic Party — the “graveyard of social movements.” Its silence on Gaza and Iran in this window is itself a political statement.
