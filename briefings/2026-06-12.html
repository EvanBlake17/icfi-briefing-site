1. Trump cancels Iran strikes, claims imminent “peace deal” as Iran stays cautious

President Trump announced Thursday that he had cancelled scheduled strikes on Iran, writing that he acted “based on discussions with the Islamic Republic of Iran brought to the highest level of Iranian leadership and approved.” Hours later he declared the war “ended” and claimed a memorandum of understanding was nearly finalized, with a peace signing possible “within days.” But the sequence was characteristically chaotic: that same Thursday morning Trump had threatened on Truth Social to invade Iran and seize Kharg Island, its main oil export terminal, before reversing course by early afternoon.

Tehran poured cold water on the claims. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said reports of a deal were “speculative” and that “nothing has been finalised,” though a separate MFA official said an MOU was “under consideration.” The US naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz remains in force pending any agreement. The US-Israel war on Iran began February 28, 2026 and is now past its 100th day. As Andre Damon noted in the WSWS Perspective, by CNN’s count Trump has declared a deal “imminent” at least 38 times since March — each followed by renewed bombing — a pattern Damon called “the mafia principle: Do as we demand, or we will murder you.”

Key facts: - Trump, listing eleven governments from Israel and Saudi Arabia to Egypt as parties to the supposed deal, conspicuously omitted Iran from the list - Brent crude fell roughly 1% to below $89.50/barrel on ceasefire hopes; global equities surged - Seymour Hersh reported Trump raised the possible use of low-yield nuclear weapons against Iran’s underground missile factories at a White House meeting; the Wall Street Journal editorial board likened his choice to the one George W. Bush faced over Iraq - Ken Klippenstein reported an April 7 order dispatched 82nd Airborne paratroopers to Israel under plans “completed since February, for seizing Kharg Island”

2. US strikes kill three Indian merchant sailors off Oman

US forces killed three Indian merchant sailors aboard the Palau-flagged MT Settebello on June 10, part of a four-day campaign of strikes on commercial vessels accused of carrying Iranian oil in violation of the US blockade. India confirmed the deaths of deck cadet Aditya Sharma, engine fitter Shivanand Chaurasiya and Chief Engineer Patnala Suresh, and summoned the US Embassy’s deputy chief of mission, Jason Meeks, to lodge “a strong protest.”

CENTCOM justified the strikes as enforcement of the naval blockade. Three ships were hit in four days: the MT Marivex (June 8, 24 Indian crew rescued by Oman), the MT Settebello (June 10, three killed), and the MT Jalveer (June 11, when a US aircraft fired two Hellfire missiles into the engine room, with all 20 Indian crew surviving). The strikes underscore how maritime workers from the Global South are bearing the human cost of the US war on Iran.

Key facts: - India’s Shipping Minister Sarbananda Sonowal called the deaths “deeply unfortunate”; New Delhi demanded “immediate de-escalation” - India described the incidents as “a direct result of the ongoing conflict in the region” - Indian nationals crew a large share of the world’s merchant fleet, placing them disproportionately in the blockade’s line of fire

Sources Al Jazeera India says US hit another ship off Oman, confirms 3 dead · Business Standard India lodges strong protest after US strikes kill 3 sailors

3. World Bank slashes global growth forecast to 2.5%, blames Iran war

The World Bank cut its 2026 global growth forecast to 2.5% — the lowest since the Covid pandemic and down from 2.9% in 2025 — attributing the downgrade directly to the energy shock from the Iran war. The bank warned of a worst-case scenario of just 1.3% growth if energy disruptions worsen and financial markets seize. Developing economies were marked down to 3.6% from 4.4% in 2025, with the Middle East/North Africa/Afghanistan/Pakistan region cut by 2.7 percentage points to just 1.6%.

The forecast lands the cost of the war squarely on workers and the poor, particularly in the worst-hit developing nations where food insecurity exceeds 61%. World Bank chief economist Indermit Gill cited slower population growth, falling private and public investment, rising debt and slower trade.

Key facts: - Brent crude is projected to average $94/barrel in 2026 — 36% higher than 2025 and 50% above January 2026 projections - Headline global inflation is running at roughly 4% - President Ajay Banga said the bank made $60 billion immediately available to developing nations, with potential for $100 billion over 15 months: “We are providing liquidity where it is needed now”

Sources Reuters World Bank lowers 2026 global growth forecast to 2.5% · Common Dreams Trump’s Iran war slowing global economic growth

4. SpaceX stages largest IPO in history as markets surge

SpaceX began trading on the Nasdaq under ticker SPCX on Friday in what is being described as the largest initial public offering in history. Shares were priced at $135, raising roughly $75 billion and implying a market capitalization near $1.78 trillion. The offering was reported to be multiple times oversubscribed. The debut coincided with a sharp global market rally driven by Trump’s claims of an Iran de-escalation — a convergence the WSWS flagged as the structural logic of US imperialism, with financial interests and military posturing moving in lockstep.

Key facts: - The IPO opened as Dow and S&P 500 futures rose ~0.5%, building on Thursday’s powerful rally (Dow +1.86%, Nasdaq +2.54%) - The Procure Space ETF (UFO) is up ~69% year-to-date, partly on the SpaceX “halo effect” - The offering concentrates enormous financial power in the hands of Elon Musk, whose dual role as government cost-cutter and defense contractor exemplifies the fusion of oligarchy and the state

5. Sudan’s civil war enters fourth year with 33.7 million needing aid

Sudan’s civil war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces entered its fourth year with an estimated 33.7 million people — roughly two-thirds of the population — requiring humanitarian assistance in 2026. The crisis remains the largest on Earth while receiving a fraction of the coverage devoted to the Iran war. Germany hosted an emergency international conference aiming to raise $1 billion toward a $3 billion 2026 crisis plan, even as the Hormuz closure disrupts the global aid shipping that Sudan depends on.

Key facts: - 14 million people have been internally displaced or fled as refugees, including 4.3 million across borders - 61.7% of the population is acutely food insecure; families report one meal per day or less, with some eating animal feed or boiling leaves - Over one-third of health facilities are non-functional; the health system is on the edge of collapse

6. Pope Leo XIV visits Canary Islands “Dock of Shame”

Pope Leo XIV, the first American pope and successor to Francis, made the first-ever papal visit to the Canary Islands on June 11, completing a trip Francis had planned before his death. He visited the Port of Arguineguín on Gran Canaria — known as the “Dock of Shame,” a primary entry point for migrants crossing from West Africa into Europe — and met with roughly 1,000 refugees and migrants. “Human dignity has no passport and does not lose its value when crossing a border,” Leo declared, adding: “We cannot grow accustomed to counting the dead.”

Key facts: - Earlier in his Spain tour, the Pope criticized Europe’s restrictive immigration policies before the Spanish Parliament and celebrated mass before 40,000 people - The visit comes amid a Europe-wide hardening against migration, as governments redirect spending toward military buildup

7. Supreme Court blocks Alabama nitrogen gas execution

The US Supreme Court ruled Thursday that Alabama cannot execute Jeffrey Lee using nitrogen hypoxia gas, upholding a lower court order that the method violates the Eighth Amendment’s ban on cruel and unusual punishment. Lee, convicted of a 1998 double murder during a pawnshop robbery, argued that nitrogen gas causes prolonged, painful suffocation and proposed a firing squad as an alternative. He had been scheduled to die July 11.

Key facts: - Justices Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito and Neil Gorsuch would have allowed the execution but issued no written dissent - Alabama has carried out eight nitrogen executions since the court approved the method in 2024 — seven of them in Alabama - Lee’s case — a poor defendant convicted of a robbery-murder — is emblematic of the class and racial dimensions of who faces execution in the US

Italy lifts defense spending to 2.8% of GDP ahead of NATO summit

Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni confirmed Italy will arrive at the July NATO summit having raised defense and security spending to 2.8% of GDP — up 0.71 points from 2025 — as the alliance pushes toward its newly adopted 5% target. Meloni argued headline figures are insufficient: “A tank that costs millions of euros can be destroyed by a drone that costs €20,000,” urging allies to prioritize drones, satellites and data infrastructure. Part of the increase reflects domestic security and policing reclassified under NATO accounting rules. Every percentage point redirected to NATO comes at the expense of healthcare, housing and pensions.

Germany’s Merz pairs rearmament abroad with austerity at home

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz delivered a roughly 20-minute Bundestag government statement on June 11 ahead of the June 18-19 European Council meeting, framing aerospace-sector expansion as economic success; the WSWS’s Johannes Stern identified it as a military-industrial buildup paired with social counterrevolution. Merz reaffirmed Germany’s commitment to Ukraine and sustained pressure on Russia, and signaled ambition to project German power into the Persian Gulf, while warning that several European states now spend more on debt interest than on defense — “We must not allow European budgets to reach such a condition.” Healthcare reforms, pension restructuring and budget consolidation concentrate the pain on workers; Stern warned of parallels to pre-war German militarism.

Chilean students protest education cuts and criminalization of youth

Thousands of students, teachers and activists clashed with police across Chile in renewed marches against President José Antonio Kast’s education cuts and the criminalization of youth, with the WSWS’s Mauricio Saavedra documenting how the Broad Front and Communist Party administered the same market-based education system they once opposed before Kast took office. Since assuming the presidency on March 11, the ultraconservative Kast has pledged to cut roughly $6 billion in public spending over 18 months, forcing nearly 3% cuts across all ministries — hitting school feeding, education-access and scholarship programs. The CAE student loan program ballooned enrollment from ~450,000 to 1.17 million by 2015; by 2025-26, roughly two-thirds of borrowers are in arrears and over 550,000 are in default, even as the Treasury’s aggressive new collection drive lifted recoveries some 295% in early 2026. Youth unemployment stands at 21.6% and underemployment of educated workers reaches 35.7%.

Pentagon shut down for hours over false anthrax alarm

The Pentagon was locked down and partially evacuated Thursday afternoon after a biosecurity sensor triggered an anthrax alert, sealing floors 2-5 in corridors 4-7. Arlington County Fire Department hazmat units responded alongside the Pentagon Force Protection Agency, with personnel in full chemical protective gear and gas masks. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell confirmed at 1:31 p.m. that “subsequent testing confirmed no hazard existed” and “normal operations have resumed.” The sensor system was found to be malfunctioning. The incident disrupted operations at the world’s largest office building for several hours.

Sources Washington Post Pentagon lockdown over hazardous materials investigation · CNN Pentagon lockdown and evacuation

Trump SNAP cuts drive millions off food assistance

The “One Big Beautiful Bill Act” is enacting a $187 billion assault on SNAP through 2034 and has already stripped at least 3.5 million people from food assistance between enactment and February 2026, as Kate Randall reported for the WSWS. Average benefits of roughly $6/day ($2/meal) are framed as deliberately inadequate. The measure expanded work requirements from ages 18-54 to 18-64 and narrowed the definition of a dependent child from under-18 to under-14, cutting off parents of teenagers. Before the cuts, 47.9 million Americans lived in food-insecure households; food insecurity affects 24.4% of Black households versus 10.1% of white households. The USDA terminated its annual Household Food Security Report in September 2025, eliminating the data infrastructure needed to track the damage.

Sources WSWS ‘Work or starve’: Trump’s SNAP cuts drive millions from food stamp rolls

UAW announces deal to end American Axle strike

After a 10-day strike by 1,000 workers in Three Rivers, Michigan, the UAW bureaucracy announced a tentative agreement with American Axle, as the WSWS’s Kevin Reed reported. The deal offers $30/hour by 2030 — up from $22/hour today, but below the $29/hour workers earned in 2008, which would equal roughly $45/hour in 2026 inflation-adjusted dollars. The rushed ratification timetable (Friday review, Saturday Q&A, Sunday vote) comes amid simultaneous contract rejections at Nexteer (rejected three times) and Dana (90%+ margins), signaling broader rank-and-file rebellion across the auto parts sector. The American Axle strike has drawn regional and trade labor press coverage.

Science, technology, and public health

  • US measles cases hit 2,030 across 40 jurisdictions — worst year in decades as kindergarten MMR coverage falls to 92.5%
  • H5N1 spreads in poultry across 12 states — over 100 million birds culled cumulatively; 71 US human cases since 2024
  • Three journals simultaneously expand finerenone to non-diabetic kidney disease — NEJM, JAMA and Lancet studies could add millions of eligible patients
  • AI policy clash — Anthropic’s CEO demands government blocking authority days after a deregulatory White House order

Measles and H5N1. The CDC confirmed 2,030 measles cases this year across 40 jurisdictions as of June 4, with 30 active outbreaks accounting for ~93% of cases — putting the US on pace for its worst measles year in decades. Kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.5% for the 2024-25 school year, below the 95% herd-immunity threshold. Separately, the USDA confirmed active H5N1 detections in commercial poultry across 12 states, triggering depopulations that have surpassed 100 million birds cumulatively this cycle. The CDC reports 71 total US human cases since February 2024; NIOSH has relaunched its dairy-worker safety campaign targeting roughly 500,000 agricultural workers at elevated occupational risk.

Finerenone studies. Three studies published simultaneously the week of June 5 in the New England Journal of Medicine, JAMA and The Lancet — presented at the 2026 European Renal Association Congress in Glasgow — show that finerenone, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist previously approved only for diabetic chronic kidney disease, significantly protects the hearts and kidneys of patients with non-diabetic CKD, including glomerular disease. It is the most concentrated simultaneous release of finerenone evidence to date and could expand the eligible patient population by millions globally.

Roundup: - AI regulation clash: In a June 10 essay, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei argued voluntary transparency is no longer sufficient and that governments should have standing legal authority — with revenue-based civil penalties — to block AI deployments that fail mandatory third-party safety testing. The call followed scrutiny of Anthropic’s “Mythos” model, initially found capable of exploiting critical software vulnerabilities. - White House AI order: A June executive order requires CISA to issue critical-infrastructure cybersecurity guidance within 30 days and directs Treasury, NSA and CISA to build a voluntary framework within 60 days to classify “covered frontier models” — but explicitly prohibits any mandatory government pre-clearance or licensing for AI releases, setting up a direct tension with Amodei’s proposal. - EU AI Act: On June 10 the European Commission published its Code of Practice for marking and labeling AI-generated content, ahead of binding enforcement in August 2026. - COVID-19: Transmission is declining or likely declining in 41 of 50 states. Ensemble models forecast ~1,000 new hospital admissions for the week ending June 13; COVID ED visits are at ~0.087% of all visits, down from 0.11% the prior week — a low summer baseline with no surge underway.

Arts and culture

The most significant cultural news of the day was the loss, confirmed earlier this month, of Marjane Satrapi, the Iranian-French cartoonist and filmmaker, who died June 4 at age 56. Satrapi won worldwide acclaim for Persepolis, her graphic-novel bildungsroman of growing up during and after the 1979 Iranian Revolution and the Iran-Iraq war, later adapted into an Oscar-nominated 2007 animated film. Her death lands with particular weight as the US-Israel war devastates the Iranian population: Persepolis remains one of the most humane and widely read accounts of how ordinary Iranians lived through revolution, theocratic repression and imperialist-fueled conflict — a corrective to the dehumanizing war propaganda now saturating Western media. Satrapi’s work insisted on the universality of the Iranian people’s experience against both the clerical regime and Western militarism.

In literature, novelist Dave Eggers discussed his new novel Contrapposto, a project he says has been on his mind for nearly two decades. Eggers’s fiction has long engaged questions of surveillance, technology and the alienating structures of corporate capitalism (The Circle), and the new work continues his interest in the social pressures bearing on the individual.

Roundup: - Documentary: American Pachuco: The Legend of Luiz Valdez, David Alvarado’s film on the Chicano theater pioneer, screened June 11 as part of a Latino Arts Festival collaboration after winning the 2026 Sundance Audience Award for US Documentary. Luis Valdez’s El Teatro Campesino emerged directly from the 1965 Delano grape strike and farmworker organizing — a reminder of the historical bond between cultural production and the labor movement. - Music obituaries: Avant-garde guitarist James Blood Ulmer died June 3 at 86; his “harmolodic” fusion of jazz, funk and blues, developed in collaboration with Ornette Coleman, was among the most original American musical voices of the late 20th century. R&B vocalist Peabo Bryson, a two-time Grammy winner, died June 2 at 75. - Stage and screen: Actor Anthony Head, known to a generation for Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Ted Lasso, died June 1 at 72. British character actor Patrick Godfrey died June 4 at 93. - History: Gordon S. Wood, the influential historian of the American Revolution, died June 7 at 92 after being struck by a car; his scholarship reshaped understanding of the social forces behind the founding of the United States.

The concentration of deaths of older artists and intellectuals is a reminder of a generation formed in the postwar decades now passing — figures whose work, at its best, treated art as a means of grappling with history and society rather than as disposable content.

Nexteer worker fired for opposing UAW sellout — Sheila Brehm, Jerry White

Antwiane Sanders, a 10-year Nexteer Automotive employee in Saginaw, Michigan, was fired after opposing the tentative contract at a plant roll-out meeting. The authors document that UAW International Representative Jason Tuck — earning $148,000/year — collaborated with management to remove Sanders. The contract offers only $27/hour after four years despite an 86% strike-authorization vote that the apparatus ignored; Dana workers rejected similar deals by 90%+ margins. The article frames Sanders’s termination as deliberate intimidation by a bureaucracy acting as management’s enforcer. For the editorial board, the firing is a sharp illustration of the union apparatus’s integration into corporate management and a concrete organizing issue for the rank-and-file committee network.

What the USPS Workers Rank-and-File Inquiry uncovered about Palmetto RPDC — Jane Wise

A worker-led inquiry into the Palmetto Regional Processing and Distribution Center documents the deaths of four workers — Shannon Barnes, Eric Smith, Russell Scruggs Jr. and Demarcus Little — over roughly two years. In Little’s case, emergency response was delayed about 19 minutes from the initial 911 call. The inquiry found no written safety protocols, no on-site medical staff, no defibrillators, blocked cell coverage and inadequate lockout/tagout procedures. OSHA fined USPS just $26,481 for one incident in which a worker fell into a sorting machine. The article connects the deaths to the “Delivering for America” consolidation and privatization program. The inquiry is itself a model of the rank-and-file investigative method the ICFI promotes against the suppression of worker safety data.

Canada Post workers vote reluctantly for concessions-filled contracts — Daniel Berkley

Canada Post workers ratified five-year contracts — 85.9% for RSMC members, 90% for Urban Postal Operations — after two strikes within a year (fall 2024, fall 2025) that were both terminated under government pressure. Berkley, a member of the Postal Workers Rank-and-File Committee, argues the votes reflect resignation rather than endorsement: the restructuring plan calls for cutting the postal workforce by up to two-thirds over ten years and eliminating door-to-door delivery within five. The article links the Canadian, US (Palmetto) and broader international postal struggles through the IWA-RFC against a coordinated, government-backed privatization drive.

Educators in US and UK support fight against Australian Education Union sellout — Committee for Public Education

This compilation of solidarity messages cites San Francisco Unified’s $114 million in cuts, over 1,000 Los Angeles layoff notices, a 20% loss of real income for UK teachers since 2010, and Detroit’s 2023 contract that achieved only 26% ratification while eliminating 300+ positions — framing the Victorian sellout as one front in a global assault on education requiring coordination through the IWA-RFC.

Australian pseudo-left tries to head off rebellion against Labor-union sellout of Victorian teachers — Committee for Public Education

The article critiques the Australian Education Union’s deal with Victoria’s Labor government — locking in real wage cuts and stripping strike rights — and attacks “Socialists in Schools,” a Socialist Alternative formation, for channeling teacher anger back into the union. It compares the deal to the 2024 Victorian nurses agreement (deferring gains to 2027) and the 2022 NSW agreement (2.5-3% increases amounting to real cuts), and calls for rank-and-file committees demanding compensation for lost wages and class-size limits.

New Zealand’s pseudo-left ISO calls for “anti-war” alliance with pro-imperialist Labour and Greens — Tom Peters

Peters dissects the New Zealand International Socialist Organisation’s call for coalition-building with Labour and the Greens as an “anti-war” strategy, characterizing it as a 21st-century Popular Front subordinating working-class independence to bourgeois parties. He documents that the 2017-2023 Labour-Greens government strengthened the US alliance, expanded military spending and endorsed Israeli operations in Gaza, and exposes the ISO’s selective “anti-imperialism” — backing Ukrainian resistance to Russia while opposing US wars — as pro-NATO alignment masquerading as principle.
