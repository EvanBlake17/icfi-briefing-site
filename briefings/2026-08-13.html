Colombia earthquake death toll passes 265 as rescue window closes

The 7.4-magnitude earthquake that struck western Colombia — the strongest to hit the country in 47 years — has killed at least 265 people, with rescue crews entering what officials describe as the final hours in which survivors can realistically be pulled from collapsed buildings. More than 3,700 people have been wounded and over 25,800 families displaced or otherwise affected across Cali, Pereira, Manizales and Quibdó.

The gap between official and civilian casualty accounting is itself a political fact. The government lists nearly 500 people as missing; crowd-sourced databases maintained by families and volunteers put the number above 4,100. President Abelardo de la Espriella, who took office pledging law-and-order austerity, has moved to declare an economic emergency, a mechanism that will govern how reconstruction is financed and who bears the cost.

Key facts:

  • Death toll at least 265, with more than 3,700 wounded and 25,800+ families affected.
  • Official missing count near 500; civilian-run databases list more than 4,100.
  • De la Espriella: “Our efforts are focused on the disappeared.”
  • Cali’s mayor: “The next 20 hours are the most critical to find and rescue people alive. Every minute is vital.”
  • Foreign aid: US pledged over $15 million plus disaster teams; Mexico sent 19.5 tons of supplies and 255 specialists; El Salvador dispatched two planes with 100 tons of aid.

The scale of building collapse in dense urban working-class districts, and the reliance on volunteer digging in the critical first 72 hours, points to the familiar pattern in which seismic risk is socially distributed — concentrated in substandard, unregulated housing.

Ukraine strikes Novorossiysk, damaging four Russian warships

Ukrainian jet drones and Neptune cruise missiles struck Russia’s principal Black Sea naval base at Novorossiysk overnight, damaging four warships in what is the most destructive single attack on the port since it became the Black Sea Fleet’s main harbor. Two Grigorovich-class frigates capable of launching Kalibr cruise missiles — the Admiral Essen and Admiral Makarov — were hit, along with a Buyan-M missile ship and a patrol vessel.

The strike also hit two grain terminals and the pier of the Sheskharis oil terminal, one of Russia’s largest crude export outlets. Russian officials reported at least three people killed; a regional governor said drone debris struck a residential area and killed an eight-year-old child. Novorossiysk became Russia’s primary Black Sea base after earlier Ukrainian strikes forced the fleet to abandon Sevastopol.

Key facts:

  • Four vessels damaged: Admiral Essen, Admiral Makarov, a Buyan-M missile ship, and a patrol vessel.
  • Sheskharis oil terminal pier and two grain terminals struck — direct hits on export infrastructure.
  • At least three killed, including an eight-year-old child hit by drone debris in a residential district.
  • Over the four weeks from July 14 to August 11, Russian forces achieved a net territorial gain of just 33 square miles, according to the Russia Matters war tracker.

The near-total stalemate on the ground alongside escalating deep-strike exchanges on energy and port infrastructure is the signature of a war of attrition being sustained by NATO weapons transfers with no negotiated exit on offer from any capital.

Israel kills more than 25 Palestinians over the weekend, rejects Trump’s ceasefire terms

More than 25 Palestinians were killed in Gaza over the weekend by Israeli airstrikes, shelling and shooting, extending a pattern in which July recorded over 150 deaths — the highest monthly toll of 2026 despite the nominal ceasefire framework. An Israeli strike near Al-Aqsa Hospital destroyed medical supplies delivered by the World Health Organization, deepening shortages of insulin, dialysis consumables and disinfectants.

Israel’s government has formally rejected the withdrawal terms of Trump’s 15-point plan, citing “serious security concerns” and insisting no pullback will occur before the complete disarmament of Hamas — a condition that functions as an open-ended license for continued operations.

Key facts:

  • 25+ killed over the weekend; 150+ killed in July, the highest monthly toll this year.
  • WHO-delivered medical stock destroyed in a strike near Al-Aqsa Hospital; insulin and dialysis materials now critically short.
  • A World Food Programme survey found 40 percent of Gaza households cannot afford basic food and nearly 50 percent have completely run out of food at home.
  • Israel cites “serious security concerns” in refusing withdrawal ahead of full Hamas disarmament.

The hunger data is the decisive point: nine months into the “ceasefire,” the engineered starvation of the population continues as policy, with the aid architecture used as leverage rather than relief.

Trump’s secret escape from Ankara exposes crisis of the administration

Reporting by the Washington Post, followed by the New York Times, has revealed that Trump did not fly home from the NATO summit in Ankara aboard Air Force One. After boarding the aircraft in view of cameras and waving to the press, he was moved off the plane via an airport catering truck raised to a rear service door and transferred to a waiting C-32A military jet, accompanied by only a handful of personal aides — executive assistant Natalie Harp, valet Walt Nauta, deputy chief of staff Dan Scavino — and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth. The stated justification was intelligence on an alleged Iranian plot to shoot down Air Force One with a shoulder-fired missile.

Everyone else stayed on the decoy: Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller, communications director Steven Cheung, and the full traveling press corps. Some White House staff aboard the aircraft reportedly did not know.

Writing in today’s WSWS Perspective, Patrick Martin poses the question the administration has refused to answer: if the threat was credible, why was the flight not cancelled, and why were more than a hundred people left aboard as, in effect, bait to “draw enemy fire”? Martin argues the assassination-plot narrative most likely originates with the Netanyahu government, which has been promoting claims of Iranian plots against Trump as a pretext for resuming full-scale bombing of Iran.

Key facts:

  • Trump transferred to a C-32A; Hegseth was the only cabinet-rank official to accompany him.
  • The Secret Service and Pentagon have deflected questions to each other and to the White House; Cheung offered only that “there are many enemies of America who have their sights on him.”
  • The FBI has subpoenaed New York Times reporters, with agents visiting reporters’ homes and confronting family members, over reporting on the Qatari jet gifted to Trump as a replacement Air Force One.
  • The Pentagon revoked the security clearance of former Air Force Secretary Frank Kendall over alleged leaks.
  • On returning from Ankara, Trump declared that in the event of his assassination Iran would be bombed “on an unprecedented scale,” claiming 1,000 missiles were “Locked and Loaded.”

Iran and Oman near deal to reopen Strait of Hormuz

Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said a joint statement with Oman on reopening shipping lanes through the Strait of Hormuz is “under review and in the final drafting stage,” after more than three weeks of negotiations that have produced broad agreement on inbound and outbound routing. The strait has been effectively closed since the US-Israeli war on Iran began in February, removing from world markets a chokepoint that normally carries roughly a quarter of seaborne oil trade and a fifth of LNG shipments.

Tehran has made clear it intends to extract concessions from Washington before fully reopening the waterway — the closure being the one instrument of leverage Iran retains after six months of bombardment. Vice President JD Vance told Fox News the administration wants to “maximize the amount of oil and gas that are coming out of the Strait of Hormuz.”

Key facts:

  • Hormuz normally carries ~25 percent of global seaborne oil and ~20 percent of LNG trade.
  • Closed since February 2026; negotiations mediated by Oman have run over three weeks.
  • Iran explicitly conditions full reopening on US concessions.
  • The IEA has cut its 2026 oil demand forecast by 1.6 million barrels per day, citing high fuel prices and Hormuz disruption.

Federal judge expands nationwide block on Trump’s mail-ballot order

US District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston has blocked the Postal Service from implementing Trump’s executive order restricting mail-in voting anywhere in the country, extending an earlier ruling that had applied to nearly two dozen states. The order, signed in March on the basis of unproven claims of 2020 mail-ballot fraud, directed USPS to compile lists of eligible voters supplied by states and to deliver absentee ballots only to individuals appearing on those lists — converting the postal service into a gatekeeper of the franchise ahead of the November midterms.

The administration is now seeking Supreme Court intervention to reinstate the restrictions before the election.

Key facts:

  • Nationwide injunction issued August 11 by Judge Talwani, US District Court for the District of Massachusetts.
  • The order would have required USPS to withhold absentee ballots from anyone not on state-supplied lists.
  • Prior ruling from the same judge already blocked the order in nearly two dozen states.
  • Trump is petitioning the Supreme Court for emergency relief.

The reliance on a single district judge to hold the line — and the administration’s expectation that the Supreme Court will overturn her — underscores that the defense of voting rights has been left entirely to the courts, with the Democratic Party offering no independent mobilization.

Sudan’s war grinds on as El-Obeid faces daily drone strikes and cholera

UN Deputy Secretary-General Amina Mohammed warned that “Sudan has already lost a generation to its earlier wars, and it is losing another one now,” as hundreds of thousands of civilians in El-Obeid, capital of North Kordofan, endure daily drone strikes, a collapsing water supply and a spreading cholera outbreak. More than 100,000 displaced people are sheltering in camps in and around the city under conditions of extreme hunger, with UN agencies reporting high rates of sexual violence against women and girls.

RSF leader Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo (“Hemedti”) released a video ordering his forces to seize Khartoum, even as Sudanese government forces claim advances in North Kordofan.

Key facts:

  • At least 59,000 people killed since the war began in 2023, per ACLED.
  • 100,000+ displaced sheltering in El-Obeid camps; water infrastructure described by the UN as in “critical” condition.
  • Cholera spreading through displacement camps; daily drone strikes on civilian areas.
  • Hemedti has ordered a renewed RSF push on the capital.

Both belligerents are sustained by external sponsors — the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and, at a remove, the major powers competing for Red Sea position and Sudanese gold — while the humanitarian catastrophe receives a fraction of the coverage devoted to conflicts of Western strategic interest.

Taiwan mounts largest-ever reservist call-up

Taiwan’s annual ten-day defense exercises, underway through early August, involve the largest reservist mobilization in the island’s history. Beyond conventional maneuvers, the drills are testing the relocation of weapons production lines and the conversion of civilian factories to wartime military output — a shift from deterrence exercises to industrial mobilization planning.

  • Chinese state broadcaster CCTV released footage on August 1 of a large-scale amphibious assault exercise integrating drones and unmanned ground vehicles.
  • Japan’s newly released defense white paper states that the military balance across the strait is “rapidly tilting in China’s favor.”
  • A Taiwanese court sentenced a Chinese immigrant to seven years’ imprisonment for fraud and for acting at Beijing’s direction to influence Taiwanese politics — part of a widening internal security apparatus.

The convergence of Taiwanese industrial mobilization, Japanese rearmament and Chinese amphibious rehearsal marks a qualitative escalation in the preparations of all sides for a conflict none claims to want.

Hungary’s new government moves to dismantle Orbán’s state apparatus

The Hungarian National Assembly has continued its constitutional overhaul under Prime Minister Péter Magyar, whose Tisza party won 141 of 199 seats in the 2026 election, ending Viktor Orbán’s sixteen-year rule. On August 11, parliament moved to install András Baka — the Supreme Court president Orbán removed from office in 2011, whose case became a landmark European rights judgment — as part of a sweeping replacement of Orbán-appointed officeholders including the head of state and senior judges.

  • Tisza’s two-thirds majority has passed constitutional amendments creating a new anti-corruption agency with broad investigative powers.
  • Magyar has termed the campaign “Operation Clean Sweep,” describing the vote as “a crucial milestone in dismantling the Orbán political and economic mafia.”
  • The reforms are tied to an agreement with the European Commission to unlock €16.4 billion ($19 billion) in funds frozen over rule-of-law disputes, with a target of end-August.

The framing of the purge as anti-corruption obscures its content: an institutional reordering demanded and financed by Brussels, carried out by a right-wing formation whose economic program differs from Orbán’s chiefly in its orientation toward EU capital rather than domestic oligarchy. Nothing in the constitutional rewrite addresses Hungarian workers’ wages or the country’s role as a low-cost assembly platform for German industry.

National Steel Car workers strike in Hamilton, Ontario

More than 1,200 workers at National Steel Car, the railcar manufacturer in Hamilton, Ontario, walked out at 12:01 a.m. on August 12 after rejecting the company’s contract offer by 79 percent — the second strike at the plant in three years. Of 1,202 United Steelworkers members, 1,092 cast ballots.

  • The rejected offer provided wage increases of 3 percent in year one and 2 percent in each of years two and three.
  • The central grievance is the piecework “incentive” system, which workers link to three deaths at the plant between 2020 and 2022.
  • The 2023 strike at the same plant lasted 41 days and ended in a settlement the WSWS characterizes as a sellout by the USW apparatus.

Writing on the WSWS, Dylan Lubao argues the piecework regime is not an incidental abuse but the mechanism by which the company converts safety into throughput, and that the strike’s outcome depends on wresting control from the USW bureaucracy that negotiated the system’s continuation.

Markets rally on in-line July CPI

July consumer price data released August 12 came in at +0.1 percent month-over-month and +3.4 percent year-over-year headline, with core at +0.2 percent monthly and +2.5 percent annually — squarely in line with consensus and read by markets as reinforcing a disinflation narrative. JPMorgan Wealth Management now sees a possible 25 basis-point hike in September on energy-driven inflation risk, while other pricing implies meaningful odds of a cut — an unusually wide split.

Tariff burden lands on households as 50 percent Canada duties near

New Section 338 tariffs of 50 percent on roughly $16 billion of Canadian agriculture, dairy, furniture and alcohol take effect August 19. The Tax Policy Center estimates Trump’s 2026 tariff program will cost the average US household about $920 this year — a consumption tax falling hardest on the lowest-income deciles, imposed at the same moment the Fed contemplates raising rates to suppress the resulting price increases. National Guard deployment in Washington extended to 2029

Twelve months after Trump declared a crime emergency in Washington, DC and deployed the National Guard on what was presented as a temporary basis, the mission has been extended through January 2029. As of August 4, more than 4,600 military personnel drawn from the District and 24 states and territories remained deployed in the capital, alongside an undisclosed number of federal law enforcement officers.

Trump used Truth Social on August 12 to attack a Center for American Progress report assessing the deployment’s effects, after CAP’s Chandler Hall discussed the findings on C-SPAN. The Pentagon has separately been directed to establish National Guard rapid-response units in every state — a standing domestic intervention force.

US measles cases hit a 35-year high

As of August 6, the CDC has confirmed 2,465 measles cases across 47 jurisdictions in 2026 — surpassing the full-year 2025 total of 2,289 and marking the highest annual count since 1991. Seven states account for over 84 percent of cases, led by South Carolina with 670 and Utah with 522. Ninety-three percent of those infected were unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status.

The United States now stands to lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000, which requires that no chain of continuous transmission exceed twelve months. The proximate cause is not scientific uncertainty but the deliberate degradation of public vaccination programs and the promotion of anti-vaccine positions from within the federal health apparatus itself.

COVID-19 growing in 43 states as Asian positivity climbs

CDC modeling as of August 5 shows COVID-19 infections growing or likely growing in 43 states, with the sharpest increases in the West and South, even as aggregate respiratory illness activity remains very low. In China, sentinel hospitals reported a 22.3 percent COVID positivity rate for July 27 to August 2. Taiwan recorded a 27.6 percent one-week jump in COVID-related emergency and outpatient visits, reaching 24,645.

The late-summer uptick suggests a wave building ahead of autumn, with obvious implications for vaccine timing — at a moment when the US vaccination infrastructure is being dismantled rather than mobilized.

H5N1 expands its ecological range

  • Victoria, Australia logged 60 positive H5 cases as of August 12.

Lancet trial finds statin discontinuation at 75 non-inferior

A French randomized trial by the SAGA/SITE Study Group, published August 11 in The Lancet Healthy Longevity, found that in adults aged 75 and over taking statins for primary prevention — with no prior cardiovascular disease — discontinuation was non-inferior to continuation for all-cause mortality over three years. The finding supports individualized deprescribing and reduction of pill burden in older patients, though it will predictably be seized on by insurers and health systems as a cost-cutting rationale rather than a clinical one.

LiteLLM supply-chain breach exposes 434,000 CI/CD pipelines

Threat intelligence firm CloudSEK reported that a March 2026 compromise of two LiteLLM PyPI packages (versions 1.82.7 and 1.82.8), live for roughly 40 minutes, potentially exposed more than 2,500 organizations and approximately 434,000 continuous-integration pipelines — including at Nvidia, AWS, Samsung, Cisco and Siemens. The FBI issued a FLASH advisory in July warning that credentials stolen in the incident remain weaponizable. It is the largest AI-infrastructure supply-chain breach identified so far this year, and a demonstration that the entire commercial AI buildout rests on volunteer-maintained open-source components with no security funding.

Arts and culture

Kennedy Center ordered to pay musician who refused to perform under Trump’s name

A District of Columbia Superior Court has ordered the Kennedy Center to pay $252,479.70 in attorney’s fees to jazz musician Chuck Redd, who cancelled his annual Christmas Eve performance after the institution’s board voted to add Trump’s name to the building. The court dismissed the Center’s suit against Redd in June, finding it had failed to prove he had signed any performance contract. The decision is significant beyond its dollar amount: a federally chartered cultural institution attempted to use civil litigation to punish an individual artist for a political refusal, and lost.

Redd’s cancellation was one of the earlier acts in an exodus that has now hollowed out the institution. The Washington National Opera — resident at the Kennedy Center since 1971 — announced in January that it would move its performances elsewhere, a departure Kennedy Center president Richard Grenell reframed as a strategic decision by “the Trump Kennedy Center” to end an exclusive partnership. Composer Philip Glass withdrew the world premiere of his Symphony No. 15, “Lincoln,” stating that the leadership’s values were incompatible with the work’s message. House Republicans have proposed naming the Opera House after Melania Trump.

What is being enacted is not simply vandalism of a building’s signage but the conversion of a national cultural institution into an instrument of personal cult and state ideology. The response — individual withdrawal by artists — has been principled but atomized, and has left the institution’s programming to be filled by those willing to accept the terms.

Venice announces its 83rd edition

The Venice Film Festival, running September 2–12 on the Lido under Alberto Barbera, opens with Danny Boyle’s Ink, on Rupert Murdoch’s acquisition of The Sun, with Guy Pearce as Murdoch and Jack O’Connell as editor Larry Lamb. That a major festival opens with a dramatization of the origins of the modern tabloid press — the machinery through which British working-class consciousness was systematically degraded across four decades — is at least a promising choice of subject.

The competition includes Lee Chang-dong’s Possible Love, his first feature since Burning (2018); Florian Zeller’s Bunker; Martin McDonagh’s Wild Horse Nine; and Casey Affleck’s directorial effort Company. Honorary Golden Lions go to George Clooney and Ellen Burstyn. Trade reporting notes the selection is unusually light on studio and streamer product and heavily weighted toward male directors.

Beyond the factual account summarized above, Martin’s Perspective supplies the historical framing that the bourgeois reporting omits entirely. He recalls that the US-Israeli war on Iran opened with the assassination of the country’s top leadership, including Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei “and most of his family, children and grandchildren among them,” followed by the bombing of a school near the Strait of Hormuz that killed “more than 160 schoolgirls and their teachers.” Iran did not capitulate as Trump predicted; instead it closed the strait, exercising the one form of economic leverage Washington has proven unable to counter. Martin cites reports that US stockpiles of anti-missile interceptors and other munitions are nearing exhaustion, degrading American capacity for war against Russia or China.

The Ankara episode is characterized as “a political humiliation for American imperialism” — the US president compelled to “steal away from the capital of a NATO country like a thief in the night,” which is irreconcilable with the claim that Iran has been militarily destroyed and is eager to negotiate.

The concluding indictment generalizes beyond Trump: an oligarchy that has ordered operations killing “more than 3,400 Iranians, among them hundreds of children; more than 73,000 Palestinians in Gaza; 18 American soldiers whose deaths the Pentagon then tried to erase from its records,” while treating human life as “the cost of doing business.” Martin: “Their own safety is paramount and inviolable; everyone else is expendable.” He closes by locating the administration in a compound crisis — geopolitical debacle abroad and “mounting social confrontation with the working class at home.”

Australian union calls off strike by Victorian doctors — Martin Scott, Eric Ludlow

The Australian Salaried Medical Officers’ Federation cancelled a strike by Victorian public hospital doctors scheduled for August 14 — the first such stoppage in twenty years, backed by a 97 percent strike vote — after the state Labor government challenged the technical validity of the strike notice. Rather than await a Fair Work Commission determination, the union capitulated pre-emptively.

The article’s significance is in the mechanism: Labor did not have to defeat the strike politically or industrially. It only had to raise a procedural objection, and the union apparatus supplied the retreat. This is the operational content of the Fair Work regime, which converts every dispute into a legal question adjudicated by a body designed to make industrial action nearly impossible. Scott and Ludlow argue that Victorian doctors, whose grievances concern unsafe rostering and understaffing that directly threaten patients, cannot advance through a union structure that accepts the legal framework as legitimate, and call for rank-and-file committees able to coordinate action independently of ASMOF and across the health workforce. The conclusion is programmatic: the defense of public healthcare requires the socialist reorganization of society, not better-drafted strike notices.

Homeless people die in New Zealand winter — Tom Peters

At least two homeless people have died during freezing winter temperatures in New Zealand, as rough sleeping in Wellington rose to 163 from 145 a year earlier and emergency night shelters closed. Peters cites an Auckland doctor whose homeless patients have life expectancies roughly 30 percent below the national average — a figure that quantifies the mortality cost of the housing crisis with unusual precision.

The political point is directed at the entire parliamentary spectrum: Labour, National, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori all accept the property relations that make housing an investment asset before it is shelter, and the shelter closures occurred under funding decisions none of them meaningfully contested. Peters connects the New Zealand situation to the international pattern of homelessness expanding in the wealthiest countries during a period of record asset prices.

Chile’s courts seal impunity for Carabinero officer who blinded Gustavo Gatica — WSWS reporter

The Santiago Court of Appeals rejected nullity appeals and confirmed the acquittal of former Carabineros lieutenant colonel Claudio Crespo for the shotgun pellets that permanently blinded Gustavo Gatica during the 2019 social uprising, in which 3,838 people were hospitalized and 460 suffered eye wounds. The judgment is now final and unappealable, closing the avenue pursued by the Public Ministry, the INDH and the state defense council. The tribunal accepted “privileged self-defense” on the grounds that Gatica was carrying a stone.

The WSWS locates the decisive fact in the law rather than the verdict: the Naín-Retamal Law’s privileged self-defense provision, applied retroactively, was passed under the Boric government with Communist Party support. President José Antonio Kast has publicly welcomed the ruling. The article’s conclusion is that the parliamentary road — including the “left” governments that presented themselves as the political expression of the 2019 revolt — has produced not accountability but a codified legal shield for police violence, and that justice requires independent revolutionary organization of the working class.

Australia: Vote no to the third AEU-Labor sellout! Build the Victorian Educators Rank-and-File Committee! — Committee for Public Education

A statement opposing the third enterprise agreement put to Victorian public school educators, after members rejected two prior versions by 57.7 percent and 51.2 percent. The offer provides 28–32 percent over four years plus a $2,000 payment, but carries a no-strike clause running through 2030 — meaning educators would surrender the right to industrial action for the entire agreement term in exchange for increases that will be substantially consumed by inflation. The CFPE calls for a no vote, 40 percent raises, capped class sizes, and the construction of a rank-and-file committee independent of the Australian Education Union apparatus, which has now three times attempted to secure ratification of essentially the same framework.

Trump administration moves to arm ICE agents with electric-shock torture gloves — Jacob Crosse

ICE is procuring “G.L.O.V.E.” electric-shock devices delivering 324 to 362 volts and capable of up to 90 minutes of continuous shock application, in a contract valued at $10–20 million within the $170 billion immigration enforcement package. The manufacturer’s own manual acknowledges a risk of death.

Crosse’s central political argument concerns the Democratic Party’s role: by agreeing to separate ICE funding from broader appropriations, Democrats preserved the ability to vote against symbolic measures while allowing the money for the repressive apparatus to flow uninterrupted. The devices are designed for close-quarters application to restrained individuals — their function is not crowd control but the infliction of pain on people already in custody. Crosse situates the procurement in the construction of a domestic paramilitary force whose eventual target is not limited to immigrants.

Historian Mario Kessler denounces sentencing of Ukrainian Trotskyist Bogdan Syrotiuk — Mario Kessler

Historian Mario Kessler, formerly of the University of Potsdam and a specialist in the history of German socialism and antisemitism, has issued a statement denouncing the Ukrainian court’s conviction of Bogdan Syrotiuk on charges of working for the Russian state. Kessler notes the elementary fact that Syrotiuk explicitly and publicly condemned the Russian invasion, making the charge factually unsustainable on its face. He invokes Trotsky’s record — opposition to antisemitic pogroms carried out by all sides in the civil war, and support for Ukrainian national self-determination — to establish that the political tradition Syrotiuk represents is the opposite of Great Russian chauvinism. The statement’s value lies in its provenance: an academic historian with no organizational connection to the ICFI publicly characterizing the prosecution as political persecution. The WSWS campaign for Syrotiuk’s release continues.

Official who threatened rank-and-file candidate Will Lehman suspended from UAW membership, but might remain on union payroll — Jerry White

Court-appointed UAW monitor Neil Barofsky has suspended official Raymond Jensen Jr. from union membership for two years and barred him from future union office, after Jensen posted an AI-generated image depicting socialist UAW presidential candidate Will Lehman bound, gagged and held at gunpoint. Jensen may nonetheless retain his $202,000 salary.

White’s argument is that the outcome demonstrates the limits of relying on the federal monitor. The monitorship was established to police the apparatus after the corruption convictions, but it operates within the framework of preserving that apparatus — it can suspend a membership while leaving the salary, the position and the underlying structure intact. The threat against Lehman was not an aberration by one individual but an expression of the bureaucracy’s response to a rank-and-file challenge to its existence. White concludes that workers must build rank-and-file committees rather than appeal to court officers.

Pseudo-left press review

  • Jacobin — three articles, two of them on a single Democratic primary, treating a near-miss on the Democratic ballot line as proof the “socialist electoral project” has room to grow
  • Left Voice — reports the same primary cycle as a “progressive sweep” while running a genuinely useful exposé on privatized surveillance infrastructure
  • Liberation News / PSL — sharpest rhetoric against the Democratic establishment, yet frames anti-Zionist and anti-ICE sentiment entirely through Democratic primary outcomes
  • Socialist Alternative — the only US tendency explicitly rejecting both parties, but channels this into “left independent” electoralism
  • Socialist Worker, SWP UK — from-below framing of South Asian uprisings; skeptical of Unite’s re-elected leadership
  • Socialist Appeal / RCP — British capitalism in terminal decline, with no illusions in Labour but no strategy beyond propaganda

Jacobin and DSA

Jacobin devoted two of its three notable pieces to the Wisconsin Democratic primary defeat of Francesca Hong. “The Socialist Electoral Project Still Hasn’t Hit Its Ceiling” presents the loss as evidence of expanding left strength, measured by vote share within a Democratic primary. “Five Takeaways From Francesca Hong’s Near-Win in Wisconsin” extracts tactical lessons on the same terrain — canvassing operations, endorsement dynamics, the role of the state party.

The unexamined premise of both is that the vehicle for socialist advance is the Democratic ballot line, and the measure of progress is how close a DSA-aligned candidate came to defeating the machine. Neither article poses the question of what a Hong victory would have obligated her to — the Wisconsin Democratic caucus, the party’s national fundraising apparatus, its unbroken support for the Gaza and Iran wars. The “ceiling” metaphor is itself revealing: it treats the Democratic Party as a container that can be filled from below rather than a state-integrated institution designed to absorb and dissipate exactly this energy.

“John Deere Is Testing Its Workers’ Resolve” is stronger, covering the UAW contract fight with genuine sympathy for the shop floor. But it treats the UAW apparatus as a neutral or merely inadequate instrument rather than as management’s partner, and offers no perspective for workers beyond hoping the union negotiates harder.

Assessment: A day’s output almost entirely absorbed in the internal metrics of Democratic Party primaries — the clearest possible expression of the DSA’s function as a left flank of the party it claims to be transforming.

Left Voice

“Sanders-Backed Flanagan Wins Minnesota Senate Primary, Continuing Progressive Sweep” reports a Sanders-aligned candidate’s victory as part of a broader wave. The framing — a “sweep” of progressive candidates within Democratic primaries — sits uneasily with Left Voice’s own stated Trotskyist opposition to class collaboration. The article does not draw the conclusion its politics would require: that a Sanders endorsement is precisely the mechanism by which anti-establishment sentiment is recaptured for the Democratic Party.

“How Flock Is Building America’s Private Surveillance Infrastructure” is the more valuable piece — a documented account of how the automated license-plate-reader company has built a national surveillance network sold to municipalities, arguing correctly that privatization is not the problem and municipal ownership is not the solution, since the class function of the apparatus is unchanged.

Assessment: Capable investigative work coexisting with an electoral commentary that reports Democratic primary results in the vocabulary of the milieu it formally criticizes.

Liberation News (PSL)

“Desperate Democratic Establishment Narrowly Defeats Hong in Wisconsin” is the most class-antagonistic treatment of the primary, naming Governor Evers and the “big-business” Democrats as the opponent and advancing concrete demands — minimum wage increases, healthcare, repeal of the anti-union Act 10. “Flanagan Wins Big in Primary as Minnesotans Reject AIPAC and ICE” reads a Democratic primary result as a popular referendum against AIPAC and immigration raids.

The contradiction is structural: the PSL denounces the Democratic Party as an instrument of capital, then reports its primaries as the arena where mass consciousness registers itself. Treating a Sanders- and Warren-backed candidate’s win as a mass anti-Zionist verdict inflates an electoral datum into a political mandate and obscures that the winner will caucus with the party funding the war.

Assessment: Militant tone, national-reformist content — a program of demands to be won from the existing state, with no perspective of independent working-class power.

Socialist Alternative

With no output in the immediate window, the most recent significant pieces are “Support Left Independent Candidates—Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties,” which rejects both parties by name and cites ICE killings and US backing for the Gaza and Iran wars, and “South Africa: Xenophobia & The Street War Against Black Immigrants,” which traces anti-immigrant violence to mass unemployment and indicts the ANC’s post-1994 neoliberal turn.

The South Africa piece is a serious class analysis of a national liberation party’s integration into capitalist rule. The electoral piece, however, substitutes “left independent” campaigns — the Sawant model — for the construction of a revolutionary party, offering municipal-reform electoralism outside the Democratic Party rather than a break with electoralism as strategy.

Assessment: Correct on the party question, reformist on the programmatic one.

Socialist Worker (SWP UK)

“Revolt Spreads in India and Pakistan” celebrates spontaneous mass uprisings across South Asia, paired with anti-monarchist commentary on the Mountbatten-Windsor funeral. “Sharon Graham Wins After Poor Choice in Unite Union Leadership Race” applies the classic SWP rank-and-file line — skepticism toward Graham and the Unite machine, a call for shop-floor organization independent of officials.

The limitation is the same one the SWP has carried for six decades: mass struggle is celebrated as self-sufficient, with the party reduced to an auxiliary that encourages spontaneity rather than fighting for a program. “Rank-and-file organization” without a struggle for political leadership leaves the bureaucracy in place.

Assessment: Genuine internationalist instinct, no theory of the party.

Socialist Appeal and the RCP (IMT)

“Burnham and British Capitalism – Bouncing Into an Abyss” argues Labour’s post-Burnham polling recovery is illusory given the underlying crisis of British capitalism. “UK Foreign Office Decimated: Britain’s ‘Soft’ Power Gets Even Softer” reads Foreign Office austerity as symptomatic of British imperialism’s terminal decline rather than as underfunded diplomacy.

Both avoid the reformist illusions in Labour that characterized this tendency’s earlier entrism, and the imperialist-decline framing is analytically closer to Marxism than most of what appears elsewhere in this survey. What is absent is any programmatic conclusion — the pieces describe capitalist crisis as spectacle, with the revolutionary party appearing only as an assertion at the end.

Assessment: Sound diagnosis, propagandistic prescription.

Cross-tendency notes: Three US outlets led with the same Wisconsin primary and none broke toward independent working-class politics outside the Democratic ballot line. Strikingly, no article across any of the six tendencies in this window took a stated position on an ongoing imperialist war — Gaza, Iran or Ukraine appeared only obliquely. No direct polemics against the ICFI or Trotskyism appeared in the window scanned.

1. Northern California Teamsters strike shuts down concrete supply at 15 CalPortland plants

More than 350 ready-mix drivers organized in four Teamsters locals have been out since August 6 over pensions, healthcare, working conditions and wages, halting concrete supply across a major construction market. The strike is a test of whether the Teamsters apparatus under Sean O’Brien will conduct a serious fight or settle quickly, and it involves a workforce with direct leverage over the construction cycle. The WSWS has no coverage of a stoppage now in its second week.

2. Measles reaches a 35-year high as the US approaches loss of elimination status

The 2,465 confirmed cases already exceed the 2025 total, with 93 percent of infections in unvaccinated or unknown-status individuals and two states accounting for nearly half the burden. This is the direct, measurable result of the anti-vaccine turn in federal public health policy — a subject on which the WSWS has campaigned consistently since the pandemic — and the loss of elimination status would be a landmark of social retrogression.

Recommended reading NBC News US measles cases hit 35-year high

3. National Guard occupation of Washington extended to 2029 as rapid-response units ordered in every state

A deployment presented as a temporary crime emergency has been extended more than three years, with 4,600 troops still in the capital and the Pentagon directed to stand up Guard quick-reaction forces nationwide. This is the institutionalization of domestic military deployment, and the near-total absence of congressional resistance is itself the story.

Recommended reading Washington Times Washington grapples with a National Guard deployment extended until 2029

4. Fifty percent tariffs on Canadian goods take effect August 19 as household costs reach $920

The new Section 338 duties hit roughly $16 billion in agriculture, dairy, furniture and alcohol, layering onto a tariff program the Tax Policy Center estimates will cost the average household $920 this year. The Canadian labor bureaucracy’s response has been to demand protection for Canadian capital rather than a joint struggle with US workers — the nationalist trap the WSWS has analyzed repeatedly.

Recommended reading Tax Foundation Tariff tracker: 2026 Trump tariffs and trade war by the numbers

5. NYC public defender strike spreads to the Bronx

Public defenders have extended their strike into the Bronx over pay and unsustainable caseloads, in a borough where the indigent defense system is closest to collapse. The strike exposes the class content of the criminal legal system — the constitutional right to counsel underwritten by an overworked, underpaid workforce — and connects public-sector austerity directly to mass incarceration.

6. Hungary’s constitutional purge and the €16.4 billion price of EU compliance

The Tisza government’s “Operation Clean Sweep” is being conducted to a Brussels timetable, with a two-thirds majority rewriting the constitution and replacing the head of state and senior judiciary in exchange for the release of frozen EU funds by the end of August. The presentation of this as democratic restoration requires interrogation: a right-wing party is reorganizing the Hungarian state to the specifications of European finance capital, and no section of the working class has been consulted.

Two malicious packages live for forty minutes compromised credentials across 2,500 organizations including Nvidia, AWS, Samsung, Cisco and Siemens, with the FBI warning the stolen credentials remain usable. The episode illustrates that the trillion-dollar AI buildout rests on unpaid open-source maintenance labor, and raises the question of who bears the risk when that infrastructure fails.
