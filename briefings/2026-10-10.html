1. Trump trades Russian diesel for midterm votes as Putin stalls Ukraine talks

On Friday, President Trump announced that the United States will import significant quantities of Russian diesel under an agreement with Vladimir Putin. The Associated Press called it a “sharp reversal” of years of US pressure on Moscow, aimed at bringing down fuel prices before the November 3 midterms. Trump blamed Ukraine’s drone campaign against Russian refineries for the rise in diesel prices. Volodymyr Zelensky responded: “Gifts to Putin will not bring peace.” A senior Ukrainian official told the Financial Times that Kyiv will keep striking Russian energy infrastructure, and Al Jazeera reported a Ukrainian drone hit on a Russian fuel site within hours of the announcement.

The same day, envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner met Ukrainian negotiators, the E3 (Britain, France and Germany), the European Commission and NATO in Miami. Witkoff called the talks “constructive” and “productive” and said they focused on ways to de-escalate and end the war before winter. According to Axios, the central proposal is a partial ceasefire covering energy infrastructure and safe passage for grain ships in the Black Sea. In a phone call with Trump the same day, Putin played down any early resumption of three-way talks. The Kremlin said drone attacks on Moscow during last month’s parliamentary elections had undermined negotiations. The New York Times reported that Ukrainian drones closed Moscow’s airports on Friday, forcing government planes believed to be carrying Putin home from Turkmenistan to circle or divert.

Key facts: - Kramatorsk: A Russian strike on buses in Kramatorsk on Thursday killed at least 30 people (AFP), with Ukrainian authorities later putting the toll at 33. Al Jazeera called it the deadliest single attack this year. - Death toll: AP counts more than 50 people killed by Russian strikes over three days. On Friday Russia struck Dnipropetrovsk region nearly 40 times. The UN warned that strikes had killed about 100 civilians in a week: “The current cycle of escalating attacks against civilians must end now.” - Escalation warnings: On Thursday Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the war had reached a dangerous “stalemate,” adding: “The other way that stalemates end is through escalation.” He warned it could spread to NATO countries bordering Ukraine. - Cross-border strikes: A Ukrainian strike killed two people in Russia’s Belgorod region, according to TASS via Reuters.

2. Washington sanctions the International Criminal Court itself hours after Navi Pillay wins the Nobel Peace Prize

On Friday the Norwegian Nobel Committee awarded the 2026 Peace Prize to Navanethem “Navi” Pillay, the 85-year-old South African jurist, “for her efforts to promote peace and international law.” Pillay was an ICC judge from 2003 to 2008 and then UN High Commissioner for Human Rights. She later chaired the UN Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory, which concluded last year that Israel’s conduct in Gaza amounted to genocide. Committee chair Jørgen Watne Frydnes called the prize a message to those undermining international institutions and spoke of a global “shift toward power politics.” Benjamin Netanyahu said the committee had “lost its moral compass.”

Within hours, the Treasury Department put the court itself on the US sanctions list under Executive Order 14203. Until now, sanctions had covered 13 individual court officials. The institution’s US-linked assets are now frozen, and dealings with it are generally barred. Rubio called the ICC a “rogue court” and said: “Either the ICC will end its threats, or we will end the ICC.” The dispute is centered on the court’s November 2024 arrest warrants for Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant.

Key facts: - Treasury licenses: General License 13 lets the court wind down operations, including salaries, rent and utilities, until April 7, 2027. Separate licenses cover software, pensions and services for detainees. - Allied reaction: Canada, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands and Britain issued a joint statement saying they “regret and strongly disagree” with the decision. Norway condemned it separately. The Netherlands has discussed invoking the EU blocking statute, which would forbid European companies from complying. - ICC response: The court called the sanctions an attempt to obstruct justice and asked member states to protect its operations. - Pillay’s current role: She sits as an ad hoc judge at the International Court of Justice in Gambia v. Myanmar, the Rohingya genocide case. - Significance: Imposed on the day of the award, the sanctions show openly that the institutions of “international law” are subordinated to US power once they touch on crimes by Washington and its allies.

3. Iran escalates tanker attacks in Hormuz as Washington hardens nuclear demands

On Friday Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said it had attacked the LPG tanker NV Sunshine for trying to cross the Strait of Hormuz “illegally,” setting it on fire. The IRGC warned that vessels leaving its designated routes would be “punished.” Ali Mohammadi, political deputy of the IRGC Navy, said Iranian forces have been running nightly operations in the strait since the war began. CNBC reports that tanker attacks are at their highest level of the war as Tehran tries to choke off a recovery in Gulf exports. Kpler data show that Gulf oil flows excluding Iran’s recovered in September to more than 81 percent of pre-war levels. On Tuesday, 12 crew members were injured on a Panama-flagged tanker, according to India’s Ministry of External Affairs.

There is no movement on the diplomatic side. Vice President JD Vance has demanded deep cuts to Iran’s enrichment capacity: “If you don’t want a nuclear weapon, then why do you need 60 percent enriched fuel?” Rubio: “Iran cannot have a nuclear weapon, simple, period, end of story.” President Masoud Pezeshkian called negotiations “futile,” pointing to repeated attacks that followed earlier rounds of talks. Trump rejected a proposal Iran presented at the UN General Assembly. Under it, fighting would stop on all fronts including Lebanon, the US would lift its naval blockade and oil sanctions and unfreeze Iranian assets, Hormuz would reopen after seven days, and nuclear talks would follow.

Key facts: - Blockade: Trump says the US naval blockade remains “in full force and effect,” even after promising not to resume strikes before November 3. - Oil reaction: Oil fell early Friday on Trump’s comments about talks, then settled higher as Hurricane Isaias shut in Gulf of Mexico output (see story 4). Brent settled at $104.72. - Wider front: The Wall Street Journal reported escalating Houthi attacks inside Saudi Arabia this week. - Precedent: A June 17 memorandum of understanding briefly eased the fighting before collapsing. The war is now in its eighth month.

4. Hurricane Isaias hits the Gulf Coast and shuts in 70 percent of Gulf oil output

Isaias, the first Atlantic hurricane of an unusually late season, made landfall near Destin on the Florida Panhandle at about 8:30 p.m. local time Friday as a Category 2 storm. The National Hurricane Center initially reported 105 mph winds and later revised the figure to 100 mph. The storm weakened quickly. By early Saturday it was a post-tropical cyclone with 65 mph winds, moving north over southern Alabama at 20 mph. The NHC still warned of dangerous flash flooding from the Panhandle into southern Appalachia.

Key facts: - Power outages: About 668,000 customers were without power around 12:30 a.m. ET: roughly 409,000 in Florida, 234,000 in Alabama and 26,000 in Georgia. - Wind and water: Gusts reached 76 mph in Destin and 72 mph in Orange Beach, Alabama. The forecast called for 2 to 4 feet of storm surge and 4 to 8 inches of rain, with isolated totals up to 15 inches. - Emergency measures: Florida declared a state of emergency in 25 counties, and Alabama declared one statewide. Destin’s evacuation zones A and B were under mandatory orders, and the mayor warned that first responders would not reach anyone who stayed until the storm had passed. The Guardian reported millions under evacuation orders across the region. - Energy: Crews evacuated 129 of 371 Gulf production platforms and 8 of 11 offshore rigs. More than 70 percent of daily Gulf oil production and nearly 60 percent of gas production were shut in, which adds to a diesel shortage already severe because of the Iran war. - Casualties: No deaths had been reported as of early Saturday.

5. Youth revolt spreads from France to Belgium as police violence against minors mounts

On Saturday Reuters reported that French policing is “under scrutiny” as injuries to schoolchildren mount. One case it detailed is Eden, 14, from Argenteuil, whose jaw was broken and several teeth smashed by a tear-gas canister. Teenagers kept up blockades after the government made what Reuters described as unspecified promises about teacher shortages and crumbling buildings. In Belgium, the movement that began on October 1 with school blockades in Liège turned into a national strike day on Friday. Police used tear gas in Brussels, about 140 flights were cancelled, and a second strike is called for Monday, October 12. Police in Liège alone have made 107 arrests.

Spain’s unions have now fixed November 11 for a 24-hour general strike over housing and wages. It will be the first general strike jointly backed by the CCOO and UGT federations in 14 years, and it falls weeks before a snap general election. As Gnana and Lantier wrote on the WSWS, the French intersyndicale has put off national action until November 5, and neither the union federations nor La France insoumise have called a general strike or demanded Macron’s removal.

Key facts: - Belgian cuts: The Wallonia-Brussels Federation’s “program decree” cuts €500 million by 2029. Teachers would work 22 periods instead of 20 with no extra pay, eliminating about 1,300 full-time posts. University fees would rise from €835 to €1,194, and free school meal funding would fall from €21 million to €14 million (WSWS, citing official figures). - Austerity and war: In Liège, protesters denounced cuts made “to invest in war,” linking the education budget to NATO rearmament. - Belgian unions: Teachers’ unions have extended strike notice to October 16. Education Minister Valérie Glatigny (MR) says she will make no concessions and accuses “far-left extremists” of manipulating students. - Spread: France, Belgium, Spain, Portugal (public sector strikes) and Germany (anti-conscription protests) now all have mass movements whose common targets are austerity, militarism and police repression.

Sudan: drone strike on displaced families in Blue Nile

On Friday a drone struck a gathering of displaced people in the Abu Shandi area of Wad al-Mahi locality in Sudan’s Blue Nile state, near the Ethiopian border. The Sudan Doctors Network counted 31 killed and 19 wounded and held the Rapid Support Forces responsible. Four of the dead were children, and six children and eight women were among the wounded. Local authorities gave a toll of at least 30. A UN spokesperson cited reports of at least 40 dead, “most of them women and girls,” and called the attack “horrific.”

  • RSF offensive: The RSF and its ally, the Sudan People’s Liberation Movement-North, have stepped up attacks in Blue Nile in recent weeks. On October 3, authorities accused the RSF of drone strikes on the governor’s residence and civilian sites in the state capital, Damazin.
  • Displacement: UN agencies say thousands of civilians in the area have fled sustained attacks in recent weeks. Nationally, the war that began in April 2023 has displaced about 13 million people.
  • RSF response: The RSF had not commented.

Gaza one year after the ceasefire

A year after the US-brokered 20-point agreement, Israeli strikes and gunfire killed at least eight Palestinians across the Gaza Strip on Thursday and Friday. The IDF said it killed Muhammad Muhsen Nimr Yazji, whom it identified as a commander in Hamas’s Shati Battalion, in Gaza City, and also shot dead a man who crossed the “Yellow Line” in the north. A UN agency says almost all of Gaza’s residents now need shelter, and UN News reports that most women and girls are homeless, as El Niño raises the threat of winter flooding. Press assessments marking the anniversary agree that the deal’s later phases, covering Hamas disarmament, the international force and reconstruction, have stalled.

Israel’s northern fronts: Lebanon and Syria

  • Lebanon: The Israeli army said one soldier was killed and three wounded in south Lebanon on October 9. Israel has held a strip of territory there since its March invasion, and Lebanese officials put the death toll since March above 4,300. Lebanon’s health authorities said an Israeli drone strike on a car near the Syrian border wounded six people, five Syrians and one Lebanese. The IDF said the target was a Syrian operative plotting attacks for Iran. Earlier in the week President Joseph Aoun asked Washington to help stop Israeli attacks, which continue despite a truce.
  • Syria: Israeli troops raided Rasm al-Haydarah in Quneitra province on Friday, a day after advancing into eastern Samadaniyah. The Syrian Network for Human Rights has documented 453 Israeli incursions since 2024, with 69 people killed and 162 detained, 35 of whom remain unaccounted for. The UN’s October 9 briefing described the incursions as intensifying.
  • Link to Iran talks: Ending the Lebanon offensive is one of Tehran’s conditions for a deal, so the occupation stands directly in the way of any settlement.

Netanyahu tries to suppress Haredi anti-draft protests

Thousands of ultra-Orthodox men blocked roads across central Israel on Thursday against conscription. One demonstrator was dragged by a vehicle, and police arrested three people in clashes. An Israeli TV report says Netanyahu asked his Haredi coalition partners to cancel the protests because of the “optics.” The parties replied that the extremist Jerusalem Faction had called them. With an election campaign under way, opposition figures Yair Lapid, Yair Golan, Gadi Eisenkot and Naftali Bennett are attacking the government over draft exemptions. They are attacking it from the right, on the demand that more young men be sent to fight on Israel’s several fronts.

Mexican health workers defy their union with a national stoppage

On October 8 workers at the Mexican Social Security Institute (IMSS) walked out of more than seven hours of talks at the Interior Ministry (Segob) and called a national stoppage without the approval of their union, the SNTSS. On Friday, El Universal reported protests and road blockades in several states. Workers at Clinic 33 in Tizayuca, Hidalgo, blocked the México–Pachuca highway, and unionized staff in Chihuahua occupied hospitals and clinics for seven hours to demand higher wages and supplies. The agreement the SNTSS negotiated gives 2.9 percent on base pay, below inflation, against a 10 percent demand. The national minimum wage rose 13 percent. Workers have also set up tents outside IMSS headquarters on Paseo de la Reforma. A ratification vote run by the union is set for October 14. As Nakai Beltran wrote on the WSWS, delegates called it “not a democratic consultation, it is a rigged one.”

Labour holds Starmer’s old seat on a record-low turnout

In Thursday’s Holborn and St Pancras by-election, called after Keir Starmer stood down as an MP, Labour’s Sagal Abdi-Wali won with 12,292 votes (45.1 percent), a majority of 3,364. Green Party leader Zack Polanski took 8,928 votes (32.8 percent). Turnout was 38.7 percent, the lowest in any English by-election this parliament and 15.4 points below 2024. Labour is presenting the result as a “Burnham bounce” for Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who replaced Starmer in July. George Galloway’s Workers Party won 1.1 percent and lost its deposit. Abdi-Wali came to Britain from Somalia as a child refugee.

Wall Street rebounds as consumer gloom deepens

US stocks recovered Friday from Thursday’s AI-driven selloff. The Dow gained 0.83 percent to 51,660.18, the S&P 500 rose 0.59 percent to 7,811.54, just below its all-time high set three days earlier, and the Nasdaq Composite added 0.64 percent to 27,366.17. All three major indices ended the week higher. Investors were encouraged by Trump’s statement that Iran talks were “productive” and that any attack would wait until after the midterms. Ten-year Treasury yields stood at 5.24 percent and two-year yields at 4.79 percent, levels that reflect the Federal Reserve’s September 16 rate increase under Chair Kevin Warsh, its first hike in three years.

The same day, the University of Michigan’s preliminary October consumer sentiment index fell to 46.3 from 48.1 in September. That is a five-month low and below expectations. The current-conditions gauge dropped to 44.7. One-year inflation expectations rose to 4.7 percent and five-year expectations to 3.5 percent. Share prices sit near records while the population reports some of the bleakest conditions in the survey’s history, a split that tracks the divide between capital and labor.

Bipartisan backlash over Hegseth’s livestreamed execution

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s plan to broadcast the December 3 firing-squad execution of Fort Hood shooter Nidal Hasan drew criticism across the political spectrum on Friday. A Pentagon official confirmed to Newsweek that the execution would be livestreamed. Vice President Vance told the Wall Street Journal he doubted the broadcast would really take place and said he would not watch it. A US archbishop called the plan “barbaric.” Republican and Democratic lawmakers condemned it, and Mediaite quoted critics calling it a “government-backed snuff film.” HuffPost reports the online far right “bays for blood” in support. The UN human rights office has said that broadcasting the execution would violate the absolute prohibition of torture.

In the day’s WSWS Perspective, Evan Blake argues that the plan reveals weakness as much as aggression. Trump’s approval is near record lows, fewer than three in ten Americans support the war on Iran, and support for the death penalty is at 52 percent, near a half-century low. As a result, he writes, the administration “lacks popular support” and turns to the military and to spectacle.

Trump’s approval falls to 30 percent as Republicans fear an economic rout

In a Pew Research Center survey taken September 28 to October 4, 30 percent approve of Trump’s performance and 68 percent disapprove. That is his lowest rating of the second term and close to his first-term low of 29 percent. Among his own 2024 voters, approval has fallen to 67 percent, from 95 percent in February 2025. Among his 2024 voters under 35, it is 49 percent. Just 35 percent are confident in his economic decisions, and 69 percent lack confidence that he keeps business interests separate from presidential decisions.

  • Republican mood: AP reports Republican anxiety that a $1 billion war chest may not be enough. MAGA Inc. held nearly $416 million at the end of August, and Republicans have spent about $500 million since Labor Day with little movement in the polls.
  • Economy: 65 percent of Americans say Trump’s policies have made the economy worse. Among Republicans, the share rating conditions good or excellent fell from 49 percent in January to 36 percent.
