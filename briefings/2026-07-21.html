1. US-Iran war enters tenth consecutive night of strikes

US forces carried out a tenth consecutive night of strikes on Iran, hitting military command centers, missile and drone launch sites, and air defense systems, with target lists now expanding to civilian infrastructure. The war, running since late February, has moved decisively against Washington’s expectations of a quick aerial campaign. Three US service members were killed over the weekend — two at a base in Jordan and one in northern Iraq — and nearly 100 US troops have been injured since July 7. Iranian counter-fire has continued despite ten nights of bombardment.

The conflict is strangling global shipping. Only nine vessels passed through the Strait of Hormuz in the past 24 hours, against a pre-war average of roughly 130 per day. The Houthis declared a maritime blockade, Saudi Arabia vowed measures to protect shipping, Kuwait intercepted “hostile targets,” and Jordan downed Iranian missiles.

As Andre Damon reported on the WSWS, retired Lt. Gen. Keith Kellogg — a former Trump envoy — has now publicly called for a ground invasion: “You’re going to need ground troops to do something… you could take a couple key strategic targets.” Damon notes the Pentagon has concealed casualties, with WSWS placing the toll at least 18 dead and 447 wounded, and frames the ground-invasion proposals as a mark of a failing campaign.

Key facts: - Ten consecutive nights of US strikes; targets expanded beyond military sites to infrastructure. - Three US troops killed over the weekend; nearly 100 injured since July 7. - Hormuz traffic collapsed to nine vessels in 24 hours, from ~130/day pre-war. - Brent crude near $88/barrel; US gasoline back above a $4.00/gallon national average. - Mediators are pressing Tehran to accept a 10-day ceasefire to salvage the June 17 interim deal.

2. Andy Burnham becomes UK’s seventh prime minister in a decade

Keir Starmer resigned and traveled to Buckingham Palace to tender his resignation to King Charles III after barely two years in office, brought down by collapsing approval ratings over economic performance and slow progress on immigration. He is replaced by Andy Burnham, 56, the former Greater Manchester mayor known as the “King of the North,” who becomes Britain’s seventh prime minister in a decade — installed without a general election after winning a single Makerfield by-election in June to re-enter Parliament.

Reform UK’s Nigel Farage demanded fresh elections: “Westminster wants to crown Andy Burnham off the back of a single by-election.” A Conservative lawmaker dubbed him “Keir Starmer with a Northern accent.” Burnham himself warned of Britain drifting “towards the politics of the United States.”

As Chris Marsden wrote on the WSWS, Burnham’s “soft-left” reputation is a marketing device: he will “step up Labour’s agenda of austerity and war,” continuing the militarist and cost-cutting policies that discredited Starmer while offering populist packaging to contain rising social anger.

3. Russia launches largest ballistic missile barrage of the war on Kyiv

Russia fired 41 missiles — including roughly 40 Iskander-M ballistic and Zircon hypersonic missiles — and 125 drones at Ukraine overnight July 18-19, in what Acting Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha called the largest missile barrage on Kyiv since the war began in February 2022. Ukraine says it intercepted 108 drones and 18 missiles. At least one person was killed and 16 wounded in Kyiv, with fires in five districts damaging residential buildings and a dormitory. Separate strikes on Kramatorsk and Zaporizhzhia killed three and wounded nine civilians.

In retaliation, Ukraine claims its drones struck 11 Russian vessels — tankers, cargo ships and a tugboat — in the Azov Sea, an escalation of the naval dimension of the war.

Sources Al Jazeera Russia launches largest ballistic missile attack on Kyiv since start of war · Kyiv Independent Ukraine war latest

4. Zelensky government shaken by protests over sacked defense minister

Daily protests in Kyiv since July 16 are demanding that Volodymyr Zelensky reinstate Mykhailo Fedorov as defense minister and remove commander-in-chief Oleksandr Syrskyi. Fedorov, credited with expanding drone warfare during his six-month tenure, accused Syrskyi of “splitting the country” and engineering his ouster through an ultimatum to Zelensky. Syrskyi is criticized for a centralized, “Soviet-style” command structure; a decision on his fate is expected within days.

As Jason Melanovski noted on the WSWS, the crisis exposes deepening factional warfare within the Ukrainian regime as battlefield attrition and popular exhaustion mount — an internal rupture in a government sustained by NATO backing.

Key facts: - Two core demands: reinstate Fedorov, remove Syrskyi (confirmed by organizer Dmytro Koziatynskyi). - Protests are the largest sustained wartime demonstrations against the Zelensky government.

Sources NBC News Ukraine protests over Zelenskyy’s dismissal of defense minister · Kyiv Independent Zelensky weighing Syrskyi dismissal as protesters plan rally · WSWS Protests shake Zelensky government after dismissal of Ukrainian defense minister

5. Israel continues near-daily strikes on Gaza despite ceasefire

Israel is carrying out near-daily strikes on Gaza in open defiance of the October ceasefire. At least five Palestinians were killed on July 16 in strikes near Tuffah in Gaza City, in a bombed displaced-persons tent camp, and by tank fire in eastern Gaza City. More than 1,100 Palestinians have been killed since the truce took effect, and Israel now controls nearly 70% of Gaza’s territory.

A relative of one victim told Al Jazeera: “The entire people of Gaza have not lived a single day or a single moment of ceasefire. This ceasefire is an illusion.” Reconstruction is projected to cost over $70 billion and take at least a decade, with rebuilding unlikely to begin before Israeli elections on October 27. The promised governance handover to a Palestinian technocratic committee and the proposed international security force remain unimplemented.

Sources Al Jazeera Israeli attacks kill at least five Palestinians in Gaza despite ceasefire · NPR Israel maintains military control over Gaza under Trump ceasefire

6. Venezuela earthquake death toll passes 5,000

The June 24 twin earthquakes (magnitudes ~7.2 and ~7.5) have now killed 5,119 people, mostly in La Guaira state, with 16,740 injured. Roughly 1,000 buildings — including hospitals — plus more than 400 schools and water systems were damaged or destroyed; 80% of La Guaira’s buildings collapsed. The IMF released £346 million in emergency funds and UNICEF sent an initial 47-tonne aid shipment to support more than 100,000 children and families.

UNHCR warned that “as the death toll rises, needs are skyrocketing,” citing prior aid cuts to Venezuela that have limited response capacity — a reminder that years of US sanctions gutted the infrastructure now failing under catastrophe.

Sources Al Jazeera Venezuela quake death toll tops 5,000 as IMF releases emergency aid · UN News Venezuela earthquake: needs skyrocket as toll rises

7. Wall Street sells off on AI valuation fears and war-driven oil spike

US equities sold off on escalating Middle East tensions and warnings that speculation in AI-linked stocks has reached “extreme levels.” Bank of America analysts told clients such conditions have historically preceded a valuation “snapback.” Trump’s remark at the NATO summit that the Iran ceasefire is “over” sent oil prices surging and hit markets sharply. Despite indices near record highs, 61% of the US public says it is pessimistic about the economy — a level of gloom not seen since just after the pandemic.

As Nick Beams noted on the WSWS, roughly 60% of S&P 500 gains have been driven by a handful of AI-tied giants and the top 10 companies now account for 41% of the index’s market capitalization — a concentration he compares to the conditions preceding the 1929 crash.

North Korean foreign minister meets Putin in Moscow

North Korean Foreign Minister Choe Son Hui met Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on July 19, invited by Russian counterpart Sergey Lavrov, with no stated multilateral occasion for the trip — fueling speculation over a fresh Kim Jong Un–Putin summit. Putin reiterated “gratitude to the North Korean leadership and people” for supporting Russia’s military operations against Ukraine. Choe said Kim remains committed to the “comprehensive development” of Russia-DPRK ties.

Kim last visited Russia’s Far East in 2019 and 2023; Putin invited him again at their June 2024 Pyongyang summit, where both leaders signed a mutual-aid pact. The deepening military-economic axis reflects the reorganization of world alliances driven by the US-NATO proxy war.

Sources NPR Putin meets North Korean foreign minister in diplomatic push · World Politics Review Putin, North Korea’s foreign minister, and Kim

Sudan’s civil war deepens Darfur humanitarian catastrophe

Sporadic clashes continue across West Darfur, and more than 3,500 people were displaced from a single village in North Darfur’s Um Baru locality on July 3. The UN’s Under-Secretary-General for Humanitarian Affairs raised alarm over drone attacks on civilians and infrastructure, warning that escalating hostilities continue to disrupt aid operations nationwide. Sudan’s war — among the world’s largest displacement crises — continues to receive minimal sustained Western coverage relative to its scale.

United States

For-profit ICE guard charged with attempted murder after shooting protester

Brandon Booth, 42, a guard at GEO Group’s for-profit Aurora ICE Processing Center in Colorado, faces attempted murder and other charges for shooting at demonstrators outside the facility. The bullet struck 21-year-old protester Emma Landis in the heel; she was hospitalized. The shooting follows a separate case in which an ICE agent with a documented history of head injury and domestic violence was involved in the fatal shooting of 25-year-old Joan Sebastián Durán Guerrero.

The violence is part of a pattern. As reported by the WSWS and Democracy Now!, three immigrant workers have been killed by ICE in eight days — including Lorenzo Salgado Araujo in Houston — and hundreds have protested. ICE captured over 10,000 people in five days at the end of June, more than 2,000 per day. WSWS analyses tie the killings to the privatized detention apparatus and to a Trump administration escalating conspiracies against democratic rights.

Gasoline back above $4/gallon as Hormuz shipping collapses

The US-Iran war has pushed the national gasoline average back above $4.003/gallon as Strait of Hormuz traffic fell from an average 130 vessels per day to just nine over the past 24 hours. The price shock is a direct working-class cost of imperialist military escalation, arriving as 61% of Americans report pessimism about the economy despite record-high equity indices and headline inflation moderating to 3.5%. The mainstream financial press treats the divergence between market performance and mass living standards as a “sentiment puzzle” rather than a class contradiction.

Ebola confirmed in DRC displacement camp

At least two Ebola cases have been confirmed at the Kigonze displacement site in Bunia, Ituri province, in the Democratic Republic of the Congo — a camp housing roughly 15,000 people uprooted by the ongoing conflict in the country’s mineral-rich east. The intersection of armed conflict, resource extraction and collapsing public-health infrastructure creates ideal conditions for the virus to spread among a densely packed, malnourished population. Aid agencies warn that surveillance and containment capacity is severely limited by years of underfunding and the wider humanitarian emergency across the eastern DRC.

US fast-tracks experimental nuclear reactors to power AI data centers

A Trump-initiated push is driving expedited construction of experimental nuclear reactors explicitly to supply power for AI data centers. The program raises acute questions about regulatory shortcuts, the public subsidy of private AI infrastructure, and the subordination of environmental and safety oversight to the demands of the tech industry. Data-center electricity demand — projected to strain grids across multiple states — is being met not by expanded public provision but by bespoke reactors dedicated to corporate compute, a striking illustration of resources marshaled for profit rather than social need.

Arts and culture

The clearest cultural signal of the day comes from Australia, where — as Kaye Tucker reports on the WSWS — a series of major musical theater productions has been cancelled as inflation and austerity crush ticket sales. Rising living costs have priced working-class and middle-class audiences out of live performance, while producers, facing thin margins and no public cushion, pull shows rather than absorb losses. The cancellations are not an aesthetic failure but an economic one: the same cost-of-living crisis driving down real wages is hollowing out the audience base for the performing arts, and the retreat of public arts funding leaves cultural production entirely at the mercy of the market. Theater, historically one of the most socially embedded art forms, becomes a luxury good — a direct cultural expression of deepening class inequality.

A second, sharply political cultural moment came at the World Cup final in New Jersey, where Donald Trump was loudly booed by the crowd, an episode Patrick Martin analyzes on the WSWS. Mass sporting events remain among the few arenas where large cross-sections of the population express collective sentiment unmediated by official channels, and the jeering of a sitting president prosecuting a war and a domestic assault on immigrants registered a broad, inchoate popular hostility that the political establishment and media work to suppress. Such spontaneous eruptions — like the booing of political figures at stadiums in earlier decades — are cultural barometers of a legitimacy crisis.

Roundup: - Theater/economics: The Australian cancellations follow a broader international pattern of regional theaters and touring musicals shuttering as subsidies are cut and disposable income contracts. - Sport as culture: The World Cup final booing underscores the growing gulf between an unpopular ruling elite and mass audiences, and the establishment’s discomfort with unscripted popular expression. - Cultural framework: Both stories illustrate the WSWS approach — treating cultural events not as entertainment or spectacle but as social phenomena revealing the underlying state of class relations, popular consciousness, and the fate of publicly accessible culture under austerity.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s output centers on the Mamdani mayoralty in New York. “Trump Attacks Socialism Because He Knows Americans Want It” argues that the right’s hostility to “democratic socialism” reflects its growing popular appeal — a formulation that conflates the DSA’s Democratic Party reformism with socialism and treats electoral polling as the measure of political progress. “Zohran Mamdani’s City Hall Wants to Build Tenant Unions” promotes a municipal government initiative to sponsor tenant organizing, presenting the capitalist state as an instrument for fostering grassroots struggle. “Jacob Riis, NYC’s ‘People’s Beach,’ Is Under Threat” defends a public beach against luxury development. The consistent line is that the machinery of the Democratic Party and city government can be harnessed to working-class ends — the classic reformist illusion that dissolves the independence of the working class into the capitalist state and its parties.

Left Voice

Left Voice positions itself to Jacobin’s left while sharing its orientation toward the Mamdani phenomenon. “Why Is Zohran Mamdani Endorsing Strikebreaker Kathy Hochul?” sharply criticizes Mamdani for backing the Democratic governor who broke labor struggles — a correct exposure, yet one that treats Mamdani as a wayward ally to be pressured rather than as an agent of the Democratic Party. On the war, “Defend Iran, but Don’t Support the Theocracy” advances a “revolutionary defeatism” formula that in practice adapts to bourgeois nationalism, calling for the defense of the Iranian bourgeois state against imperialism without a clear program for the independent mobilization of the Iranian and international working class against both Washington and Tehran. “ICE Killings in Houston and Maine Should Reignite a Combative Resistance” calls for militant action against the ICE terror. The overall orientation combines genuine militancy with a centrist refusal to draw the sharp organizational line separating revolutionary politics from the pro-Democratic milieu.

Liberation News / PSL

The PSL’s coverage pairs sharp anti-ICE agitation with electoralism. “ICE terror can be stopped — and restarted — at any moment. It’s up to the working class to end it for good” documents that three immigrant workers were killed by ICE in eight days and over 10,000 people were captured in five days, correctly identifying bipartisan congressional funding as the cause. “PSL Statement: Stop Trump’s War on Iran!” opposes the war but frames it in terms of anti-imperialist campism. “PSL Statement: New York is ready for socialism” embraces the Mamdani campaign as evidence of a socialist opening, obscuring its character as a Democratic Party operation. The PSL’s combination of street militancy and uncritical promotion of Democratic “socialists” reflects its Stalinist-derived program of building pressure campaigns rather than an independent revolutionary party.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

The SWP’s coverage includes “Trump ramps up attacks on Iran,” describing the US assault, and “State deepens drive to war on uni and college campuses,” reporting that 35 institutions have joined the Defence Universities Alliance in an expansion of military-academic ties. “‘We won’t be starved out’ — Cancer research workers refuse to bow to bosses’ pay squeeze” covers an indefinite strike at the Institute of Cancer Research. The reporting is factual, but the SWP’s perspective subordinates these struggles to the building of broad protest coalitions and left-Labour pressure, rather than a fight for the political independence of the working class.

Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

The IMT’s British organ leads with “Hundreds dead: Nottingham NHS inquiry exposes deadly maternity care crisis,” a strong exposure of the systemic collapse of NHS maternity care, and “Reform’s ‘shameless and deceptive’ ‘Women Protection Act,’” attacking the far-right party’s cynical legislation. “Solidarity protests rally behind ‘Azad’ Kashmir” backs the Action Committee movement. While the IMT’s exposures of capitalist decay are often sharp, its “Are you a communist?” recruitment orientation and national-protest framing substitute organizational growth and radical phraseology for the construction of a genuine revolutionary international leadership.
