1. Wholesale prices surge as war inflation moves down the supply chain

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Thursday that the Producer Price Index rose 1.3 percent in September. Forecasters expected 0.6 percent, and the increase was more than triple August’s 0.4 percent. Wholesale prices are up 6.3 percent from a year earlier, the biggest annual gain since the turn of 2022–23 (ABC puts it at December 2022, Zacks at January 2023). The wholesale energy index rose more than 10 percent in the month. The figures came a day after the consumer price report and show that the fuel shock from the Iran war, now in its eighth month, is moving into the cost of goods generally. That points to further consumer price increases in the coming months.

The same day, Freddie Mac reported that the average 30-year mortgage rate held at 7.40 percent, its highest since November 2023. The 10-year Treasury yield, which mortgage rates follow, was about 5.10 percent Thursday. Before the war it was 3.97 percent.

Key facts: - Core PPI: Up 0.7 percent in the month against 0.3 percent expected, and 5.0 percent over the year. Trade and distribution margins “expanded significantly,” a sign that wholesalers and retailers are raising prices faster than their own costs. - Retail sales: Up 0.8 percent in September against 0.4 percent expected, after a revised 1.3 percent rise in August. Sales at gas stations rose 6.4 percent, so much of the increase reflects higher prices rather than more goods bought. - Diesel: AAA says diesel averages $6.31 a gallon, about 66 percent higher than when the war began on February 28. ABC profiled an Oklahoma rancher whose operating costs are being eaten up by fuel. - Housing: Since the late-February low of 5.98 percent, mortgage rates have risen 1.42 percentage points, adding about $376 a month on a $400,000 loan. Existing-home sales were flat last year at a 30-year low and are below last year’s level again. - Manufacturing: The Empire State survey jumped to 25.5, its strongest since December 2021. The Philadelphia Fed index slipped to 20.9 against 32 expected. - Politics: ABC reports affordability as the top voter concern going into the November 3 midterms.

WSWS Perspective writer Andre Damon argues that the Democrats are campaigning on this anger while sharing “Trump’s commitment to social austerity.” He also argues that Trump’s Russian diesel deal “will do little” for prices that are driven mainly by the war on Iran.

2. Russia launches more than 850 drones at Ukraine as Washington weighs cutting missile warnings

Zelenskyy said Russia launched more than 850 attack drones between early Wednesday and 9 a.m. Thursday, adding that there was “not a single hour when our skies were clear.” Strikes hit an industrial plant in Kropyvnytskyi and a home outside Dnipro. Russia’s Defense Ministry said Ukraine had resumed strikes on Russian energy infrastructure, including an attempted “massive attack” on the Ryazan oil refinery. Zelenskyy said Moscow “deliberately chose to escalate” once it received sanctions relief. Trump’s “energy ceasefire” has not been confirmed by either side. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Moscow wants not a ceasefire but “lasting peace,” and called the diesel deal and peace talks “separate tracks.”

Semafor reported Wednesday, citing an administration official, that White House officials are discussing whether to stop sharing the US intelligence that gives Ukraine early warning of Russian missile launches. The official said there was no consensus. Trump said Tuesday the US was still sharing intelligence. A Ukrainian defense ministry official told ABC that the restrictions placed on Starshield, the Pentagon-contracted military version of SpaceX’s network, on October 14 “disrupted” a number of “critical missions.” The limits began easing later that day.

Key facts: - Musk’s distinction: On X, Musk separated Starlink, which he said is limited to civilian and defensive use and “cannot be used for offensive military action,” from Starshield, which he said the US government owns and controls. He did not say who ordered the restriction. A Reuters source said SpaceX limited terminal connections, which made Ukrainian drone interceptions and mid-range strikes harder. - Zelenskyy: Asked on Piers Morgan Uncensored whether the disruption was deliberate, he said of Trump and Musk: “I am sure they knew what they did, absolutely.” He called it a big mistake. - ISW: Analyst George Barros said the limits would “significantly degrade” Ukraine’s mid-range strike capability. Ukraine has never been allowed to use SpaceX networks for deep strikes into Russia. - The diesel deal: After a call with Putin, Trump authorized a Treasury license that overrides sanctions. Eurasia Group’s Gregory Brew estimates the deal covers about 74,000 barrels a day, a fraction of what Russia exported a year ago. He said the main purpose is lowering fuel prices before the midterms. - Opposition: On October 13, twelve senators from both parties wrote to Trump asking him to rescind the deal. Republican Sen. Thom Tillis pressed Musk publicly. Poland pays for Ukraine’s Starlink subscriptions, and its Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski wrote: “As your European customers, we are watching you.”

Andre Damon’s WSWS Perspective argues that the Democrats, not Trump, are planning the larger escalation. Rep. Jim Himes, the ranking Democrat on the House Intelligence Committee, told the Warsaw Security Forum that a Democratic Congress would move Ukraine aid in “nine days or nine weeks.”

3. Pentagon prepares “mother of all bombs” option on Iran as Houthis strike Aden palace

ABC reports that senior administration and defense officials have prepared a list of options for Trump as he considers escalating against Iran. One is the GBU-43 Massive Ordnance Air Blast, the “mother of all bombs.” Trump has not decided. He told Newsmax the weapon is “not nuclear at all,” and he has said he will not strike Iran before the November 3 election. A White House official said: “We do not comment on sensitive military plans that may or may not exist.” ABC calls the Strait of Hormuz the “primary flashpoint.”

The Saudi–Houthi war, which Washington is considering joining directly, spread further Thursday. Yemen’s health minister said a Houthi attack on the al-Maashiq Presidential Palace in Aden, seat of the Saudi-backed government, killed five people and injured 16. Saudi coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki said Riyadh’s King Khalid Airport has been attacked 20 times since October 1, and that attacks between October 7 and 11 killed at least 17 people and injured 333. The Houthis say Saudi aircraft carried out 65 air and missile strikes in 24 hours, for a claimed total of 2,388 since the escalation began. ABC could not verify that figure.

Key facts: - Hormuz shipping: Two Emirati cargo ships sank near the strait after attacks, the first such sinkings in months. UK Maritime Trade Operations warned that two container ships had been hit by unidentified projectiles. ABC footage shows containers floating off Oman after a reported sinking. - France: Foreign Ministry spokesman Pascal Confavreux said French military assets to protect Saudi Arabia’s Yanbu East–West Corridor, the pipeline route that bypasses Hormuz, are “currently being deployed.” - Oil supply: The IEA said Persian Gulf crude shipments recovered to 13.9 million barrels a day in September, more than 75 percent of the pre-war level, but warned the recovery is “vulnerable to renewed attacks.” - Sanctions: The administration is reportedly considering easing some sanctions on “shadow fleet” tankers to increase tanker supply, which shows how far the war has strained global shipping.

As the WSWS has documented, Washington has been part of the Saudi war for more than a decade under three administrations. Its current role includes the September approval of a possible $5 billion bomb sale to Riyadh.

4. Teenage gunman kills three children and a teacher at Oregon elementary school

A 16-year-old Bend resident forced his way into Silver Rail Elementary School shortly before 1 p.m. Thursday by shooting through locked doors. He killed three children, aged 5, 8 and 11, and a teacher. A 7-year-old and a 54-year-old teacher are in critical condition. At 12:59 p.m. a driver had called police to say someone with a long gun was shooting at them, and the school went into lockdown at 1:01. A community member saw the shooter going into the building, followed him, and shot him dead. Police have not named the shooter or said whether he had any connection to the school.

Key facts: - Response: The FBI is assisting and ATF agents were reportedly on their way. Police cleared the school room by room, and families were reunited at a nearby church. All Bend-La Pine Schools classes and events are canceled Friday. - Officials: Gov. Tina Kotek thanked the bystander who “heroically intervened to stop further loss of life.” Superintendent Steven Cook called it an “unthinkable tragedy.” - Unknowns: Police said more information would likely come Friday, including the bystander’s identity. Nothing has been released about the shooter’s motive, how he got the weapon, or his history.

The killings came four days after the arrest of a 19-year-old neo-Nazi accused of trying a mass shooting at an Illinois Hindu temple (see United States).

5. Justice Department sues California over mail ballots as powder letters reach Senate offices

On Thursday the Justice Department followed through on its 48-hour ultimatum and sued California. It is asking a judge to order the state to set aside mail ballots returned by people on a federal list of “likely” noncitizens. The suit was filed by the deputy attorney general’s office, not the Civil Rights Division, and relies on the Supremacy Clause. Los Angeles County Clerk Dean Logan refused to open the password-protected thumb drive that held the list. He said it arrived with its password on a sticky note and was not a secure way to send voter data. Secretary of State Shirley Weber called the effort unlawful.

The same day, law enforcement sources told ABC that more than 100 letters containing white powder are in the mail system. Election offices in at least 15 states have reported receiving or being targeted by suspicious mail. Letters addressed to Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, Sen. Chris Murphy’s Connecticut office, Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand’s New York office and Sen. Lisa Blunt Rochester were intercepted. No injuries have been reported. The FBI, Capitol Police and Postal Inspection Service are investigating. It is unclear whether the letters are connected to those sent to state election offices earlier in the week.

Key facts: - DOJ’s claim: The department says it is not seeking a “purge,” only that ballots from listed names be set aside. When Nevada checked 185 names flagged by DHS in an earlier case, every one belonged to a lawful citizen. - Scale unknown: The DOJ letter did not say how many names are on the drive. Reports put it in the thousands. - Courts: California officials point out that the department has lost more than two dozen times in its efforts to get state voter rolls. - Congressional security: The Senate Sergeant at Arms told offices to watch for letters with multiple postage markings. House offices may send district mail to the Capitol for screening.

6. Israel halts commercial goods into Gaza

Israeli authorities said no commercial goods had entered Gaza for two days. COGAT, the Israeli military body that controls access, announced the halt on Wednesday and blamed Palestinian merchants. It said they had stopped shipments in anger at Hamas taxes. “The merchants don’t want to bring goods into Gaza,” said deputy spokesman Lt. Daniel Kantorovich. Nahed Sheheiber, who heads Gaza’s private truckers union, said the opposite: “The merchants are not the ones who stopped the shipments.” The dispute centers on a 10,000-shekel (about $3,226) per-truck payment sought by Gaza’s Economy Ministry. Sheheiber calls it a tax, and the ministry calls it a donation toward civil servants’ pay.

Key facts: - Truck numbers: COGAT says about 250 truckloads a day are entering. The US-brokered ceasefire set a minimum of 600. - Goods affected: Fruit, meat, winter clothing and other commercial goods. Aid agencies bring mainly staples such as flour. - Famine risk: The IPC says conditions have improved since famine was declared in August 2025 but are still at crisis levels, and warns that “even small disruptions to humanitarian access could rapidly reverse recent gains.” Shaina Low of the Norwegian Refugee Council: “The markets need to be functioning.” - Deaths: Gaza’s Health Ministry reports more than 74,000 Palestinians killed, including more than 1,400 since the ceasefire. Five Israeli soldiers have been killed in that period.

Roof collapse at Peshawar school kills 31 children

The roof of a private school in Dir Colony, a crowded central neighborhood of Peshawar in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, collapsed Thursday. There were 110 students inside. Officials first reported 33 dead and later revised the toll to 31. All of the children who died were 13 or younger. Dozens more were injured, some critically, and rescuers kept searching the rubble for trapped children. Police say the building was made of substandard materials and was carrying too much weight. They arrested the owner.

Pakistan’s low-cost private schools fill gaps left by a public system that has been starved of funds for decades. Many run in buildings that are never inspected.

Mauritius extends curfew and threatens terrorism charges over cost-of-living unrest

Protests over water shortages and rising prices began in Mauritius last week. They intensified on Friday, after a police officer shot and wounded a 27-year-old woman in Camp Levieux, south of Port Louis. The officer has been arrested. Prime Minister Navin Ramgoolam’s cabinet has extended the nightly curfew (8:30 p.m. to 5 a.m.) through Friday morning.

  • Arrests: 94 through Tuesday, including 16 minors and a police officer, and 26 more on Wednesday night.
  • Repression: Cases are being fast-tracked, with possible charges under the Prevention of Terrorism Act. Social media is being monitored for “incitement,” and the sale of fuel in portable containers is restricted. A proposed amendment to the Children’s Act would lower the age for detention and prosecution from 14 to 12.
  • The causes: Reservoirs stood at 57.1 percent on October 8, against 87.4 percent a year earlier, and Mare aux Vacoas at 35.7 percent. Inflation rose to 4.1 percent in September from 3.4 percent, driven by petrol and food.
  • Schools: Closed Thursday and Friday.

The unrest is one of a growing number of social explosions in import-dependent countries hit by the war-driven fuel shock.

Maduro pleads not guilty to torture conspiracy in New York

At the Moynihan federal courthouse in Manhattan on Thursday, Nicolás Maduro and his wife Cilia Flores pleaded not guilty to a superseding indictment. Maduro faces one count of conspiracy to commit torture. Flores faces that count and a narcoterrorism count. Prosecutors allege that from around 2013 Maduro “presided over this system of repression and torture” in Venezuelan prisons, including against Americans, because he blamed the US for Venezuela’s instability. Maduro said he “reject[s] this document in its entirety” and called the accusations false.

  • Both pleaded not guilty in January to the original narcoterrorism and drug trafficking charges, after US forces seized them in a military raid on Caracas.
  • His lawyers argue he is immune from prosecution as a sitting head of state.
  • Trial is set for June 14, 2027.

Adding a torture charge recasts a prosecution that began as a drug case into a human-rights case, at a time when Washington is itself running a war that has killed thousands and a detention system the WSWS has repeatedly exposed. The precedent of seizing a foreign head of state by force has not been tested in court.

EU leaders clash over seven-year budget plan

At a Brussels summit on Thursday, EU leaders rejected Ireland’s compromise proposal for the 2028–2034 budget from two directions. The plan is €1.62 trillion ($1.81 trillion), €141 billion less than the Commission’s opening figure. Swedish Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson called it “far too big, far too expensive” and said Sweden’s net contribution would rise almost 60 percent. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said “no member state in the EU can afford to pay contributions” at that level. On the other side, 17 “Friends of Cohesion” states oppose the cuts to farm subsidies and regional funds, which together make up about two-thirds of the budget.

  • Defense is among the priorities that could be scaled back if no money is found, a sign of how rearmament competes with what remains of EU social and regional spending.
  • Budget Commissioner Piotr Serafin warned that the cuts “risk leaving us with less capacity to act together.”
  • Talks resume in late November and at the mid-December summit. Funds must start flowing by January 2028.

Ebola outbreak in Congo passes 9,000 cases with no approved vaccine

The Ebola outbreak in North Kivu and Ituri provinces of the Democratic Republic of Congo has reached 9,066 cases and 4,381 deaths, a case fatality rate of 48.2 percent (CIDRAP, citing Reuters, October 13). Only the 2014–2016 West African epidemic was larger. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, for which no vaccine or treatment is approved. Doctors Without Borders is running the Bravo study to see whether Ervebo, the vaccine for the Zaire strain, gives any protection. So far 857 frontline workers have been vaccinated. CEPI is preparing an adaptive phase 2/3 trial, “Tumaini,” to test Ervebo and at least two Bundibugyo-specific candidates, but it still needs ethical and regulatory approval. Eastern Congo is also a war zone. Health workers are working without an approved vaccine, after years of cuts to international health funding.

HHS moves to let pharmacies compound unproven peptides

On Thursday, Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s Department of Health and Human Services sent an “interim access policy” for peptides to the White House Office of Management and Budget. Sources say it would tell compounding pharmacies they will not be targeted for making certain popular peptides, including BPC-157, which is promoted for muscle growth and healing injuries. The FDA had placed BPC-157 and more than a dozen other injectable peptides on its list of substances too risky to compound. In July, an advisory panel chosen by HHS recommended lifting restrictions on six of seven peptides. FDA staff scientists disagreed, saying none had been studied enough to establish safety or effectiveness. The listed risks include contamination, allergic reactions and tumor growth. Most of the data come from animal studies. Wellness clinic owner Brigham Buhler, who sells the products, called the plan “huge” and a matter of “MEDICAL FREEDOM.”
