1. Starmer resigns as UK prime minister and Labour leader

Keir Starmer resigned on June 22 as British prime minister and leader of the Labour Party, less than two years after leading Labour to one of the largest general election victories in UK history. His resignation was triggered by catastrophic local election losses in May, collapsing poll ratings, and the rapid rise of Nigel Farage’s far-right Reform UK. Starmer will remain as caretaker prime minister until a successor is chosen; nominations open July 9 and close July 16, with a new leader expected before Parliament returns in September. Greater Manchester Mayor Andy Burnham — who has just won the Makerfield by-election and entered Parliament — is the clear frontrunner.

Britain will have its seventh prime minister in ten years, a measure of the depth of the country’s political and economic crisis. As Chris Marsden wrote in the WSWS Perspective, the resignation “was not driven by popular pressure from below but was demanded by the British ruling class” to preserve Labour as an instrument of austerity and war once Starmer himself had become a liability. The ruling class had turned to Labour in 2024 precisely because the Conservatives had become ungovernable; but its majority rested on the thinnest foundation, and Starmer proved unable to deliver on his assigned tasks — stabilizing British capitalism, restoring the US-UK relationship under Trump, prosecuting the NATO war in Ukraine, and suppressing working-class resistance. Trump delivered a parting humiliation, posting that Starmer “failed badly on two very important subjects — IMMIGRATION AND ENERGY.”

Key facts: - Starmer is the UK’s sixth PM since Cameron, following May, Johnson, Truss and Sunak; Labour’s 2024 majority rested on “the lowest share of the vote of any majority government in British history.” - His appointment of Peter Mandelson as US ambassador entangled the government in the Epstein scandal; he failed to secure a meaningful trade deal with Washington while alienating European allies. - He repeatedly flinched before demands to cut welfare and to raise military spending to 3.5% of GDP by 2035; senior military officers publicly attacked him over insufficient rearmament. - Cabinet collapse preceded the fall: Health Secretary Wes Streeting and Defence Secretary John Healey resigned in the weeks before; Streeting immediately stood aside for Burnham. - Burnham has already abandoned his “end 40 years of neoliberalism” rhetoric, declaring himself “not squeamish” about welfare cuts and telling the BBC: “I do agree with what Farage is saying.” - Jeremy Corbyn’s “Your Party,” which drew 800,000 initial supporters, has been demobilized to near-irrelevance; John McDonnell has urged Burnham to readmit Corbyn to Labour.

2. US and Iran agree 60-day roadmap toward a final deal

After 18 hours of talks in Switzerland, the United States and Iran agreed on a “roadmap” to reach a final agreement ending the 2026 Iran war within 60 days. The memorandum of understanding was signed by Trump and the Iranian president on June 17, building on a June 15 framework. Vice President JD Vance led the US delegation; Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad-Bagher Ghalibaf led the Iranian side. Iran agreed to invite IAEA inspectors back, which Vance called “a major milestone” and “the first step in permanently denuclearising Iran,” describing the talks as a “very, very good” first day.

The US issued a 60-day oil-sanctions waiver — covering Iranian crude and petrochemical exports through August 21 — sending Brent crude down 3.5% to $77.70 a barrel. Iran pledged free transit through the Strait of Hormuz, and Washington agreed to lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports for 60 days, with a deconfliction cell established for the strait and a parallel track on Lebanon. Major issues remain unresolved, including uranium-enrichment levels and the disposition of Iran’s highly enriched uranium stockpile. The deal is an MOU, not a treaty — and Israeli strikes on Lebanon during the talks underscore how fragile the framework remains.

3. Israel kills 23 in Lebanon as Al Jazeera cameraman killed in Gaza

The Lebanese health ministry reported at least 23 people killed in Israeli strikes in the 24 hours ending June 22, even as the US-Iran ceasefire framework took shape. The total Lebanese death toll since the war began now stands at 2,869. An Israeli strike hit a civil defense center in southern Lebanon, killing at least six people, including three paramedics, and injuring 22. Lebanese conservationist Mona Khalil, 76, died from wounds sustained two weeks earlier. Israel continues to refuse to end its occupation of southern Lebanon despite the ceasefire framework.

In Gaza, Al Jazeera cameraman Ahmed Wishah was killed in Israeli strikes; his brother Mohamed was killed in April. Israel has now killed more than 260 journalists in Gaza since October 2023, including 12 from Al Jazeera. One IDF soldier was killed and two wounded by a Hezbollah drone in northern Israel, bringing the Israeli military death toll in Lebanon to 22. The continuing slaughter, even as Washington touts a “roadmap” to peace, exposes the gulf between the diplomatic theater and the reality on the ground.

4. Far-right Trump-backed lawyer wins Colombia presidency

Abelardo de la Espriella won Colombia’s presidential election with 49.66% against leftist Senator Iván Cepeda’s 48.70% — a margin of roughly 250,000 votes, with turnout of 26.3 million of 41.4 million eligible voters. De la Espriella, 47, is a millionaire criminal-defense lawyer with no prior political experience, holds dual US-Italian citizenship, and was publicly endorsed by Trump. His 12.9 million votes make him the most-voted presidential candidate in Colombian history.

He has pledged a 90-day military offensive against armed groups, an end to peace talks, restoration of diplomatic ties with Israel, moving Colombia’s embassy to Jerusalem, and a reduction of the state by up to 40%. An Oxford professor called the result “a dramatic ideological reversal” — Colombia’s swing from its first-ever leftist president, Gustavo Petro, to the far right in just four years. De la Espriella takes office August 7 facing a polarized Congress with no majority; public debt stands at 60% of GDP. The result fits a hemispheric pattern of Trump-aligned candidates — from Milei in Argentina onward — reversing the gains of Latin America’s “pink tide,” and follows the failure of left-nationalist governments to break with capitalism or improve workers’ conditions.

5. Bolivia declares 90-day state of emergency, deploys military

President Rodrigo Paz declared a 90-day state of emergency on June 20 — the 50th day of nationwide protests — suspending the right to protest and authorizing military deployment to clear road blockades. For over six weeks, unions, Indigenous communities and coca farmers have blockaded roads across Bolivia, demanding Paz’s resignation and an end to austerity. The trigger was a December 2025 presidential decree ending national fuel subsidies, which set off a severe economic crisis with shortages of fuel, food and medicine — described as the worst in decades amid collapsing domestic energy production.

At least 17 people have died, most linked to lack of medical access caused by transport disruptions, according to the ombudsman and human-rights groups. The protests have threatened to topple Bolivia’s first non-socialist government in two decades. As the WSWS “Workers Struggles” column notes, the Labor Federation’s withdrawal of support was what allowed Paz to impose the “state of exception” — a stark illustration of how the trade-union and nationalist apparatus protects the state against the very workers it claims to represent. The military has been ordered to “restore order.”

6. Cuba approves sweeping free-market reforms

Cuba’s National Assembly unanimously approved 176 economic reforms on June 18-19, backed by the Communist Party and former leader Raúl Castro — described by a London-based Cuban economist as “the most profound reforms since the 1959 revolution.” The measures allow private real-estate development, private banks, foreign investors to acquire stakes in state enterprises without a state partner, and firms to hire more than 100 employees for the first time. State-owned businesses will be converted into commercial ventures with shares and equity stakes — a fundamental restructuring of the property relations established after 1959.

The reforms are a forced capitulation under siege conditions, not an ideological conversion. Cuba produces only 40% of the petroleum it needs; food production is down 60%; medicine supply is at 30% of normal; and infant mortality has risen to 9.9 per 1,000 births under the US blockade. A single Russian tanker recently delivered 730,000 barrels — roughly one month’s supply — and Mexico is seeking to restart oil shipments via private firms. The Western press celebrates “reform” while obscuring that this is economic surrender extracted by decades of US economic warfare, the cost of which is borne by Cuban workers.

7. Alan Greenspan dies at 100

Former Federal Reserve Chair Alan Greenspan died Monday at 100 from complications of Parkinson’s disease. He led the Fed from 1987 to 2006, navigating the 1987 stock-market crash, the 1997-98 Asian financial crisis and the LTCM collapse, and was celebrated during his tenure as “the Maestro.” His legacy has since been substantially reassessed: critics blame his light-touch regulatory philosophy and low interest-rate policy — the so-called “Greenspan put” that socialized risk while privatizing gains — for fueling the subprime mortgage bubble that detonated in 2008.

After the crash, Greenspan acknowledged “a flaw” in his ideological framework — his belief that banks’ self-interest would protect shareholders — in testimony before Congress. It was a rare moment in which a ruling-class technocrat conceded that the theory underpinning decades of deregulation was wrong, even as the working class bore the cost of that error in foreclosures, unemployment and pension losses. His death comes the same week that the WSWS documents insurers reviving precisely the kind of ratings arbitrage that detonated in 2008 (see below), underscoring that the lessons of the crash he helped engineer remain unlearned.

Germany establishes permanent combat brigade in Lithuania

German Defence Minister Boris Pistorius visited Lithuania to observe the first exercise of the Bundeswehr’s Armoured Brigade 45 — Germany’s first permanently deployed combat brigade on foreign soil since World War II. Pistorius pledged the brigade will be fully operational with 4,800 soldiers by the end of 2027, based at Rudninkai near the border with Belarus, and warned that not all deployed soldiers will be volunteers: Germany is prepared to conscript troops for the deployment if necessary. The “Freedom Shield 2026” exercise assembled around 2,900 soldiers — roughly 2,400 of them German — and some 800 vehicles from eight NATO states at the Pabradė training ground.

The visit fell on the 85th anniversary of Operation Barbarossa, the June 22, 1941 Nazi invasion of the Soviet Union that killed at least 27 million Soviet citizens. As Johannes Stern wrote in the WSWS, the choice of date is not incidental but exemplifies the inversion of “Never again” into its opposite, with German militarism once again massing forces on Russia’s borders. Stern reports that Lithuanian authorities have reportedly threatened a memorial to Jewish partisans at the Bundeswehr site.

Trump Reflecting Pool renovation becomes $14.7 million debacle

The Trump administration’s renovation of the National Mall Reflecting Pool — drained and repainted “American flag blue” at Trump’s personal insistence for the nation’s 250th-anniversary celebrations — turned dark green with algae less than two weeks after refilling. According to Interior Department contract information, the cost ballooned from an initial $1.8 million to $14.7 million, with contracts steered without competitive bidding to Trump-connected firms, including one owned by a convicted felon. Officials resorted to hip-waders to vacuum the water and poured gallons of hydrogen peroxide into the pool. Trump claimed the pool had been “vandalized” and said law enforcement was investigating; a former Olympic canoeist who touched a detached piece of pool lining was arrested.

The WSWS’s Patrick Martin called the episode “a case study in oligarchic looting” and a microcosm of the administration: vanity, anti-democratic procedure, crony enrichment, incompetence and scapegoating. The Democrats, he noted, responded only with “waste, fraud and abuse” framing — reducing a systemic crisis of bourgeois rule to an accounting complaint.

AI price-fixing lawsuit targets 1,700+ gas stations

A class-action lawsuit filed in California names BP, Circle K, Marathon, 7-Eleven, Walmart, Albertsons and over 1,700 stations for allegedly using AI software to coordinate fuel prices — adding an estimated 30 cents per gallon and costing consumers an estimated $134 million per penny of overcharge annually. The case is a landmark test of whether antitrust law can address algorithmic collusion, a mechanism by which capital coordinates pricing without explicit human conspiracy. It dovetails with the WSWS’s reporting on AI-driven workforce destruction: Atlassian has cut 1,600 jobs globally and WiseTech Global 2,000, with all four major Australian banks deploying automation, even as official statistics understate joblessness (Australia’s official 4.5% rate against Roy Morgan’s 10.7%). The same technology being deployed to displace workers is now alleged to be extracting wealth from consumers at the pump — two faces of capital’s use of AI against the working class.

H5N1 avian influenza spread continues

The global panzootic of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 (clade 2.3.4.4b) continues to spread through wild birds, poultry and mammals across multiple continents. Recent surveillance reviews underscore the danger: a Lancet-cited review found that human H5N1 infections can be asymptomatic and that limited human-to-human transmission may already be occurring, exposing a critical gap in systems that monitor only symptomatic illness. In a recent reference period, public-health authorities logged hundreds of H5 detections in birds across two dozen European countries and 20 human infections — including four deaths — across six countries (Bangladesh, Cambodia, China, India, Mexico, Vietnam). Experts continue to warn that the virus is a leading candidate to drive the next human pandemic, while US surveillance has been weakened by federal staffing and funding cuts that leave the population dangerously exposed.

Other developments: - Semiconductor sector stress: The global chip rout (Samsung, SK Hynix, Kioxia) reflects an AI-investment cycle now colliding with capacity gluts and export controls — with direct implications for the AI infrastructure buildout and the millions of jobs increasingly tied to it. - AI labor displacement is increasingly visible in corporate restructurings, with technology and financial firms citing automation as a primary driver of headcount reductions, a process the WSWS argues must be met with the demand for social ownership of the productive forces. - Public-health funding: Cuts to disease-surveillance infrastructure in the US continue to degrade the capacity to detect and respond to emerging pathogens, even as H5N1 and other zoonotic threats intensify.

Arts and culture

The 25th anniversary edition of the Tribeca Festival concluded in New York, with Cotton Fever, directed by Daniel Blake Schwartz, winning the Founders Award for Best US Narrative Feature. A quarter-century after the festival was created in the aftermath of September 11 to revive lower Manhattan, Tribeca has become a significant proving ground for American independent film — a sector squeezed between studio consolidation, streaming economics and the collapse of mid-budget production. The festival’s narrative slate again reflected filmmakers grappling with economic precarity and social dislocation, even as the broader industry channels resources toward franchise spectacle and algorithmically optimized content.

In photography, British artist Rene Matić won the £30,000 Deutsche Börse Photography Foundation Prize for the exhibition AS OPPOSED TO THE TRUTH at CCA Berlin — work that interrogates questions of belonging, race and national identity in contemporary Britain. The prize remains one of the most consequential in the medium, and its recognition of socially engaged documentary practice stands against the tendency to treat photography as decorative commodity.

Roundup: - Literature: American poet Sasha Debevec-McKenney won the 2026 Swansea University Dylan Thomas Prize — the world’s largest literary award for writers under 40 — for her debut collection Joy Is My Middle Name. The recognition of a debut poetry collection is notable in a publishing market that increasingly marginalizes verse. - Political fiction: Winners of the 2026 Orwell Prize for Political Fiction are to be announced June 25 at the Bloomsbury Theatre, University College London. Orwell’s name continues to anchor a literary culture that takes the political novel seriously — a tradition under pressure from both market forces and a publishing establishment wary of controversy. - Museums: The Crystal Bridges Museum of American Art (Bentonville, Arkansas) opened a 114,000-square-foot expansion this month, nearly doubling its size — a reminder of how private fortunes (the museum was founded by a Walmart heir) increasingly shape which American art reaches the public. - Film festivals abroad: The Shanghai International Film and TV Festival proceeds under themes emphasizing international exchange and emerging talent, even as US-China tensions and tariff disputes complicate cross-border cultural production.

These developments unfold against a backdrop of intensifying attacks on arts funding and cultural institutions internationally — austerity, defunding and political pressure that fall hardest on independent and socially critical work. The task remains, in the WSWS framework, to defend genuine artistic achievement and the social conditions that make it possible, against both market philistinism and the nationalist instrumentalization of culture.

Insurance companies use rate shopping to boost returns from private credit — Nick Beams

Beams analyzes insurers commissioning private letter ratings on opaque debt securities to minimize required capital reserves — regulatory arbitrage flagged by FT columnist Gillian Tett. US insurance groups now hold roughly $807 billion in illiquid assets (~20% of $4 trillion total holdings), up from 18% the prior year. Columbia University economists found a “10-fold increase in the use of private ratings since 2018,” concentrated among private-equity-owned insurers; conservative estimates suggest the practice frees up $4.5 billion annually in capital that would otherwise be reserved. The Bank for International Settlements has warned of systematically inflated ratings and the risk of “fire sales.” Beams draws explicit parallels to the subprime ratings fraud that precipitated the 2008 crash — a connection sharpened by Greenspan’s death this week.

The ignominious role of the Chilean ‘left’ in Kast’s assault on immigrants — Mauricio Saavedra

Part 2 of a two-part series argues that Chile’s Broad Front and Communist Party directly prepared the ground for José Antonio Kast’s far-right anti-immigrant program by embracing immigration restriction under Boric (2022–2026). Boric’s government conducted over 4,350 expulsions and deployed military to borders; the Communist Party ran Jeannette Jara for president on a platform including “strict border control” and “immediate expulsion,” differing from Kast only rhetorically. Nearly 40% of shantytown families in Chile are immigrants, with poverty rates roughly double those of non-immigrants. Saavedra advances an internationalist position: unconditional opposition to all deportations and border controls, grounded in working-class unity.

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s “Reforming Capitalism Is Not Enough” argues social democracy falls short of genuine socialist transformation and calls for a “class rupture” — a nod to Vivek Chibber’s academic framework. The argument remains entirely theoretical and electoral. The flag: Jacobin’s institutional practice systematically orients toward the Democratic Party (DSA endorsements, Sanders campaigns, AOC boosting); the “dirty break” it periodically invokes has never materialized. “Getting Beyond the Politics of Powerlessness” addresses why left movements stall, invoking Occupy-era lessons, but frames the problem as tactical rather than political — conspicuously avoiding the role of the Democratic Party in demobilizing those movements. “The Hamilton Era Is Gone Forever” offers cultural commentary as a substitute for programmatic clarity. Overall orientation: left-rhetorical cover for a fundamentally social-democratic, Democratic Party–oriented practice.

Left Voice

Left Voice’s positioning against both NATO and Russian imperialism remains the subject of internal debate — its “response to critics” piece signals the position has faced challenge. What the WSWS should cover today

1. Cuba’s forced capitulation: privatization under blockade siege

Cuba’s Communist Party unanimously pushed through 176 free-market reforms — the most sweeping since 1959 — not from ideological conversion but under the coercive weight of US sanctions that have cut food production 60% and medicine supply to 30% of normal. Western framing as “reform” obscures economic surrender under siege; the human cost of the blockade and what these measures mean for Cuban workers demand serious socialist analysis.

Recommended reading CBS News Cuba approves unprecedented free-market reforms

2. The class meaning of Greenspan’s death

Greenspan’s death is an occasion to examine the real consequences of his 19-year reign: the dismantling of Glass-Steagall, the suppression of derivatives regulation, and the “Greenspan put” that socialized risk while privatizing gains. His 2008 admission of an “ideological flaw” was a rare ruling-class concession that the theory behind decades of policy was wrong — but workers paid the price in foreclosures, unemployment and lost pensions.

Recommended reading 10 Things Global News Alan Greenspan dies at 100

3. Colombia’s swing to the far right and Trump’s hemispheric strategy

De la Espriella’s win completes a pattern of Trump-aligned candidates reversing left governments across Latin America. His pledge to end peace talks, launch a 90-day military offensive, and cut the state 40% follows the Milei playbook. His base, his background defending paramilitaries, and the consequences for the Colombian working class deserve treatment as a structural shift, not a horse race.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Far-right de la Espriella elected Colombia president: What’s next?

4. AI algorithmic price-fixing: the California gas-station lawsuit

A class action names BP, Circle K, Marathon, 7-Eleven, Walmart, Albertsons and 1,700+ stations for allegedly using AI to coordinate fuel prices — an estimated 30 cents per gallon extracted from consumers. The case is a landmark test of whether antitrust law can address algorithmic collusion, a growing mechanism by which capital coordinates pricing without explicit human conspiracy.

Recommended reading 10 Things Global News California lawsuit alleges AI fuel price-fixing

5. Czech constitutional crisis as NATO rearmament drives political conflict

PM Babiš barring President Pavel from the NATO summit delegation, over the country’s failure to meet the 2% spending benchmark, shows how NATO’s rearmament demands are fracturing the institutions of member states. The episode illustrates the anti-democratic pressures that militarization exerts on parliamentary forms across Europe.

Recommended reading 10 Things Global News Czech president excluded from NATO summit

6. Global semiconductor rout and the AI investment bubble

Korea’s KOSPI triggered circuit breakers twice as Samsung and SK Hynix each plunged ~12%, with the selloff rippling into Japan and US megacap tech. The episode raises the question of whether the AI-driven equity boom is colliding with overcapacity and export controls — a financial fragility with direct consequences for jobs and pensions worldwide.
