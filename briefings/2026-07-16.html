1. US widens war on Iran with fifth day of strikes and reimposed naval blockade

The United States carried out a fifth consecutive day of airstrikes on Iran this week and reimposed a naval blockade of Iranian ports, sharply escalating a war launched on February 28 to topple the Tehran government and seize control of the Strait of Hormuz. CENTCOM announced it had disabled an empty oil tanker sailing toward Kharg Island — Iran’s main crude-export terminal — the first vessel struck since the blockade was restored. US officials described the latest wave of strikes as intended to degrade Iran’s ability to target shipping in the strait.

Iranian officials said overnight strikes hit an army barracks and other sites, killing at least seven soldiers and wounding roughly 260 people across the country. Government spokesperson Fatemeh Mohajerani said strikes on southern Iran killed more than 30 civilians. Tehran said it retaliated against US military infrastructure in Kuwait, Bahrain and Jordan. More than four months after the war began, the government still stands and the strait remains contested, pushing Washington toward open discussion of a ground invasion. Trump told Fox News, “Sometimes you need a ground campaign.”

As Andre Damon wrote in the WSWS Perspective, the war abroad and the police-state buildup at home are “two fronts of the same war.” He cited a former Pentagon official’s estimate that securing the strait would require tens of thousands of troops at “very high costs,” and noted that a Reuters/Ipsos poll found only 37 percent of Americans backed resuming the bombing.

Key facts: - Pentagon acknowledges 14 US dead and 414 wounded as of Monday; WSWS calls the figure systematically understated. - Internal Pentagon cost estimate runs up to $100 billion (NBC News); Harvard’s Linda Bilmes projects long-run costs above $1 trillion, including $200–300 billion to repair 228 damaged US military sites. - Strait shipping traffic collapsed to roughly 6 vessel crossings in a recent 24-hour window versus 18–22 per day normally.

2. Trump orders ICE traffic stops resumed as fourth immigrant is killed in eight days

President Trump directed ICE to resume the traffic-stop tactics that led to a string of fatal shootings, describing them as “one of I.C.E.’s most important and effective” enforcement tools and ordering agents to “go back and do your very important job.” His directive came as a fourth immigrant death in eight days was reported. The killings included Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, shot July 7 while driving his construction crew to work in Houston, and Joan Sebastian Guerrero, a 26-year-old Colombian father shot through his windshield in Biddeford, Maine.

Guerrero, a delivery driver who lived with his partner and young daughter, was authorized to work in the US and held a Social Security number, according to the Maine Immigrants’ Rights Coalition. ICE agents rammed his Kia and approached with guns drawn; the Department of Homeland Security said nearly 12 hours later that the officer fired “fearing for public safety.” Hundreds of people protested in Biddeford, with demonstrations continuing across the following days.

Key facts: - DHS has refused to identify the agents involved; Rep. Chellie Pingree said the department is stonewalling. - The Washington Post confirmed Guerrero had legal work authorization, contradicting the administration’s framing. - WSWS reporter Kate Randall quoted a Biddeford resident: “A badge and a gun do not give these agents a license to kill.”

3. Rubio vows to dismantle the ICC “brick by brick”

Secretary of State Marco Rubio pledged in a Wall Street Journal op-ed to “dismantle the ICC—brick by brick, if necessary,” urging allied governments to join a diplomatic campaign against the court. Rubio accused the International Criminal Court of “waging a war against our country” through “the force of so-called international law” and described it as “backed and run by a powerful network of leftist nongovernment organizations, smug globalists, and hostile Third World governments united by their enmity toward the U.S.”

The tools cited include travel bans, visa revocations and expanded sanctions. Damon argued the op-ed amounts to the administration’s own admission that immigration enforcement, foreign war-making and domestic political prosecution operate under a single shield of impunity — Rubio explicitly framed the campaign as protecting “Border Patrol agents,” “US Marines” and “federal prosecutors.” Human rights groups said the target is not the ICC but the postwar “rules-based international order.”

4. China’s Q2 GDP misses Beijing’s own target for the first time since COVID

China’s economy grew 4.3 percent year-on-year in the second quarter, its weakest quarterly pace in more than three years and below both the ~4.5 percent forecast and Beijing’s own 4.5–5.0 percent full-year target — the first such miss since China stopped setting formal annual targets during the pandemic. Growth decelerated sharply from 5.0 percent in the first quarter. Analysts attributed the slowdown to sagging domestic consumption, a still-contracting property sector and a difficult labor market, compounded by surging energy and materials prices driven by the Iran war.

Exports remained the standout, rising 17.6 percent in the first half and 27 percent year-on-year in June, while industrial production rose 5.3 percent. But property investment fell 18 percent and fixed-asset investment 5.7 percent over the first half, underscoring the depth of the domestic demand crisis. The data has intensified expectations of new stimulus.

5. Trump to deliver prime-time address on “election integrity”

President Trump announced a 9 p.m. Thursday prime-time address from the White House expected to focus on “free and fair elections,” “recently declassified intelligence” on US elections, and alleged “vulnerabilities in voting machines.” The White House said the speech would also touch on Iran. Reporting indicates Trump intends to amplify long-debunked claims about the 2020 election and press Republicans to pass the SAVE America Act, a strict federal voter-ID measure stalled in Congress, ahead of November’s midterms.

Repeated audits and reviews — many run by Republicans, including Trump’s own former attorney general — found no significant fraud in 2020. Damon pointed to the timing of the address, delivered amid ongoing bombing of Iran, as evidence the administration is constructing an authoritarian apparatus ahead of the elections.

Ebola death toll in DR Congo passes 700

Two months into the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo, the death toll has surpassed 700, with the DRC reporting 1,963 confirmed cases and 719 deaths in data through July 12. Ituri province remains the epicenter, with 1,772 cases and 608 deaths across 26 of 36 health zones. The WHO, which declared the outbreak a public health emergency of international concern on May 16, says roughly 80 percent of new cases in eastern Congo are emerging from unknown transmission chains — a sign the epidemic is spreading faster than health teams can track it.

The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo species rather than the more common Zaire ebolavirus, meaning existing certified vaccines and therapeutics do not directly apply, complicating the response. WSWS medical writer Benjamin Mateus reports the WHO estimates true case counts are two to four times the confirmed figure, and that the international response is losing ground amid inadequate resourcing.

Majority of House Democrats vote to cut Israel aid as Jeffries opposes

For the first time, a majority of House Democrats voted to block military aid to Israel. On Wednesday, 103 Democrats backed an amendment by Republican Rep. Thomas Massie to cut off funding — up from just 37 on a similar vote two years ago — though the measure failed 104–314, with 98 Democrats opposed and 10 voting present. The vote exposed a rare split in Democratic leadership: House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries and Caucus Chair Pete Aguilar voted against, while Minority Whip Katherine Clark voted for.

Jeffries had sent a “dear colleague” letter urging Democrats to reject the amendment as “overly broad,” claiming it would limit humanitarian aid and embassy operations, and arguing “there are more decisive ways to achieve the urgent change necessary when it comes to the far-right Netanyahu government.” WSWS writer Barry Grey argued the maneuver exposes the gap between the Democratic leadership’s activist-facing rhetoric and its continued underwriting of Israeli military operations, noting the vote followed a record AIPAC earmark. The shift in the caucus reflects collapsing popular support for the Gaza war, even as leadership holds the line.

White House seeks extradition of Cox heir arrested in Spain over Palestinian advocacy

James “Fergie” Cox Chambers Jr., an heir to the Cox Enterprises media fortune and a prominent funder of Palestinian and left-wing causes, was arrested on July 10 in Ibiza, Spain, on a US Justice Department request and now faces extradition proceedings. Chambers, who cut ties with his family in 2023 and sold his stake in Cox Enterprises for an estimated $250 million, is charged under a sealed indictment with international money laundering and “material support” for foreign terrorist organizations — charges carrying up to 30 years in prison. Spanish police surrounded his car as he drove with his family before detaining him.

Chambers has used part of his inheritance to fund humanitarian initiatives in Gaza and legal-defense funds for left-wing activists in the US and UK. Supporters, including his partner Stella Schnabel, call the prosecution politically motivated. WSWS writer Kevin Reed presents the case as part of the administration’s expanding use of terrorism statutes to criminalize pro-Palestinian advocacy — targeting even a member of one of America’s wealthiest families.

Minnesota Allina Health doctors authorize strike

More than 130 unionized doctors at Allina Health’s Mercy and Unity hospitals voted roughly 90 percent to authorize a strike after two years of failed negotiations, while Allina hospice workers staged a one-day walkout. The action unfolds amid a proposed merger with California-based Sutter Health that would create a combined $26 billion, 39-hospital system serving more than 5 million patients — a deal both the doctors’ and nurses’ unions oppose as consolidation that suppresses pay and raises costs. The physicians are demanding cost-of-living adjustments after a year without raises, paid sick leave, an end to noncompete agreements, and a voice in how AI is deployed in patient care. The WSWS situates the fight within nationwide healthcare consolidation and the automation threat to jobs.

US measles cases near 2,300, threatening elimination status

The CDC reported 2,231 confirmed measles cases in the United States as of July 9 — just 58 short of the 2,289 recorded for all of 2025, with six months of the year remaining. There have been 32 outbreaks, and 93 percent of cases are outbreak-associated, spanning more than 40 states and the District of Columbia. Officials attribute the surge chiefly to falling vaccination coverage: MMR coverage among kindergartners dropped from 95.2 percent in 2019–2020 to 92.5 percent in 2024–2025, below the 95 percent threshold needed for herd immunity. The CDC warned that summer travel will likely drive further cases, and authorities will formally review whether the US retains its measles elimination status in November.

H5N1 now confirmed on every continent

The dominant H5N1 strain (clade 2.3.4.4b) is now circulating in wild birds, poultry and a growing list of mammals across all six inhabited continents plus Antarctica, the CDC’s mid-2026 global summary reports. Of 1,022 human cases worldwide since 1997, 12 were reported outside the US between August 2025 and June 2026, with three deaths (one in Bangladesh, two in Cambodia). In the US, 71 human cases were identified from 2024 to May 2026, mostly from dairy-cow and poultry exposure. The detection of H5N1 in a Dutch dairy cow marked the first confirmed cattle infection outside North America — a warning sign of the virus’s spread — though the WHO continues to assess the overall public health risk as low, with no sustained human-to-human transmission documented.

Arts and culture

The death of Sam Neill at 78, announced by his family on July 13, marks the loss of one of the English-speaking world’s most versatile screen actors. The New Zealand-born Neill reached global audiences as paleontologist Dr. Alan Grant in Steven Spielberg’s Jurassic Park (1993), but his most artistically substantial work lay elsewhere: as the repressed colonial husband in Jane Campion’s The Piano (1993), in Gillian Armstrong’s My Brilliant Career (1979), and in the paranoid science fiction of Event Horizon and In the Mouth of Madness. Neill, who had announced in April that he was cancer-free after years of treatment for blood cancer, died of pneumonia in Sydney. His career spanned the flowering of Australian and New Zealand national cinema in the late 1970s, and he brought an understated intelligence and moral ambivalence to roles that could easily have been played as caricature. He was also an outspoken critic of streaming-era pressures on working actors.

The death of political cartoonist Pat Oliphant at 90, on July 13 in Santa Fe, removes one of the most incisive graphic satirists of the American century. Australian-born, Oliphant won the Pulitzer Prize in 1967 and for decades skewered US presidents of both parties with a savagery rarely permitted in today’s consolidated, risk-averse media. His work — and the near-total disappearance of the syndicated editorial cartoon as a form — is itself a measure of the narrowing of political expression under corporate media ownership.

Also notable:

  • Censorship upheld: An Australian Federal Court dismissed pianist Jayson Gillham’s discrimination suit against the Melbourne Symphony Orchestra, which cancelled his 2024 concert after he dedicated a piece to “the journalists of Gaza” and denounced their “targeted assassinations.” Justice Graeme Hill ruled the MSO’s cancellation was not substantially motivated by the political content, citing a “custom” that classical musicians do not make political statements from the stage without approval — a chilling institutional codification of self-censorship. WSWS writer Oscar Grenfell situates the ruling within a wider pattern of suppressing pro-Palestinian expression in Australia.
  • Film and colonialism: Jacobin’s critique of Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey, filmed in occupied Western Sahara, notes how the production’s use of Moroccan-controlled territory lends de facto legitimacy to a decades-long colonial occupation — a reminder that the logistics of blockbuster filmmaking are never politically neutral.
  • Music: Welsh singer Bonnie Tyler, whose gravel-voiced 1970s and ’80s hits made her a global star, died July 8 at 75 following surgery in Portugal.

The article situates Bavi within intensifying climate-driven disasters, noting it followed Super Typhoon Sinlaku across the same islands in mid-April, and indicts the subordination of disaster preparedness to militarization and profit.
