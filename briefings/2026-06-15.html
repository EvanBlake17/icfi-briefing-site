1. US and Iran reach framework deal to end war and reopen Strait of Hormuz

The United States and Iran announced a 60-day ceasefire on Sunday, June 14, suspending a war that began February 28, 2026, with US-Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and two senior IRGC commanders. Pakistan brokered the agreement; PM Shehbaz Sharif announced the “permanent termination of military operations on all fronts,” including Lebanon. A formal memorandum of understanding is set for signing Friday in Geneva/Switzerland by Vice President JD Vance.

The framework provides for the US lifting its naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, full withdrawal of US and Iranian forces, suspension of sanctions, and the release of $24 billion in frozen Iranian assets over 60 days, with nuclear negotiations to follow. Iran’s blockade, in place since April 13, had shut roughly 20 percent of global oil supply. As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, the Trump administration achieved “none of its stated aims” — regime overthrow, destruction of the nuclear program, dismantling the military, or seizing the strait — and the ceasefire represents a humiliating failure of imperialist objectives after a two-month blockade failed to force surrender.

Key facts:

  • The Iranian health ministry put the death toll of the bombing campaign at at least 3,468 people; the blockade ran approximately 100 days.
  • Iran’s deputy foreign minister Kazem Gharibabadi confirmed the agreement; implementation does not begin until signing.
  • This is the second Iran ceasefire; a June 2025 deal ending the “Twelve-Day War” held until February 2026.
  • Democrats, led by Rep. Seth Moulton, called the framework “basically a surrender document.”
  • Israeli strikes on Hezbollah in Beirut Sunday delayed the announcement by hours; Trump said the action “should not have happened.”

2. Russia launches massive overnight barrage on Ukraine, killing 11 and burning a UNESCO monastery

Russia fired 70 missiles and 611 drones across Ukraine overnight June 14–15, in one of the heaviest single barrages of the war. Ukrainian air defenses reported intercepting 632 aerial targets. At least 11 people were killed and 53 wounded; in Kyiv five died and more than 30 were wounded, including children aged five and six. Five rescuers were killed in Kharkiv when a second Russian strike hit them as they fought a fire started by the first — a “double-tap” tactic, according to Interior Minister Ihor Klymenko.

The roof of the Dormition Cathedral at the 11th-century Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra (Monastery of the Caves), a UNESCO World Heritage Site, caught fire. Metropolitan Epiphanius condemned the strike as a crime “against humanity, against history, against Christianity”; French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot compared it to “bombing Notre Dame in Paris.”

Key facts:

  • The attack came hours after Trump phoned Putin and Zelensky separately on Sunday, ahead of the G7 summit.
  • Zelensky said: “This is how Russia shows the world its intention to continue the war,” and called on the G7 for “decisive and substantive” action and more air defense.
  • Of the intercepts, Ukraine counted 50 missiles and 582 drones downed.

3. G7 summit opens at Évian-les-Bains as 20,000 protest behind 4,000 troops

The 50th G7 summit opened June 15 in Évian-les-Bains, France, running through June 17 — Trump’s first G7 since the US-Iran war began. France shifted the dates to accommodate Trump’s 80th-birthday celebration on Sunday, which featured an MMA event on the White House South Lawn attended by roughly 4,300 people. The Iran ceasefire, Ukraine peace prospects, and global trade imbalances under US tariffs dominate the agenda.

The UK, France, Germany, and Italy issued a joint statement declaring that “Iran must never acquire a nuclear weapon,” while offering sanctions relief in exchange for steps on Tehran’s nuclear program. An estimated 20,000 protesters demonstrated against the summit; police deployed tear gas and water cannons against roughly 600 Black Bloc participants, and Switzerland mobilized 4,000 troops to ring the area. As the WSWS and others have noted, corporate coverage reduced a mass anti-war mobilization to a story about “violence.”

4. Israel defies Trump, strikes Beirut and declares indefinite occupation of Lebanon and Syria

Hours before the US-Iran announcement, Hezbollah launched several drones toward northern Israel and Israel responded with a strike on a Hezbollah command center in Beirut, delaying the ceasefire announcement. Trump publicly rebuked Netanyahu, saying the Beirut strike “should not have happened,” while insisting the deal remained “complete.”

Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz then declared that Israel opposes any withdrawal from Lebanon and intends to remain “indefinitely” in lands it currently holds in Lebanon, Syria, and Gaza — a public declaration of annexation that directly undermines the regional ceasefire framework. “If Iran attacks Israel due to events in Lebanon, we will strike it with full force,” Katz said. The statement goes further than the Netanyahu government’s position before the war began, and threatens to doom the broader settlement even as it is signed.

5. World Bank says Middle East war has cut global growth to the lowest rate since COVID

The World Bank’s June 2026 Global Economic Prospects report, released June 11, cut the 2026 global growth forecast to 2.5 percent, down from 2.9 percent in 2025 — the slowest pace since the pandemic. Forecasts for two-thirds of all economies have been downgraded since January. The Bank attributes the deterioration directly to the Strait of Hormuz closure, which drove sharp increases in energy and fertilizer prices, intensifying global inflation and raising the threat of a food crisis across the Global South.

Key facts:

  • The Middle East and North Africa region was cut most sharply — from 4.0 percent growth in 2025 to just 1.6 percent in 2026, a 2.7-point downgrade.
  • Developing economies as a whole are forecast at a post-pandemic low of 3.6 percent.
  • Worst case: if energy disruptions worsen alongside financial stress, global growth could collapse to 1.3 percent.

6. Elon Musk becomes world’s first trillionaire on the eve of the UAW convention

On June 12, 2026, Elon Musk became the world’s first trillionaire following SpaceX’s stock-market debut — the company began trading at $150 a share after a $75 billion IPO, the largest ever, valuing SpaceX above $1.7 trillion. The milestone — “a thousand billions or a million millions” — was widely reported as a marker of historically unprecedented wealth concentration, arriving as the United Auto Workers opened its Constitutional Convention. The Socialist Equality Party’s Perspective links the two facts causally: every dollar in concessions extracted from autoworkers over four decades, it argues, financed the accumulation now concentrated at the apex of the capitalist class.

Key facts:

  • UAW membership has fallen from 1.5 million in the late 1970s to roughly 370,000 today, with autoworkers only half that figure.
  • Real top assembly wages remain roughly where they stood in 1978; average real auto wages fell over 19 percent between 2008 and 2023.
  • The SEP dates the turn to 1979, when UAW President Douglas Fraser accepted Chrysler concessions and joined its board of directors.

Armenia’s pro-Western PM Pashinyan wins parliamentary election

Armenia’s Central Election Commission confirmed June 14 that PM Nikol Pashinyan’s Civil Contract party won 49.81 percent of the June 7 vote, taking 61 of 105 parliamentary seats. The pro-Russian Strong Armenia party came second at 23.29 percent and has appealed for annulment. Russia imposed trade restrictions on Armenia in the days before the vote in an apparent attempt to influence the outcome. The OSCE said the result reflected “genuine voter choice” but cited divisive campaign rhetoric. The win cements Armenia’s pivot toward the EU and away from Russian-dominated institutions, including its suspended participation in the CSTO. Absent from the celebratory framing: the 100,000-plus ethnic Armenians expelled from Nagorno-Karabakh in 2023 who remain displaced.

Philippines earthquake toll passes 61 as the seabed rises two meters

Updated figures as of June 15 show 61-plus confirmed dead and more than 40 still missing from the magnitude 7.8 earthquake that struck near General Santos City, Mindanao, on June 8–9; 456-plus are injured and 145,693 people affected. Over 1,100 aftershocks have been recorded. New scientific findings reported June 15 show the seabed along the Sarangani and Davao Occidental coasts was raised up to two meters, with shorelines extended outward by 200 meters — exposing coral reefs, seagrass beds, and dead marine life on which coastal fishing communities depend. The quake struck on the first day of the Philippine school year; collapsed roads and bridges and disrupted power and cell networks have hampered relief.

Switzerland rejects far-right 10-million population cap

Swiss voters rejected a far-right initiative to constitutionally cap the population at 10 million, with 55 percent against and 45 percent in favor on 60 percent turnout. The proposal, backed by the Swiss People’s Party (SVP/UDC), the country’s largest party, would have required Switzerland to exit the EU’s free-movement agreement had the population — currently 9.1 million — approached the cap, threatening single-market access. Justice Minister Beat Jans called the result “a sign of stability, openness and reliability.”

Sources BBC / The Guardian Swiss voters reject 10-million population cap

US and Venezuela conduct joint strike on Tren de Aragua leader

A joint US-Venezuela operation killed Hector Rusthenford Guerrero Flores, described as a senior leader of the Tren de Aragua transnational gang — a rare instance of direct military cooperation under the Maduro government. The Trump administration has designated Tren de Aragua a foreign terrorist organization and used that designation to invoke emergency immigration powers, justify deportation flights to El Salvador’s CECOT prison, and target migrant communities. No casualty figures or operational details were released.

Hong Kong files 25 manslaughter charges over the Wang Fuk Court fire

Hong Kong police and the Independent Commission Against Corruption filed 25 counts — including manslaughter, conspiracy, money laundering, perverting the course of justice, and tax evasion — against seven individuals and two firms (a project consultancy and the main contractor) over the November 26, 2025 Wang Fuk Court fire. The blaze, fed by substandard mesh netting and flammable foam insulation wrapped around the towers for a renovation, killed 168 people and displaced roughly 1,800 households (about 6,000 people). As Erik Long wrote on the WSWS, the prosecutions, while warranted, obscure the capitalist housing system that forced working-class families into fire-trap conditions — what Engels termed “social murder.”

Sudan remains the world’s third-deadliest conflict, largely unreported

Sudan recorded 12,200 conflict deaths in 2025, making it the world’s third-deadliest conflict, yet it draws a fraction of the coverage given Ukraine or Iran. Continued drone attacks and escalating fighting are deepening one of the world’s worst humanitarian crises, with millions displaced. The relative silence reflects the absence of a major Western power with a public-relations interest in the story.

Black teenager sentenced to 35 years in Karmelo Anthony case

Karmelo Anthony, a Black 17-year-old, was found guilty of murder on June 9 and sentenced to 35 years in prison for the April 2025 stabbing of Austin Metcalf, also 17, at a Texas track meet — a confrontation witnesses say Metcalf initiated after telling Anthony to move from underneath his team’s tent during a downpour. A Collin County jury deliberated about three hours and rejected the defense’s “sudden passion” argument, which could have reduced the sentence below 20 years. The jury had no Black members despite Frisco being roughly 10 percent Black. As Dan Conway argued on the WSWS, the sentence — set against the acquittals of Kyle Rittenhouse, Daniel Penny, and George Zimmerman — illustrates a two-track justice system: maximum sentences for working-class youth of color, leniency where violence serves ruling-class political interests.

US government forces shutdown of Anthropic’s most powerful AI models

The Commerce Department, via Secretary Howard Lutnick, gave Anthropic 90 minutes to comply with an order shutting down Claude Fable 5 and the underlying Mythos 5 model — the first government-imposed shutdown of a deployed frontier AI system. Officials cited a “jailbreak” technique enabling the model to assist with discovering software vulnerabilities, invoking national security; Anthropic countered that “the same capability is available from other models, including OpenAI’s GPT-5.5.” Security researcher Katie Moussouris called the move “an own goal.” As Evan Blake wrote on the WSWS, the action functions as a de facto state licensing regime over advanced AI, intended to preserve US advantage over China’s open-weight models — and it sits in tension with a June 2 White House executive order that explicitly prohibited mandatory preclearance.

US measles cases near 2025’s full-year total at the half-year mark

As of June 11, the CDC reported 2,073 confirmed measles cases across 40 jurisdictions, with 30 active outbreaks; 93 percent are outbreak-associated and 89 percent involve unvaccinated or unknown-vaccination-status individuals. With six months remaining, the US is nearly matching the full-year 2025 total of 2,288 cases — evidence of a sustained collapse in herd immunity.

Sources CDC Measles cases and outbreaks

First human H5N5 infection reported; H5N1 hits poultry in 12 states

The New England Journal of Medicine reported the first known human H5N5 infection — a 75-year-old immunocompromised woman (Waldenström macroglobulinemia, on rituximab) who developed acute hypoxemic respiratory failure; no secondary transmission was detected. Separately, HPAI H5N1 is now confirmed in commercial poultry across 12 states, with mandatory depopulation pushing cumulative bird losses past 100 million and keeping egg and poultry prices elevated. Human risk from H5N1 remains low but is being monitored in farm workers.

Ebola outbreak in DRC and Uganda reaches 708 cases, 141 deaths

As Benjamin Mateus reported on the WSWS, nearly a month after a public health emergency was declared, the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak across the DRC and Uganda has reached 708 confirmed cases and 141 deaths, with contact tracing collapsed to 28 percent — far below the threshold for containment. CDC modeling shows a 65 percent probability the outbreak exceeds 20,000 cases within three months under poor-isolation scenarios. Over half of children under five in Ituri Province suffer chronic malnutrition; the USAID shutdown is cited as responsible for more than 600,000 preventable deaths, mostly children.

Other developments:

  • Finerenone: The Phase III FIND-CKD trial (presented at the European Renal Association Congress in Glasgow, June 5; published simultaneously in NEJM and JAMA) showed finerenone significantly improved eGFR slope and reduced kidney-cardiovascular events in non-diabetic CKD, extending its benefit to a large, previously underserved population.
  • COVID-19: Activity is low — declining in 38 states, growing in only 2. The dominant variant is XFG (“Stratus”), an Omicron descendant; the CDC projects ~1,000 new hospital admissions for the week ending June 13.
  • AI regulation: Colorado’s AI Act takes effect June 30, the first comprehensive US state AI law, requiring algorithmic-discrimination impact assessments and consumer disclosure for high-risk systems.

Arts and culture

The two dueling spectacles of June 14 — Trump’s UFC cage match on the White House lawn and a Democratic celebrity fundraiser in Manhattan — together offer a portrait of cultural life under a decaying social order. The “Rise Up, Sing Out: A Concert for the First Amendment” in New York, featuring Jane Fonda and Julia Roberts with tickets over $330, was staged as the liberal counterpoint to the gladiatorial brutalism downtown. As Barry Grey argued on the WSWS, both events express “the collapse of American democracy and the depravity of American capitalism in its death agony” — each party performing for its own constituency while workers have no political representation. The contrast is itself a cultural document: mass entertainment reduced to either sanctioned violence or self-congratulatory philanthropy, neither speaking to the social crisis engulfing the population.

The most substantive cultural criticism of the day came in Erik Schreiber’s WSWS review of the 2024 Spanish film Rich Flu, directed by Galder Gaztelu-Urrutia (The Platform). In the film, a mysterious disease begins killing the wealthy, forcing a media executive and her family through the dehumanizing conditions normally reserved for migrants and the dispossessed. Schreiber praises the film’s “unblinking” social commentary and Buñuel-esque absurdism — a genuine artistic engagement with class inequality — while faulting its attribution of inequality to individual “greed” rather than the structural logic of capitalism, and its utopian resolution in “small, self-sufficient, sustainable settlements,” which he reads as a regression from collective social organization. The review’s verdict is characteristic of the WSWS approach: the film reflects authentic mass opposition to injustice finding artistic form, even as its conclusions remain trapped within the horizon of bourgeois social thought.

Beyond these, the cultural sphere remains marked by the pressure of war and repression:

  • The destruction of the Dormition Cathedral roof at the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra (see Top stories) is a cultural-heritage atrocity as much as a war crime — an 11th-century UNESCO monument set ablaze, drawing comparisons to Notre Dame and underscoring how imperialist war consumes the accumulated cultural inheritance of humanity.
  • In Britain, the legal ban on Palestine Action — upheld this week and contested by its co-founder — continues a pattern of state criminalization of political art and protest, a question with direct implications for cultural and democratic rights (covered below in the pseudo-left review and coverage suggestions).
  • The reduction of major broadcast and streaming infrastructure to instruments of a billionaire-allied political project — Paramount+’s livestream of the White House cage match — illustrates the subordination of mass culture to oligarchic interests.

These developments confirm the WSWS framework: culture is not a neutral leisure category but a contested terrain in which the social crisis, the drive to war, and the concentration of wealth find expression — and against which genuine artistic truth-telling, as in Rich Flu, retains a critical charge.

Capitalist oligarchy, the UAW and the class struggle — SEP

Beyond the wage data cited above, the Perspective develops a specific critique of UAW President Shawn Fain. It argues his 2022–23 election was marked by systematic disenfranchisement — fewer than 10 percent of members voted, and Fain won “by only a few hundred ballots” — so that his more combative rhetoric masks a continuation of bureaucratic suppression rather than a rank-and-file insurgency. The piece traces the union’s degeneration to its integration into the capitalist state after World War II, and frames the 1979 Fraser board appointment as a qualitative break: the formal abandonment of any adversarial relationship to management. Its programmatic conclusion is unambiguous — rank-and-file committees independent of the apparatus, coordinated through the International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees (IWA-RFC); nationalization of the auto corporations under workers’ democratic control; and the international coordination of the working class. The Constitutional Convention is presented as a juncture: either workers begin breaking from the apparatus, or the next concessions cycle begins.

USPS workers discuss four deaths at Palmetto facility — Our Reporters

The USPS Workers Rank-and-File Committee held an online public meeting on four worker deaths — Shannon Barnes, Eric Smith, Russell Scruggs Jr., and Demarcus Little — at the Palmetto, Georgia facility over two years. The facility operated without written safety protocols, no defibrillators for 1,000-plus employees, cell-signal blockage that prevented 911 calls, and inadequate first-aid supplies. The meeting situated these deaths in an international privatization drive: Royal Mail lost 20,000 workers after its 2013 privatization, and Canada Post plans to cut staff by up to two-thirds. The committee called for organization independent of the APWU and NALC, coordinated internationally. This is a WSWS-only story with no bourgeois coverage — a documented pattern of fatal conditions at a logistics hub that the corporate press has ignored.

Chile’s Kast government seizes student debtors’ savings with Boric-era laws — Mauricio Saavedra

Over 1,500 workers have had bank accounts frozen for student-loan (CAE) debts; 693,710 of 1.28 million CAE debtors are delinquent, and collections rose 295 percent in early 2026 versus 2025. Outstanding CAE debt totals 4 trillion CLP (~$4.4 billion), 46.7 percent of it held by the lowest two income quintiles, while courts have ruled 74 percent of protection actions inadmissible. The article’s central political point: the fascistic Kast government is wielding legislation passed under the “progressive” Boric administration against workers — demonstrating the class continuity across Chile’s political spectrum and the futility of reformist politics.

Australia: Officeworks sacks hundreds of workers — Antony Walsh

Officeworks (owned by Wesfarmers, $23.5 billion half-year revenue) is eliminating customer-service and back-office positions and shifting operations to Manila and Bengaluru — posting roughly 50 offshore positions — despite reporting $212 million profit on $3.5 billion revenue in 2025. About 40 percent of staff are aged 20–30, against Australian youth unemployment of 11.1 percent. Walsh argues the trade unions have been complicit and calls for worker ownership of major corporations.

New Zealand train crash exposes inadequate safety systems — Our correspondent

On June 6 a commuter train travelling at 30 km/h struck a concrete buffer at Khandallah station, Wellington, after diverting onto a dead-end siding, injuring six of nine passengers (including three children). The article identifies it as potentially the fourth signal-passed-at-danger incident in 12 months on Wellington services, and notes that implementing the European Train Control System would cost $500–750 million while the government allocated only $106.9 million for “unspecified upgrades” — a product of privatization and austerity prioritizing military spending over public safety.

ICE escalates violent enforcement operations across Detroit Metro Area — Lawrence Porter (June 14)

Two documented incidents: on May 19, Venezuelan asylum seeker Yerlys Moreno López (who entered legally in 2024) was subjected to a high-speed chase, tackled after a collision, and suffered a fractured kneecap, lacerations requiring eight stitches, and a head injury requiring emergency surgery — her family refused hospital access under apparent ICE pressure. On June 4, 23-year-old Mauritanian asylum seeker Mohammad Salim Abdessamed was pursued by plainclothes agents in unmarked vehicles; his vehicle crashed through a fence and was impaled by a metal pole. Porter notes Michigan has two Democratic senators and a Democratic governor yet no meaningful opposition, arguing that “the defense of immigrant workers is inseparable from the defense of democratic rights.”

  • Left Voice — frames the Iran war through “US hegemony in decline,” correct on imperial overreach but evasive on revolutionary perspective

Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin’s front page led with “ICE Is Targeting the Lawyers of Immigrant Children,” documenting the administration’s move to freeze payments and demand client data from attorneys representing unaccompanied minors. The reporting is useful, but the framing remains within a civil-liberties register that looks to the courts and legal profession rather than to the independent mobilization of the working class. “How Penn Graduate Workers Got Their Union Contract” presents a “high-participation, worker-led” model of organizing — genuine in its enthusiasm for shop-floor activity, but tethered to the existing union apparatus and to the Democratic-aligned labor officialdom whose role Jacobin never interrogates. A third piece, “Europe Doesn’t Know How to Respond to China,” offers competent political economy on the EU’s dependence on low-wage labor, but treats inter-imperialist rivalry as a policy puzzle rather than a driver toward war.

Assessment: On a day dominated by the Iran ceasefire and the G7, Jacobin’s silence on both is telling. The DSA’s orientation remains domestic, reformist, and oriented toward pressuring the Democratic Party — the war that has killed thousands and reshaped the world economy does not register on its front page.

Left Voice

Left Voice’s central war analysis, “Trump’s War in Iran Exposes the Fragility of U.S. Hegemony,” argues that the administration’s belief it could wage a quick, clean campaign “shows that the United States doesn’t have the strategic advantages and power that it thought it had,” and points to imperial overextension across multiple fronts. The diagnosis of US decline is correct as far as it goes and overlaps with the WSWS’s assessment of a failed imperialist adventure.

What is missing is the decisive element: a perspective for the international working class as the social force to end the war. Left Voice’s Morenoite tradition tends to dissolve revolutionary strategy into “dynamics” and “fragility,” analyzing imperialism’s weaknesses without advancing the construction of a revolutionary leadership in the ICFI sense. Its periodic adaptation to Democratic-aligned protest milieus and identity frameworks remains the political ballast on its analysis.

PSL / Liberation News

The PSL statement, “Why Trump failed in his criminal war against Iran — and why we need to keep up the pressure,” correctly states that Trump achieved none of his war aims and ridicules his “regime change” claims. But its political conclusion — that “public pressure” forced the retreat and must continue — promotes illusions in protest politics and, more dangerously, lends uncritical support to the Iranian bourgeois state as an “anti-imperialist” resistance. This is the characteristic error of the PSL’s Stalinist-derived campism: it substitutes solidarity with capitalist regimes opposed to Washington for the independent mobilization of the Iranian and international working class against both imperialism and the Tehran bourgeoisie.

Assessment: The PSL reads the ceasefire as a popular victory, obscuring that the deal is an imperialist arrangement that leaves the Iranian masses subordinated to their own ruling class.

SWP (UK)

Socialist Worker led with “Cage fights, forever wars and epic failures: Trump-Iran deal shows US empire in decline,” the sharpest anti-imperialist framing among the tendencies surveyed, linking the White House spectacle to imperial decay. It also ran “Palestine Action co-founder vows to fight ban after judges rule it ‘lawful’” — defending democratic rights against the Labour government’s proscription — and “Activists in Unison union build vote for pay strikes.” The anti-war and anti-repression content is real, but the SWP’s perspective channels these struggles back into the existing trade-union machinery and the orbit of the Labour left, the very forces enforcing austerity and complicit in the suppression it describes.

Assessment: Strong on rhetoric against war and repression; structurally committed to pressuring the Labour bureaucracy rather than building an independent revolutionary party.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Israel’s declared “indefinite” occupation of Lebanon and Syria

Defense Minister Katz’s blunt statement that Israel will remain permanently in seized Lebanese, Syrian, and Gaza territory amounts to a public declaration of annexation that goes beyond the pre-war Netanyahu line and directly threatens the US-Iran ceasefire. This deserves dedicated analysis of how the ceasefire’s contradictions are already unraveling and what it reveals about the deeper aims of Israeli and US imperialism in the region.

Recommended reading ABC News Israel won’t leave Lebanon after US-Iran MOU, minister says

2. The energy shock is fueling a global hunger crisis

The World Bank directly links the Hormuz closure to spiking fertilizer prices and a rising food-crisis threat across the Global South, even as energy profits surge. The human cost — food insecurity in Pakistan, Egypt, and sub-Saharan Africa — has gone largely unreported amid the market relief rally. A class analysis connecting war, profit, and hunger is urgently needed.

Recommended reading World Bank Global Economic Prospects, June 2026

3. 20,000 march against the G7 as 4,000 troops ring the summit

The mass anti-war mobilization outside Évian-les-Bains — met with tear gas, water cannons, and a 4,000-troop deployment — represents the largest anti-G7 demonstration in years, yet the corporate press reduced it to “violence.” The political content (opposition to the Iran war, Ukraine arms escalation, and austerity) demands coverage that recovers the voice of the protesters.

Recommended reading 10 Things Global News G7 summit protests, June 15, 2026

4. UK to ban social media for all users under 16

Starmer’s “Australia-plus” plan to bar under-16s from TikTok, Snapchat, and Instagram — backed by 90 percent of parents in a consultation with 116,000-plus responses — raises sharp questions about state censorship, the capitalist roots of “online harms,” and the use of child-protection rhetoric to expand surveillance and content control. The class and democratic-rights dimensions deserve a Marxist treatment.

Recommended reading BBC / NBC News UK to ban social media for under-16s

5. Sudan: the world’s third-deadliest war that the West ignores

With 12,200 conflict deaths in 2025 and millions displaced, Sudan’s catastrophe receives a fraction of the coverage given Ukraine or Iran — precisely because no major power has a public-relations stake in it. A piece exposing the imperialist interests and selective attention behind this silence would fill a glaring gap.

Recommended reading UN News World news in brief: Sudan conflict deaths

6. Philippines earthquake exposes infrastructure precarity in a developing nation

Beyond 61-plus deaths, the Mindanao quake permanently reshaped the coastline — raising the seabed two meters and killing coral ecosystems that fishing communities depend on — while collapsed infrastructure crippled the response, all on the first day of the school year. This is a story about colonial underdevelopment and environmental precarity, not merely a natural disaster.
