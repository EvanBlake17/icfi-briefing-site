1. Trump demands war compensation from Iran as Hormuz talks stall

The US-Iran war entered its sixth month with negotiations over reopening the Strait of Hormuz visibly deteriorating. After Tehran demanded that Washington pay for damage inflicted during the five-month bombardment as a condition for reopening the waterway, Trump on Monday reversed the demand, saying the United States would require compensation from Iran for Americans killed and wounded — and, according to Israeli press accounts of his remarks, for damage in Lebanon and Gaza as well. Trump said Washington was “low-keying” its approach and only “semi-negotiating,” and on Tuesday claimed the strait was already open, a claim contradicted by shipping and oil markets.

Iran’s published conditions are an end to the US naval blockade of Iranian ports, the withdrawal of US forces from Iran’s vicinity, and a permanent end to the war. Tehran said a parallel arrangement with Oman over control of the strait was near completion but insufficient on its own to reopen the chokepoint through which roughly one-fifth of global crude normally passes.

Key facts: - Brent October futures were quoted at $84.11 a barrel, about 16 percent above pre-war levels, with traders attributing the persistent risk premium to the absence of any concrete agreement (Al Jazeera) - The war began February 28, 2026 with US and Israeli strikes that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei; an April 8–June 19 ceasefire and a June 17 memorandum collapsed, with fighting resuming July 8 after Iranian fire on three commercial vessels - Cumulative tolls compiled to date: 3,528 Iranians killed and 27,170 injured; 19 US soldiers and one contractor killed with 624 wounded; 40 Israeli soldiers, one contractor and 31 civilians killed; more than 1,000 Hezbollah fighters killed - US war costs were estimated at $113.3 billion as of June, alongside the administration’s $1.5 trillion Pentagon budget request - Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has issued decrees appointing six senior commanders across the General Staff, IRGC and Basij, framed as building “maximum deterrence” and intelligence “readiness” for offensive operations; President Pezeshkian said Khamenei is in good health after a seven-hour meeting, addressing his absence from public view

2. Netanyahu rejects Trump’s 15-point Gaza plan

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu formally rejected the US-backed 15-point framework produced by Trump’s “Board of Peace,” telling his cabinet that “Israel rejects the 15-point document” and that “the IDF will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is disarmed.” The plan had envisaged phased Israeli withdrawal matched against phased Hamas disarmament and a gradual transfer of administration to Palestinians. Netanyahu’s rejection followed more than a week of escalating attacks on the plan from his far-right coalition partners, with Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich insisting the document was “completely different” from what the Israeli cabinet had approved and demanding an immediate new vote.

The Board of Peace had already conceded the substance of the Israeli position before Netanyahu spoke, stating that no Israeli withdrawal would occur until Hamas disarmament was complete — inverting the reciprocity that was the plan’s ostensible basis.

Key facts: - Israeli strikes killed at least 19 Palestinians in a single day in early August, bringing that weekend’s toll to 26 - Gaza’s Health Ministry recorded 152 killed in July, the deadliest month of the year to date - Cumulative toll since the October 2025 “ceasefire” reached 1,250 killed and more than 4,100 wounded as of August 3 - The bombardment has continued uninterrupted through the disarmament negotiations, including strikes launched the day after Hamas publicly agreed to hand over weapons

3. Trump signs executive order gutting the childhood vaccine schedule

Trump signed an executive order on Monday, flanked by Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., directing federal health agencies to cut the number of diseases against which all children are universally recommended to be vaccinated from 18 to 11. The remaining seven are to be shifted into a “shared clinical decision-making” category left to individual parents and physicians — the same designation that in practice collapses insurance coverage and school-entry requirements.

The order also directs that immunizations be spread across separate medical visits rather than administered in combination, singling out the measles-mumps-rubella vaccine to be broken into three separate shots at three separate visits. Standalone measles, mumps and rubella vaccines are not manufactured for the US market. Trump repeated the discredited claim linking vaccination to autism at the signing.

Key facts: - The order tasks Kennedy, through HHS’s Task Force on Safer Childhood Vaccines, with delivering plans on vaccine research and “options for parents,” including continuing re-evaluation of risks and benefits - Pediatricians and public health bodies pushed back immediately, restating decades of safety and efficacy data; practical effects are expected to hit the fall immunization season - The order lands as US measles cases reach a 35-year high (see Science section) and while the country’s measles elimination status is under formal review

4. Russian strikes cut power to 300,000 families in Odesa

A Russian missile attack on Odesa damaged residential buildings and injured at least 14 people, with President Zelensky reporting that 300,000 families in the city lost electricity. Ukraine’s General Staff logged 170 combat engagements over the course of Monday, with Russian forces conducting 59 airstrikes, dropping 180 guided aerial bombs and launching drone attacks, of which Ukraine claims 335 were destroyed or suppressed. Ukrainian forces struck an oil refinery in Tatarstan; a blackout was reported in occupied Simferopol.

Fighting was concentrated in the Oleksandrivka direction (Kalynivka, Danylivka, Yehorivka, Rybne) and the Huliaipole direction (Vozdvizhivka, Rivne, Hirke, Charyvne). Kyiv’s cumulative claim of Russian personnel losses since February 2022 now stands at approximately 1,459,210, including 1,470 in the past 24 hours — figures that are unverified and serve a propaganda function, but which indicate the scale of attrition both sides have absorbed.

The domestic face of the war was underscored on the same day by a Nikolaev court’s 15-year sentence against socialist journalist Bogdan Syrotiuk, detailed in the WSWS coverage section below.

5. British Columbia wildfire emergency doubles to 35,000 evacuated

The number of people displaced by wildfires in British Columbia has more than doubled to over 35,000 as the province operates under the state of emergency declared Saturday. The Bald Range fire, first reported Friday evening, expanded to roughly 50 square kilometres within hours and travelled the 15 kilometres to Summerland at extraordinary speed, overwhelming the small community of Faulder en route.

Summerland (population about 12,000) was ordered evacuated overnight along with roughly 8,000 people in and around Peachland, on the west side of Okanagan Lake. The province has issued 40 evacuation orders and 49 alerts, and the emergency declaration grants powers to restrict travel and requisition accommodation.

Key facts: - The Bald Range fire had burned about 9,500 hectares (23,500 acres) and remained out of control - More than 100 wildfires were burning across BC, nearly half of them out of control, in hot, windy conditions following prolonged drought - Homes have been destroyed in the Okanagan communities; the fire front reached populated areas faster than evacuation systems could function - As the WSWS noted, Ottawa allocated roughly $800 million to wildfire response across 2019–2024, against $650 billion in corporate COVID relief and $63 billion in military spending

6. Record heat and fire across Europe and East Asia

Southern Europe is enduring a second consecutive summer of extreme heat and mass displacement. By August 4, fires this season had killed at least 17 civilians, six firefighters and two helicopter crew, and forced roughly 330,000 people from their homes in Spain and France alone. A wildfire near Athens has been contained after burning approximately 11,100 hectares. Spanish crews fought three large fires in the south on Monday, evacuating around 800 people amid shifting winds. France placed 42 departments at “high” fire risk on Tuesday, up from 17 the day before; Italy put 19 cities on red alert under a North African anticyclone.

In East Asia, the southeastern South Korean city of Yangsan recorded 42.5°C — the highest temperature in 122 years of national record-keeping, and the fifth consecutive day above 40°C in that city. President Lee Jae Myung called the heat a national disaster. Japan issued heatstroke alerts for 30 of its 47 prefectures.

Key facts: - Al Jazeera reports at least 19 heat-related deaths in South Korea since mid-May; the WSWS puts the toll at at least 30 since May, and notes Hong Kong recorded 36.9°C, its hottest day since records began in 1884, and Japan five straight days above 40°C in July - Asia is warming at roughly twice the global average rate - Record-low water levels on the Danube in northern Serbia have exposed sandbanks, emptied marinas and stranded cargo vessels

7. Pacific Islands Forum fails to condemn Chinese ICBM test

Pacific Islands Forum foreign ministers meeting in Suva failed to agree on any joint statement responding to China’s July 6 test of a nuclear-capable intercontinental ballistic missile, which was fired on short notice into Pacific waters near the Solomon Islands, Nauru and Tuvalu. New Zealand, Australia and Tuvalu pressed for condemnation; Kiribati, Nauru and Vanuatu blocked it.

New Zealand Foreign Minister Winston Peters, emerging from hours of talks, accused the dissenting states of appeasing “outside partners’ wishes, rather than those that belong to the Pacific Islands Forum” — a complaint about the misuse of consensus that, as the WSWS observed, amounts to objecting when small states use the same veto power Canberra and Wellington have long relied on. The meeting was intended to set the agenda for the leaders’ summit in Palau later this month.

Washington presses Peru’s Fujimori to break with China

Less than two weeks into Keiko Fujimori’s presidency, the Trump administration is pressing Lima to align with Washington’s campaign against Chinese influence in the hemisphere. Fujimori has publicly rejected “automatic alignment with any power” and called for a “strategically balanced” position — but her appointment of a longtime US Embassy official as foreign minister is, as Bloomberg put it, her clearest nod to Washington yet.

  • China is Peru’s largest trading partner at roughly 36 percent of trade, against the United States’ 14 percent, with some $30 billion in Chinese investment
  • The Chancay megaport, built by Chinese state company COSCO and inaugurated in November 2025, is projected to absorb $3.5 billion in investment and is the central object of US strategic concern
  • Fujimori is Peru’s ninth president in a decade, taking office amid what analysts describe as entrenched institutional collapse
  • The WSWS characterises the accompanying US “Shield of the Americas” policy as “Operation Condor 2.0,” directed not only against Chinese commercial position but against working-class movements across the region

France’s under-15 social media ban clears parliament

France’s law barring under-15s from social media platforms has completed its passage, with the Senate approving it 243 votes to two and the National Assembly 279 to 81. The ban is scheduled to bite from September 1 for TikTok, Instagram and Facebook, with existing underage accounts to be suspended by January 2027.

In a significant detail, parliament stripped the statutory age-verification mechanism from the final text, leaving the EU Digital Services Act as the enforcement instrument and delegating the design of verification tools to the platforms themselves, subject to approval by the privacy regulator CNIL. The senators’ preferred two-tier system — distinguishing “blacklisted” platforms deemed harmful — lost out to the lower house’s blanket ban.

The WSWS argues that whatever the verification mechanism finally adopted, its practical effect is the imposition of identity-document or biometric checks on the entire adult population, eliminating online anonymity and constructing surveillance infrastructure that will be turned against political dissent and antiwar organising.

Australian government prepares immigration crackdown

The Albanese Labor government is moving to drive net overseas migration down from 301,000 to 225,000 by 2027–28, a target officials describe as the “guiding star” of migration policy. Two ministerial directives now prioritise onshore over offshore applicants for skilled and family visas, slowing offshore arrivals without formal legislative change.

  • Visa application charges across the main skilled pathways rose roughly 25 percent from July 1; the 485 Temporary Graduate visa base charge is now A$5,750
  • Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke’s 24 percent fee increase is generating an additional A$1.2 billion
  • The WSWS reports that roughly 432,000 bridging-visa holders are affected and that parent-visa waiting times are being pushed toward 33 years

The policy is being pursued by a Labor government under conditions where the entire official political spectrum has adopted immigration restriction as a diversion from the housing and cost-of-living crisis.

Famine confirmed in Sudan’s El Fasher and Kadugli

The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification has confirmed famine (IPC Phase 5) conditions in El Fasher in North Darfur and the besieged town of Kadugli in South Kordofan, with 20 further areas at risk. Prolonged sieges have cut off food, fuel and medicine entirely, leaving trapped populations to survive on whatever remains inside the perimeter.

  • An estimated 21.2 million people — 45 percent of Sudan’s population — face acute food insecurity at IPC Phase 3 or above, including 375,000 in Phase 5 (Catastrophe) and 6.3 million in Phase 4 (Emergency)
  • Amnesty International’s recent report describes RSF atrocities in El Fasher as “a stain on the conscience of humanity”
  • Hunger and malnutrition have eased in areas where fighting has subsided, underscoring that the famine is a product of the war and blockade rather than absolute scarcity

China’s export surge is a price illusion

China’s July exports rose 23.9 percent year-on-year in dollar terms, beating forecasts of 22.2 percent but slowing from June’s 27 percent. Semiconductor exports jumped 117 percent and ship exports nearly doubled. In volume terms, however, exports barely moved — the headline is being flattered by the price inflation running through AI-related hardware, particularly memory. Imports rose 27.5 percent. Consumer inflation slowed to 0.5 percent, the weakest since January, while producer prices rose 3.5 percent, down from 4.1 percent.

Trump’s 50 percent Canada tariffs take effect August 19

Three proclamations signed under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — an obscure authority permitting duties up to 50 percent against countries deemed to discriminate against US commerce — impose an additional 50 percent tariff on Canadian dairy, alcoholic beverages and motor vehicles from August 19. Coverage extends to wine, hockey sticks and cement, and applies regardless of USMCA qualification. Roughly $20 billion in goods are affected, about 5 percent of the $382 billion imported from Canada in 2025.

Federal courts have ruled against ICE detention practices some 16,000 times

Federal judges have issued roughly 16,000 rulings against ICE detention practices over the past year, about two-thirds of them concerning the agency’s blanket denial of bond hearings to immigrants arrested inside the United States. According to the WSWS’s tally, 469 judges have found the policy illegal, against approximately 50 — overwhelmingly Trump appointees — who upheld it. ICE has repeatedly delayed or simply defied release orders.

At the appellate level the policy has produced a deep circuit split. The Second, Sixth, Seventh, Ninth, Tenth and Eleventh Circuits have ruled against mandatory detention without bond hearings; the Fifth and Eighth have sided with the administration. The Ninth Circuit’s 2-1 ruling included a Trump appointee in the majority. A Fifth Circuit panel separately held that ICE cannot detain beyond 90 days without an individualised custody hearing at which the government bears the burden. The administration petitioned the Supreme Court on June 26 to uphold the policy nationwide.

The detention contractors are the material beneficiaries: GEO Group and CoreCivic reported $1.4 billion in combined quarterly revenue. As Patrick Martin writes, the apparatus operates on the assumption that judicial orders are advisory.

US measles cases hit a 35-year high

The CDC has confirmed 2,465 measles cases in 2026 as of August 6, across 47 jurisdictions — already above the 2,289 recorded for all of 2025 and the highest total in 35 years. Some 94 percent (2,309 cases) are outbreak-associated, and 93 percent of patients were unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status. Utah’s ongoing outbreak leads with 516 cases.

The US National Verification Committee meets in mid-August to assess whether continuous transmission chains have now persisted beyond 12 months. Its findings go to PAHO, which will announce in November whether the United States has lost the measles elimination status it has held since 2000. This is the epidemiological context in which Monday’s executive order directs the splitting of MMR into three separate visits.

Nature study links COVID-19 to reactivation of dormant viruses

A study published in Nature on August 5 found that SARS-CoV-2 infection can reactivate latent viruses including Epstein-Barr virus and cytomegalovirus, with the degree of reactivation correlating with the severity of the acute COVID-19 episode. The finding supplies a plausible biological mechanism for a subset of long COVID presentations and reinforces the case — systematically suppressed by governments pursuing “forever COVID” policies — that infection carries durable systemic consequences.

  • Astronomy: A total solar eclipse crosses Iceland and northern Spain on August 12 — the first totality visible in mainland Europe since 1999.

Arts and culture

Venice opens ticket sales for a festival tilted away from Hollywood. The 83rd Venice International Film Festival (September 2–12) put its public screening schedule online on Friday and opened online ticket sales this afternoon. The programme is notable for the near-absence of major studio product and a corresponding weight of auteur and independent work: new films from Martin McDonagh, Florian Zeller, Werner Herzog and Danny Boyle, whose Ink opens the festival on September 2. The documentary strand includes Alex Gibney’s film on Elon Musk and a chronicle of the Oasis reunion tour.

The retreat of the studios from Venice is itself a social fact. As the streaming conglomerates consolidate and strip out mid-budget filmmaking, the festival circuit becomes the residual venue in which serious dramatic work can still find an audience — while the same films struggle to reach beyond a metropolitan art-house public. Whether this year’s competition produces anything that seriously confronts a world in its sixth month of a US-Iran war remains to be seen; recent editions have tended toward interiority and historical costume.

Palestinian culture at the Edinburgh Fringe. The 2026 Fringe (August 7–31) hosts “Welcome to the Fringe, Palestine,” organised by an independent group of Scotland-based writers, dancers, theatre-makers and publishers. A play will be streamed live by satellite from Gaza to Edinburgh audiences on August 17, alongside a full day of storytelling, poetry, stand-up, dance and theatre — including Al-Harah Theatre’s Shakespeare’s Sisters — plus Palestinian short films, discussions and a photography exhibition. The organisers state explicitly that the initiative exists to circumvent the censorship Palestinian artists face in established British cultural institutions. On August 2, pro-Palestinian protesters interrupted an event with Scottish First Minister John Swinney at the Stand Comedy Club, demanding he use the word “genocide.”

The Booker longlist and the state of the novel. The 2026 Booker Prize longlist, announced late last month, runs to 13 titles judged by a panel chaired by Mary Beard and including Raymond Antrobus, Jarvis Cocker, Rebecca Liu and Patricia Lockwood. It mixes previous laureates — Douglas Stuart (John of John), Marlon James (The Disappearers) — with Elizabeth Strout, Gwendoline Riley, Emma Cline, M. John Harrison and several debuts. The shortlist arrives September 22, the winner November 9. The selection’s range across thriller, dystopia and domestic realism reflects a literary culture in which the dystopian mode has become the default form for registering social crisis, generally at the cost of any concrete social analysis.

Attacks on cultural institutions. The White House review of the Smithsonian, launched under an executive order titled “Restore Truth and Sanity to American History,” continues to press the museums to purge “divisive” or “ideologically driven language” — that is, any critical treatment of slavery, racism or class. The administration’s budget excluded funding for the National Museum of the American Latino and the Anacostia Community Museum, and the House Oversight Committee summoned Smithsonian executive director Anthea M. Hartig on July 21 to a hearing titled “Rewriting American History.” NEA grants have been cancelled en masse and the agency’s priorities redirected toward “skilled trade jobs,” “AI competency” and support for “the military and veterans” — the open subordination of federal arts funding to militarism and workforce discipline.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: Fascist Ukrainian court sentences Bogdan Syrotiuk, socialist opponent of war, to 15 years in prison — WSWS Editorial Board

A district court in Nikolaev has sentenced Bogdan Syrotiuk, the 27-year-old socialist and member of the Young Guard of Bolshevik-Leninists, to 15 years in prison for “high treason under martial law.” The Editorial Board states flatly that “the record of the case contains no act of treason—no espionage, no sabotage, no collaboration.” The prosecution rested entirely on his writing: state experts examined 14 texts, seven were indicted, one charge was dropped after the court’s own forensic commission found nothing criminal in it, and he was convicted on the basis of six articles published on the World Socialist Web Site.

The court’s stated objections were explicitly political: Syrotiuk’s “non-recognition of Ukraine’s legitimately elected political leadership,” his “comparisons with fascists and Nazis,” his critical assessments of the Armed Forces’ performance, and his “reinterpretation of national heroes.” On the first, the Board notes that Zelensky’s term expired in May 2024 and he “remains in power more than two years past his legal mandate, ruling by decree under a state of permanent martial law” — Syrotiuk is being imprisoned for stating a fact. The “national heroes” in question are Stepan Bandera and other OUN Nazi collaborators rehabilitated by the Ukrainian state. The court disregarded a 65-page defence criminology report finding no material amounting to propaganda for Russian aggression.

The Board situates the case in a systematic campaign: communist parties and symbols banned under the 2015 “decommunization” laws; eleven opposition parties suspended in 2022 and the largest banned outright; roughly 20,000 criminal cases opened under treason and collaboration statutes; the WSWS itself banned in Ukraine since June 6, 2024. It cites the UN Human Rights Office’s documentation of “torture and ill-treatment” of detainees and Human Rights Watch’s finding that the collaboration laws are “overly broad and vague,” with prosecutions of people who performed “emergency services, construction, humanitarian relief and garbage removal” in occupied areas.

Two data points anchor the political argument. A 2025 Gallup survey found 69 percent of Ukrainians now support a negotiated end to the war, “a complete reversal of opinion since 2022”; and more than 300,000 criminal cases have been opened against Ukrainian soldiers for desertion or absence without leave since 2022, the majority within the past year. The escalating repression is a response to the collapse of popular support for the war, not evidence of the regime’s strength.

The Board treats the sentence as effectively a death sentence, citing an April 2025 UN Committee Against Torture report on overcrowding, inadequate food and water, absent independent medical examination and rising custodial deaths, and a Council of Europe anti-torture report from October 2023 describing remand prisoners “confined to their cells for up to 23 hours a day” in conditions potentially “inhuman and degrading.”

Politically decisive is the prosecution’s allegation that Syrotiuk acted “on instructions from a representative of the World Socialist Web Site, under the pseudonym ‘David’” — a reference to David North, chairman of the ICFI. The Ukrainian state is thereby asserting “the power to declare international socialist and antiwar publications criminal enterprises,” an attack extending well beyond Ukraine’s borders. The Board closes with a call for the mobilisation of the international working class, youth and progressive organisations — resolutions, public statements and signatures on the petition demanding his release — as part of “a fight against imperialist war, dictatorship and fascism.”

Trump’s lawless deportation machine: ICE actions ruled illegal 16,000 times — Patrick Martin

Beyond the litigation record summarised above, Martin’s central argument is that the sheer volume of adverse rulings, combined with ICE’s routine defiance of them, demonstrates that the immigration apparatus no longer operates within a legal framework at all. The near-unanimity of the federal bench — including Republican appointees — against the bond-hearing policy is significant precisely because it establishes that this is not a contested legal question but an executive determination to ignore the courts. Martin ties the persistence of the policy to its profitability, and situates the detention system as the training ground for methods that will be turned against the working class more broadly. The article connects to the WSWS’s ongoing campaign against the militarisation of American cities and its insistence that the defence of immigrants cannot be delegated to the Democratic Party or the courts.

US economy sheds 23,000 jobs in July — Andy Thompson

Thompson’s analysis emphasises what the headline numbers conceal. The fall in the unemployment rate is a statistical artifact of more than 250,000 people leaving the labour force — workers counted out of existence rather than into employment. He links the destruction of local government education jobs to the austerity programme being imposed on public education at every level of government, and the broader stagnation in hiring to the accelerating deployment of automation and AI in white-collar and logistics work. The article stresses that this labour market deterioration is occurring simultaneously with a record concentration of wealth in the top 1 percent and equity indices at or near all-time highs — the clearest available demonstration that share prices now move inversely to the conditions of the working class.

Australian Education Union pushing new sellout of Victorian teachers after anti-democratic conference — Committee for Public Education

The AEU is advancing a third enterprise agreement offering 6.7–7 percent annual increases — a real-terms cut against Victorian cost-of-living increases — after teachers rejected two previous offers. The union blocked any debate on the dispute at its August 1 state conference, a procedural manoeuvre the CFPE identifies as the characteristic method of a bureaucracy that fears its own membership. The article’s programmatic conclusion is the formation of rank-and-file committees independent of the AEU apparatus, linked to educators internationally through the International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees. It situates the Victorian dispute alongside the wave of contract rejections in the United States as evidence of a generalised revolt against union-negotiated concessions.

Washington pressures Fujimori to align Peru with US war drive against China — César Uco

Uco’s specific contribution beyond the trade and diplomatic facts is the characterisation of the “Shield of the Americas” framework as “Operation Condor 2.0.” The article argues that the US campaign against Chinese influence in Latin America is inseparable from the suppression of working-class and peasant movements — that the security architecture being assembled to counter Beijing is designed in the first instance for internal use against social opposition, as its predecessor was. Uco reviews the Fujimori dynasty’s own record, from Alberto Fujimori’s 1992 self-coup and the mass killings for which he was convicted, as the foundation on which Washington is now building. The piece connects to the WSWS’s coverage of the collapse of bourgeois rule in Peru — nine presidents in a decade — and argues that the working class can advance no democratic demand outside a struggle for power.

Australian government prepares vicious immigration crackdown — Mike Head

Head’s distinctive argument is that the Labor government’s migration cuts are being carried out with the active or tacit support of the entire parliamentary establishment and the union bureaucracy, and that the nationalist logic — blaming migrants for housing shortages and stagnant wages — is being cultivated precisely because it deflects from the class questions raised by the same government’s military expenditure and corporate subsidies. He notes that Labor has historically been the party of the White Australia policy and of mandatory detention, and that its current measures are being enacted without meaningful opposition from the Greens. The programmatic conclusion is the defence of the right of all workers to live and work wherever they choose, and the rejection of any national-based defence of jobs and conditions.

East Asia hit by heatwaves as climate change pushes temperatures to record highs — Ben McGrath

McGrath’s political conclusion, beyond the temperature records, concerns the response of the South Korean and Japanese trade union federations, which have confined themselves to appeals for government-mandated rest breaks and heat guidelines. He argues these appeals are directed to the same governments that are subordinating every social question to military rearmament against China, and that outdoor and construction workers — who make up the overwhelming majority of heat deaths — will obtain protection only through independent organisation and the international coordination of workers’ struggles across the region.

Pacific foreign ministers fail to agree on statement condemning Chinese missile test — John Braddock

Braddock’s contribution is the framing of the Forum rift as a manifestation of Australian and New Zealand neo-colonial pressure meeting resistance from states that have concluded they have more to gain from Beijing than from Canberra. He documents the record of Australian and New Zealand policing deployments, budget-support conditionality and interference in Pacific elections that produced this scepticism, and situates the missile test itself within the escalating US-led military encirclement of China, in which Australia and New Zealand are frontline participants through AUKUS and related basing arrangements.

British Columbia declares state of emergency as wildfires force 22,000 from their homes — Pierre Lajeunesse

Lajeunesse documents the systematic degradation of firefighting capacity in Canada: seasonal, low-paid crews; the elimination of permanent staff; the absence of any national aerial firefighting fleet; and the reliance on evacuation rather than prevention. He notes the political silence of the federal NDP and the union leaderships on the funding disparity between disaster response and military expenditure, and argues that the annual repetition of these emergencies — each described as unprecedented — reflects a mode of production incapable of planning beyond the next quarterly return.

France’s social media ban imposes universal identity checks on the entire population — Stephen Parker

Parker’s argument beyond the legislative facts is that the “child protection” framing is a familiar mechanism for the introduction of surveillance powers that are subsequently generalised. He places the law alongside France’s expanding anti-terror legislation, the state of emergency provisions normalised since 2015, and the criminalisation of pro-Palestinian and antiwar demonstrations under Macron. The near-unanimity of the parliamentary vote — including the votes of the “left” formations of the NUPES tradition — is presented as evidence that no section of the French political establishment defends democratic rights.

Jacobin advises union bureaucracy on how to respond to wave of contract rejection — Tom Hall

Hall’s polemic centres on Chris Brooks, the former UAW chief strategist paid $211,968 by the union in 2025, who attended a 2022 MIT meeting on managing workers’ “unreasonable expectations” and now writes for Jacobin on how the bureaucracy should handle the wave of contract rejections. Hall counts at least 16 rejections in 2026, including a 98.3 percent “no” vote at BP Whiting. The article’s political point is that Jacobin’s function is not commentary but consultancy: it supplies the apparatus with the language and tactics required to defeat a rank-and-file revolt it cannot suppress by open means. Hall counterposes Will Lehman’s UAW presidential campaign and its demand to abolish the union bureaucracy and transfer power to rank-and-file committees.

Jacobin / DSA

“If AOC Doesn’t Run for President, Run a Union Worker” argues that the left must contest the 2028 Democratic Party primary, describing abstention in 2024 as a “disaster.” This is the explicit subordination of working-class politics to the electoral machinery of a party of imperialist war, and it arrives while that party’s congressional delegation has voted for every appropriation of the Iran war.

“Socialists Need to Develop a Constitutional Politics” proposes pursuing “legislative primacy” through court-packing and jurisdiction-stripping, treating socialists as a pressure bloc within the House Democratic Caucus. The premise is that the constitutional order of American capitalism can be made to deliver socialist policy given sufficiently clever procedural manoeuvre — a conception directly contradicted by the executive’s open defiance of thousands of court rulings on immigration detention.

“How Capitalism Undermines Democracy” offers a structural account of how concentrated wealth erodes democratic forms, then concludes by proposing to strengthen democracy within capitalism rather than overthrow it.

The day’s output confirms Jacobin’s function as a policy shop for the Democratic Party’s left flank and, in Tom Hall’s account today, for the union apparatus itself.

Left Voice

“AOC’s ABC Interview and the Fight Over What Socialism Will Mean” criticises AOC and the DSA for treating social movements as raw material for “renewing” the Democratic Party, and calls for independent working-class politics. The critique is correct as far as it goes; what is absent is any accounting of Left Voice’s own record of orienting to the same milieu, or any concrete perspective for building a revolutionary party, which is deferred indefinitely to “the movement.”

“SOUTHCOM Announces New Military Campaign, Expanding Land Operations in Latin America” frames the expansion as a renewed Monroe Doctrine and Operation Condor, and unambiguously opposes US intervention — the sharpest anti-imperialist material from any US pseudo-left outlet today.

“The Bar Exam Is a Class Filter, Not a Test of Competence” is a critique of professional licensing with no party content, characteristic of Left Voice’s steady drift toward a middle-class professional readership.

Liberation News / PSL

“Michigan primary: A huge defeat for AIPAC and the Israel Lobby” declines to endorse the Democratic winner and describes the Democratic establishment as a servant of “billionaires, corporations, and the Israel lobby,” calling for an independent revolutionary party. The framing nonetheless treats a Democratic primary outcome as a working-class victory, which concedes the political terrain the article formally rejects, and the “Israel lobby” analysis substitutes a conspiratorial explanation for the material interests of American imperialism in the Middle East.

“One month after Lorenzo’s murder, the fight for justice continues” states plainly that the Democrats “are unwilling to fight” ICE and calls for mass action over electoral and legal channels. The abolitionist framework, however, addresses the state as a set of institutions to be dismantled rather than a class instrument.

“They can destroy a memorial, but they can’t destroy the movement!” names a Democratic mayor as complicit in police repression rather than a lesser evil.

Socialist Alternative

“Support Left Independent Candidates—Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties” calls the Democrats “corporate,” endorses independent and PSL candidates — and then extends conditional support to Sanders and Mamdani as “stepping stones.” This is lesser-evilism reintroduced through the back door of a formally correct call for a new workers’ party.

“What’s Really Behind The Crisis In Ceuta?” attributes the migrant crisis to imperialism and colonial legacy and criticises Sánchez for legitimising far-right rhetoric, without drawing conclusions about the PSOE government as such.

The organisation posted nothing between August 6 and 11.

Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

“Palestinians say ‘Gaza is a prison’ as Israel continues bombings” calls to “stop arming Israel,” implicates British complicity and describes Trump’s “Board of Peace” as a colonial carve-up.

“Teachers and parents slam Labour plan for primaries to monitor children for ‘joblessness risk’” attacks the Starmer government’s surveillance of primary-age children, but its remedy is more funding and services — a reformist demand addressed to the government it denounces.

“Anti-racists take on Farage ahead of Clacton by-election” promotes united-front street mobilisation against Reform UK without electoral endorsement, but with no perspective beyond protest.

Communist.red / RCP (IMT)

“British imperialism: The emperor has no clothes” argues Britain is a declining, US-dependent power and rejects Labour and Andy Burnham as any alternative. “From ‘beer and sandwiches’ to ‘champagne and canapés’” criticises union leaders for fostering “illusions in Labour.” “From Kashmir, to India, to Britain… Workers and youth, unite!” draws internationalist connections across South Asian and British unrest. The line is verbally revolutionary and class-independent; what remains absent is any reckoning with the IMT’s own decades of entry work inside Labour, or with the Trotskyist movement’s actual history — the questions on which the ICFI’s differences with this tendency rest.

Assessment: No tendency reviewed today endorsed an imperialist war, and several published sharp anti-imperialist material. The dividing line remains the Democratic Party and Labour: Jacobin openly, and Socialist Alternative conditionally, subordinate the working class to bourgeois electoral vehicles, while the others reject them rhetorically without advancing any programme for building a revolutionary party. None referenced the ICFI, the WSWS, or the Syrotiuk case — a silence on the imprisonment of a socialist journalist for antiwar writing that is itself politically significant.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Trump’s vaccine order and the dismantling of public health

The executive order cutting universally recommended childhood vaccines from 18 diseases to 11 and splitting MMR into three visits is the most consequential attack on public health infrastructure since the abolition of COVID mitigation. Its practical mechanism — moving vaccines to “shared clinical decision-making” — will strip insurance coverage and school-entry requirements, disproportionately hitting working-class children, and it comes as US measles cases hit a 35-year high with elimination status under review.

Netanyahu’s flat repudiation of the 15-point plan, and the Board of Peace’s prior capitulation to the Israeli precondition, expose the “peace process” as cover for continued bombardment. A WSWS analysis is needed of what the collapse means for the Trump administration’s broader regional strategy in the sixth month of the war with Iran.

3. The National Guard occupation of Washington extended to 2029

An open-ended military deployment in the US capital, with troop numbers doubling and no measurable effect on crime, is the consolidation of a precedent for domestic military rule. The WSWS has covered earlier deployments but has not addressed the extension to January 2029 or the $1.7 billion cost.

Recommended reading Washington Post Washington grapples with a National Guard deployment extended until 2029

4. Todd Blanche and the fusion of the Justice Department with the presidency

The confirmation of Trump’s personal defence lawyer as attorney general on a 50-49 vote, after Pam Bondi’s ouster, completes the subordination of federal prosecutorial power to the executive’s personal interests — the institutional precondition for the ICE lawlessness the WSWS documented today.

Recommended reading Roll Call Todd Blanche confirmed by Senate as attorney general

5. Famine in Sudan as 21 million face acute food insecurity

The IPC’s confirmation of famine in El Fasher and Kadugli, with 20 further areas at risk, has produced almost no sustained coverage in the imperialist press. The starvation of a besieged population in a war fuelled by rival regional powers and Western-aligned states deserves treatment alongside the WSWS’s Gaza coverage.

Recommended reading World Food Programme Famine conditions confirmed in Sudan’s El Fasher and Kadugli

6. Southern Europe’s fire season and the displacement of 330,000

Spain and France alone have displaced 330,000 people this summer, with at least 25 dead including firefighters and helicopter crew. The WSWS covered British Columbia and East Asia today; the European fire emergency, and the privatised and understaffed firefighting services behind the death toll, remain uncovered.

Recommended reading Euronews Europe battles the flames: wildfire contained near Athens and evacuations in Spain

7. The memory chip shortage and the inflation hidden in China’s trade data

Memory prices up five- to sevenfold since early 2025 are inflating China’s export figures — semiconductors up 117 percent in value with volumes flat — while raising the cost of every consumer electronic device. This is a concrete illustration of how the AI investment boom is transmitting price inflation into working-class household budgets worldwide.
