1. Trump calls off planned strike on Iran after “perimeters of a deal” reached

President Trump announced late Saturday that he had cancelled a planned wave of attacks on Iran, saying that Iran and “other Middle Eastern Countries” had asked Washington to hold off because “the perimeters of a deal had been agreed to.” In a social media post he wrote: “I have agreed, for the future benefit of the WORLD and, likewise, the survival of a successful and prosperous Iran, to cancel the attack, subject to being able to rapidly make a DEAL.”

The reversal followed a direct appeal from Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, who telephoned Trump on Saturday to express alarm at the strike plans. Qatar, the UAE, Turkey and Pakistan have all pressed both sides to de-escalate. Israeli Defense Minister statements earlier in the week had indicated Tel Aviv wanted the next wave aimed squarely at Iranian energy infrastructure — a deliberate escalation intended to break the Iranian economy. The announced framework contains two demands: the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and “an end to Iran’s nuclear threat.” No Iranian confirmation of the terms has been published.

Key facts: - Trump said Israel “joins him” in the pause, which is contingent on rapid conclusion of a deal - No US strikes inside Iran have been reported for several days following the “heavy wave of strikes” Trump ordered on Wednesday - US embassies in Amman, Jerusalem and Baghdad warned American citizens Saturday to prepare for “unforeseen escalation” and to be ready to depart - The war began with the February 28 assault in which US and Israeli forces launched nearly 900 strikes in 12 hours, killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei - The Islamabad Memorandum signed June 17 collapsed on July 8, when Trump declared the ceasefire “over”

2. LNG tanker struck in Strait of Hormuz as Kuwait intercepts Iranian drones

The Bermuda-flagged LNG carrier Gaslog Shanghai, carrying a Qatari cargo loaded around July 27, was struck by an unidentified projectile while transiting the Strait of Hormuz. The vessel suffered a full blackout and engine-room damage, leaving it unable to maneuver. A fire was extinguished and no crew were injured. It was one of two vessels hit inside Omani waters, 11 nautical miles northeast of Lima and 21 nautical miles northeast of Khasab. The ship had stopped transmitting its AIS signal on July 31 near the western entrance to the strait.

Kuwait’s defense ministry said its air defenses detected and intercepted Iranian drones entering national airspace, with several soldiers wounded by falling shrapnel from intercepted targets. Iranian media claimed strikes on the Al-Salem airbase, a major logistics hub for US forces, while Jordan reported intercepting ballistic missiles aimed at the Muwaffaq Salti Air Base. The Houthis denied reports they had begun imposing transit tolls on vessels through the Bab-el-Mandeb.

Key facts: - Roughly a fifth of global LNG flows and about 35% of seaborne crude trade transit Hormuz - A second Gaslog carrier, the Gaslog Salem, was hit by a drone on Wednesday at the Egyptian port of Damietta - Israel said it killed Hezbollah fighters in southern Lebanon despite the standing ceasefire framework - Netanyahu blocked a request to transfer Iron Dome systems to Ukraine

3. Russian ballistic missile barrage kills nine in Kyiv

Russia struck Kyiv overnight with 35 missiles and 185 attack drones, killing nine people and wounding 27, including four children, according to President Zelensky. Fires and destruction were recorded across five districts, with Darnytskyi worst hit — seven dead and 14 injured there. Ukrainian air defense shot down only one of 27 ballistic missiles fired, a collapse in interception rates that officials attribute directly to the exhaustion of Patriot interceptor stocks.

It was the second mass-casualty barrage in 48 hours, following a strike two days earlier that killed at least ten elsewhere in Ukraine. Ukrainian officials describe a deliberate Russian effort to exploit the interceptor shortage, which is itself a product of Washington’s redirection of air defense munitions to the Middle East theater. Ukrainian forces separately sank a Rosatom-owned container ship in the Black Sea; its 17-member crew was evacuated.

Key facts: - Only 1 of 27 ballistic missiles intercepted, against far higher rates earlier in the war - Ukraine unveiled a new domestically produced interceptor drone designed to destroy Shahed-type attack drones - Israel refused a request to transfer Iron Dome batteries to Ukraine

4. FBI investigates Iranian breach of US water systems in seven states

Michigan announced Saturday that nine of its water systems had been targeted in cyberattacks, joining Minnesota in disclosing intrusions that US intelligence officials attribute to Iran. Federal agencies say at least seven states’ water systems have been compromised but have declined to name them. The attackers reached internet-connected control systems used to manage water treatment, pressure and related operations.

State officials said all systems continued to operate safely, local operators addressed the issues, and there were no known public health impacts. The FBI said it and “interagency partners are fully engaged to protect critical infrastructure.” Officials stress the attribution remains preliminary, with no definitive forensic proof. Iranian interest in US water infrastructure is long-standing: in 2016 the Justice Department charged Iranian hackers over an intrusion at a small dam near New York City.

Key facts: - Nine Michigan systems targeted; seven states affected nationally per federal agencies - Intrusions came after an FBI warning to utilities about Iranian-linked activity - The disclosures land as Washington openly debates strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure — the reciprocal targeting of civilian infrastructure is now a structural feature of the conflict

5. Board of Peace announces Hamas disarmament roadmap for Gaza

The US-led “Board of Peace” and the International Stabilization Force announced that mediators from Egypt, Qatar, Turkey and the United States had finalized a roadmap for the complete disarmament of Hamas and all other armed groups in Gaza. Trump announced the agreement personally. Under the framework, weapons are to be handed to a National Committee for the Administration of Gaza; storage facilities will be controlled by Palestinian police officers, each individually security-vetted by Israel’s Shin Bet, with the multinational force providing oversight.

Israeli withdrawals are to be staged against verified progress on disarmament. The precise sequencing must be prepared within 14 days, subject to extension by an international verification body and the agreement of all parties. Reports indicate Iran worked behind the scenes to persuade Hamas to reject the disarmament terms. Three Palestinians were killed in Gaza City in the past 24 hours, bringing the day’s toll to seven. An Israeli airstrike on Saturday hit a medical equipment warehouse belonging to al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital in Deir al-Balah.

6. Death toll in Ceuta border crisis reaches 67 as Spain installs sea barrier

The Spanish government said Saturday that at least 67 people died in the mass crossing into the North African enclave of Ceuta — some drowned swimming around the Tarajal and Benzú breakwaters, others crushed in a stampede on the breakwater barrier. Between 50,000 and 60,000 people crossed from Morocco into the territory between Thursday and Friday, overwhelming a city of some 84,000 residents.

Madrid’s response was to install a 500-meter maritime barrier along the sea border on Saturday. Spanish officials declared the situation “almost entirely reversed,” with tens of thousands having returned to Morocco. Separately, Moroccan security forces clashed with migrants attempting to enter the Spanish enclave of Melilla, with more than 50 detained. The crisis follows a sustained rise in irregular entries during the second half of July, with roughly 1,500 arrivals between July 20 and 29.

7. Washington declares wildfire emergency as fire crosses into Spokane

Governor Bob Ferguson declared a statewide wildfire emergency Saturday as fires burned more than 312 square miles across Washington. The Old Trails Fire, which ignited around noon August 1 in the Airway Heights area, was driven by gusts of 35 to 45 mph into Riverside State Park and then across the Spokane River into the city itself. It has burned roughly 3,000 acres with about 4,000 structures threatened.

Spokane County raised evacuation status for communities northwest of the city to Level 3 — the most urgent designation, meaning immediate departure. Thousands were evacuated from north Spokane overnight. The Washington National Guard was activated for fire response. The fires are burning under a heat dome that pushed Phoenix to 116°F, conditions consistent with the intensifying fire regime documented across the western United States.

Burnham government signals defence spending increase

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who succeeded Keir Starmer on June 22, announced £1.1 billion in new defence spending at the Farnborough International Airshow, including £800 million for a new surveillance package. Burnham framed the expenditure in the language of industrial policy, pledging to “reindustrialise the nation” alongside defence businesses and declaring: “We are going to honour our commitments on defence and use that investment to transform people’s lives.”

Burnham has declined to commit to reaching 3% of GDP on defence by 2030, a retreat from Starmer’s stated ambition, while retaining the 3.5%-by-2035 pledge. The formulation — rearmament sold as regional regeneration — is the standard mechanism by which social spending is converted into military spending under a Labour government.

Pakistan-administered Kashmir under sustained state crackdown

Authorities in Azad Jammu and Kashmir have escalated repression against the mass protest movement since early June. The Jammu Kashmir Joint Awami Action Committee — which organized rallies, road blockades, sit-ins and shutter-down strikes in Muzaffarabad, Rawalakot, Mirpur and Kotli against the twelve legislative seats reserved for refugees resident in Pakistan — has been banned under anti-terrorism legislation and several of its leaders arrested.

Dozens have been killed since June, mostly protesters, ahead of the July 27 Legislative Assembly vote; the UN called on July 17 for impartial investigations. At least 38 Kashmiris remain missing, alongside internet shutdowns and mass arbitrary arrests. The movement is driven by electricity and flour price demands and the denial of political representation — a class movement being criminalized as terrorism.

Bombing at Moscow restaurant kills three

An improvised explosive device concealed in a gift box detonated on the veranda of the Balzi Rossi restaurant on Kudrinskaya Square in Moscow, killing three and injuring 21. The device, packed with metal ball bearings and with the force of approximately one kilogram of TNT, was carried by an unidentified woman attempting to enter a private banquet held on the summer terrace under strict guard.

A security guard grew suspicious and moved to search her; the bomb detonated at that moment, killing the woman, the guard and a nearby customer instantly. Russian reports indicate the device may have been detonated remotely by a third party and that the woman may not have known she was carrying an explosive — a pattern seen in earlier assassinations attributed to Ukrainian intelligence.

Famine confirmed in El Fasher and Kadugli as Sudan war grinds on

Famine conditions have been formally confirmed in El Fasher in western Darfur and in Kadugli, with 21.2 million people facing high levels of acute food insecurity after 30 months of fighting and 375,000 people in catastrophe conditions. Amnesty International’s report on the RSF siege of El Fasher — maintained from May 2024 to October 2025 with near-daily shelling and systematic blocking of food and humanitarian supplies — documents crimes against humanity and ethnic cleansing, and describes residents reduced to eating ambaz, the peanut-oil byproduct normally fed to animals.

In parallel, Sudan and Egypt agreed a 16-month plan to rehabilitate the war-damaged Jebel Aulia Dam. Protesters in Suakin and Jubayt blockaded highways over power cuts during a severe heat wave.

Truck bomb at Colombian police station in Cúcuta

A truck packed with explosives detonated before dawn Saturday outside the Norte de Santander provincial police headquarters in Cúcuta, on Colombia’s border with Venezuela, wounding eight police officers and three civilians. The blast tore off much of the building’s roofing and shattered its windows, leaving the truck engulfed in flames and damaging surrounding structures. The government blamed the ELN, the country’s largest remaining insurgent group.

The attack came six days before the inauguration of president-elect Abelardo de la Espriella, whose victory marks a sharp turn to the right in Bogotá. Cúcuta sits on the smuggling corridor that has become the focal point of Washington’s regional operations since the January seizure of the Venezuelan government, and the region’s armed groups are now operating in a theatre defined by direct US military intervention.

Ten climbers including Nirmal Purja killed in Broad Peak avalanche

All ten climbers struck by an avalanche Thursday on Broad Peak, the world’s twelfth-highest mountain at 26,414 feet in Pakistan’s Gilgit-Baltistan, have been confirmed dead. Among them was Nirmal Purja, 43, the Nepali-born former British special forces soldier who set the record for the fastest ascent of all fourteen 8,000-meter peaks and became internationally known through the Netflix documentary 14 Peaks: Nothing Is Impossible.

Also killed were Nepali climber Pur Bahadur Gurung, Omani climber Nadhira Al Harthy — the first Omani woman to summit Everest — and American climber Mallory Geis. Recovery operations continued through the weekend. The deaths again raise the largely unexamined question of the commercialization of high-altitude mountaineering and the Nepali and Pakistani workers on whom the industry depends.

UEFA declares loss of confidence in FIFA president Infantino

UEFA announced Saturday it has lost confidence in FIFA president Gianni Infantino and that “no option should be off the table,” pledging a full review of his abandoned plan to sell stakes in the World Cup to private equity investors. Infantino had proposed “FIFA Forward Enterprise,” a $20 billion company to run the World Cup with outside investors. Opposition mounted daily from Tuesday’s announcement; his senior adviser, who sat on a White House panel, resigned, and Asia’s confederation joined Europe and North America in opposition. Infantino scrapped the plan early Saturday.

Per FIFA statutes, 20% of the 211 national associations — 43 countries — must request an extraordinary Congress to place a no-confidence vote on the agenda. UEFA’s 55 members alone exceed that threshold. The episode is a compressed illustration of the financialization of global sport: the attempt to convert a tournament watched by billions into a private-equity asset was defeated not by any democratic body of players or supporters, but by rival bureaucracies protecting their revenue share.

First Americans lose Medicaid under work requirements

Nebraska became the first state to disenroll people under the Medicaid work requirements enacted in Trump’s budget law, removing roughly 200 beneficiaries on August 1 for failure to document compliance. State estimates put eventual losses in Nebraska alone at around 30,000. Most adults aged 19–64 without dependents in the 40 expansion states plus DC must now document at least 80 hours per month of work, job training, education or community service.

Projections indicate between 4.9 and 10.1 million people will lose coverage by 2028, and — critically — that between 19% and 37% of those who already work will nonetheless lose coverage, largely because of documentation barriers. Montana began enforcement July 1; Arkansas is running a “soft launch” with no disenrollments until the federal January 1, 2027 deadline; California, New York, Illinois and Washington will implement at the deadline.

CWA and IBEW extend Verizon contracts to 2030, pre-empting a strike

The collective agreements covering roughly 20,000 Verizon workers across the Northeast and mid-Atlantic expired August 1. No strike occurred: following a third round of “early bargaining,” CWA District 1 members ratified contract extensions running to August 3, 2030, with CWA District 2-13 and the IBEW locals in New England, New York and New Jersey also ratifying. The unions promoted the deals as containing wage increases and improvements to retirement security and retiree health care “with no givebacks.”

Retiree health care had been identified by Labor Notes as the central issue heading into 2026, and the 2016 Verizon strike lasted seven weeks. The mechanism here — pre-emptive early bargaining that removes the expiration date as a point of leverage and forecloses a strike before members can organize one — is the same instrument being deployed across the 2026 contract cycle. Separately, 24,600 National Nurses United members at Kaiser in Northern California face a contract expiring this month.

US measles total passes 2,371, elimination status “highly likely” to fall

The CDC confirmed 53 new measles cases in its latest weekly update, bringing the 2026 US total to 2,371 confirmed cases across 45 jurisdictions. That already exceeds the 2,289 cases recorded in all of 2025, itself the highest annual total since 1991. Utah leads with 514 cases. Ninety-three percent of patients are unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status.

CIDRAP analysis concludes the United States — measles-free since 2000 — is now “highly likely” to lose its elimination status when the Pan American Health Organization reviews transmission data this fall. Losing elimination status requires twelve months of continuous transmission of a single chain. This is the direct, measurable consequence of the dismantling of public health infrastructure and the elevation of anti-vaccine ideology to federal policy.

EU AI Act transparency obligations take effect today

As of today, August 2, 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act becomes enforceable. Providers and deployers must disclose AI use in direct human interactions, label AI-generated content, and disclose emotion recognition systems and deepfakes. Penalties reach €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover. The European Commission adopted implementation guidelines on July 20. This is the most consequential near-term AI regulatory deadline anywhere in the world, affecting essentially any enterprise using generative systems to produce public-facing material. Notably, it addresses disclosure rather than the underlying questions of ownership, labor displacement, or military application.

COVID hospitalizations rising in 41 states

CDC’s ensemble forecast projects approximately 1,600 new confirmed COVID-19 hospital admissions for the week ending August 1, up from 1,200 for the week ending July 18. As of July 29, infections were estimated to be growing in 41 states and declining in none, with the earliest signs of increase concentrated in the West and South. Updated vaccine uptake among adults stands at just 17.5%. National activity is shifting from “low and stable” toward growth — the pattern that preceded prior late-summer waves.

Nearly 50% of US adults are projected to have obesity by 2030, up from 35% in 2010 — a trajectory inseparable from food industry profit structures, the cost of fresh food, and the intensification of working hours.

Arts and culture

Vincent Pastore, 1946–2026. The actor was found dead Saturday afternoon at his home in the Bronx, aged 80, apparently of natural causes. Pastore played Salvatore “Big Pussy” Bonpensiero on The Sopranos, and his character’s arc — an aging, financially squeezed soldier who becomes an FBI informant and is executed by his closest friends — was among the series’ most sustained explorations of loyalty, betrayal and the disintegration of a social milieu. Pastore came to acting late, after years running a New Rochelle nightclub, and brought to the role a lived familiarity with the world David Chase was dissecting. The Sopranos remains the most serious American television treatment of the decay of postwar working- and middle-class life in the New Jersey suburbs, and Pastore’s performance was central to it. His subsequent typecasting in a long series of mob roles is its own comment on how the industry processes talent.

Sam Neill, 1947–2026. The WSWS published Richard Phillips’s obituary of the New Zealand actor, who died aged 78. Phillips situates Neill as a key figure in the Australian and New Zealand new wave — a body of work including Sleeping Dogs, My Brilliant Career and later The Piano that emerged from a period of state film funding and genuine social questioning in both countries. Neill’s international career, from The Hunt for Red October to Jurassic Park to Peaky Blinders, made him a familiar face, but the more substantial achievement lies in the earlier films. Tributes came from Toni Collette (“You legend. You sweetheart.”), Karl Urban and Alan Cumming.

The pro-business record of departing Australian union chiefs McManus and O’Neil — Martin Scott

Sally McManus and Michele O’Neil are departing the ACTU leadership after a tenure defined by the enforcement of real wage cuts through the Fair Work Act’s suppression apparatus and through the Labor government’s Accord-style partnership arrangements. Scott traces the continuity from the Hawke-Keating Accords, under which the union bureaucracy took direct responsibility for policing wage restraint and enterprise bargaining, through to the present. The concrete measure of the record is the collapse of strike activity to historic lows alongside the deepest sustained decline in real wages since records began. The article’s programmatic conclusion is the one the ICFI has advanced consistently: the ACTU is not a workers’ organization that has been badly led but an industrial police force, and the way forward lies through rank-and-file committees independent of it.

Australian pseudo-left seeking to head off rebellion against union bureaucracy by Victorian teachers — Committee for Public Education

The CFPE documents the intervention of Socialist Alternative and allied groups among Victorian teachers, whose function is to channel opposition to the Australian Education Union leadership back into demands that the same officials “fight harder” — the standard mechanism by which a rebellion against an apparatus is converted into pressure on it. The article is useful as a case study in how pseudo-left formations operate as the last line of defence for union bureaucracies once the bureaucracy’s own authority has been exhausted.

Australia: Significant opposition to union sellout deal at Western Sydney University — A WSU Rank-and-File Committee member

A first-hand account of the NTEU’s promotion of an enterprise agreement at Western Sydney University against substantial staff opposition, written by a rank-and-file committee member. Its value is documentary: the specific voting arrangements, the timing of the ballot, and the arguments made by NTEU officials to secure a result. It records the organizational form the WSWS advances — a committee constituted by workers themselves, publishing its own account — in operation.

UAW presidential debate reveals class divide between socialist autoworker Will Lehman and pro-apparatus candidates — Shannon Jones

Jones reports on the UAW presidential debate, contrasting Lehman’s programme — abolition of the apparatus, transfer of power to rank-and-file committees, international unification of autoworkers against the plant closures and mass layoffs sweeping the industry — with the competing candidates’ shared premise that the apparatus is to be reformed and preserved. The exchange is significant because it occurred inside an official union forum: the disputed 2022 election, the federal monitorship, and the collapse in participation have created conditions in which a socialist worker can put the question of the bureaucracy’s existence directly to the membership. Jones connects the debate to the ongoing rank-and-file struggles at Stellantis and among Detroit-area workers.

Union bureaucracy, DSA rally behind Troy Jackson after collapse of Graham Platner campaign — Jacob Crosse

Crosse documents the rapid redirection of union officialdom and DSA resources behind Troy Jackson in Maine following the collapse of the Graham Platner Senate campaign. The pattern is the recurring one: an insurgent campaign presented as a break with the Democratic establishment collapses, and the same apparatus that promoted it transfers its endorsement to a more conventional figure without any accounting. The article’s significance for editorial discussion is its concrete documentation of the DSA’s function as a recruiting and stabilizing mechanism for the Democratic Party in a period when the party’s base is disintegrating.

Berlin court declares the city’s transport company must continue an advertising campaign for the far-right portal “Nius” — Andy Niklaus

A Berlin court ruled that the BVG, the city’s public transport company, is legally obliged to continue carrying advertising for Nius, the far-right media portal financed by billionaire Frank Gotthardt. The BVG had sought to cancel the campaign. Niklaus places the ruling in the context of the German judiciary’s systematic use of formal legal doctrine — here, the neutrality obligations of a public entity — to compel state institutions to underwrite far-right propaganda, alongside the Bundeswehr’s rearmament and the AfD’s normalization. The article draws the connection the WSWS has developed since the Höcke rulings: the courts are not neutral arbiters being outmaneuvered but active participants in the rehabilitation of the far right.

Detroit legal aid strikers call for unity: “We should all be out together” — Lawrence Porter

Porter reports from the picket line of striking Detroit legal aid workers, who provide representation to tenants facing eviction and workers facing benefit denials while themselves working at poverty wages. The quotation in the headline — “We should all be out together” — reflects the sentiment Porter documents among strikers for a unified struggle across the isolated bargaining units into which they have been divided. The article connects the strike to the broader wave of struggles in the Detroit area and argues for the formation of a rank-and-file committee to link them.

Who died and why: COVID-19, class and mass death in the United States — Benjamin Mateus

Mateus’s analysis examines the class composition of COVID-19 mortality in the United States, drawing on excess mortality and occupational data to demonstrate that the pandemic’s death toll was distributed along class lines — concentrated among workers who could not work from home, in congregate housing, in meatpacking and warehousing and transit, and among those without access to paid leave. The article’s argument is that “who died and why” is not a medical question but a political one: the decision to keep workplaces open, taken jointly by both parties and enforced through the union apparatus, was a decision about whose lives would be expended. Mateus connects this to the current abandonment of surveillance and mitigation, and to the ICFI’s call for a global scientific elimination strategy. It is the most substantial analytical piece in the current WSWS slate and merits full reading.

Workers Struggles: Asia and Australia

The weekly compilation covering strikes and disputes across the region, including actions by garment, transport and public sector workers. Useful as a reference for identifying struggles not covered elsewhere in the international press.

Jacobin / DSA

“The Cuban Revolution Was a Revolution in Art and Culture” (Abel Prieto, August 1) — Jacobin gave its platform to Cuba’s former culture minister for a celebration of the revolution’s literacy campaign and artistic institutions. Prieto’s account is the official one: it presents the Cuban state’s cultural apparatus as the flowering of revolutionary creativity, with no mention of the Padilla affair, the Quinquenio Gris, the UMAP camps, or the ongoing detention of artists. The publication of an unchallenged apologia for a Stalinist cultural bureaucracy, at a moment when the Cuban state is presiding over blackouts and mass emigration, expresses Jacobin’s characteristic method: dissenting aesthetics, official politics.

“Mamdani-Style Mayors Should Study Peru’s Democracy Experiment” (Jared Abbott and Stephanie McNulty, July 31) — proposes participatory budgeting on the Peruvian model as a template for left-led US municipal administrations. The programmatic content is the administration of austerity with a consultative veneer: participatory budgeting distributes a fixed and shrinking pool of municipal funds while leaving untouched the bond market’s control over city finances.

“The New Spider-Man Is Pure Fun” (Eileen Jones) and “Take a Moment to Feel the Pain of the Rich” (Danny Dorling, July 31) round out the output. Dorling’s piece — a critique of press sympathy for billionaires — is unobjectionable as far as it goes, and goes no further than moral indignation.

Assessment: Jacobin’s orientation remains the reconciliation of a radical readership to municipal Democratic administration, with Stalinist historiography supplying the revolutionary credentials.

Michigan and Minnesota have disclosed intrusions into water treatment control systems attributed to Iran, with seven states affected. The story deserves treatment because the bourgeois press frames it exclusively as foreign aggression while omitting that Washington is simultaneously preparing strikes on Iranian energy infrastructure — the reciprocal targeting of the civilian infrastructure on which working people depend is now a routine instrument of both sides, and the working class in both countries pays.

Only one of 27 ballistic missiles was intercepted over Kyiv, killing nine. The interceptor shortage is a direct consequence of the redirection of US munitions to the Iran theater, and Israel’s refusal to release Iron Dome systems. This is the concrete meaning of a multi-front imperialist war: the workers of Kyiv are being killed because of allocation decisions made in Washington and Tel Aviv.

3. Sixty-seven dead at the Ceuta border: Fortress Europe’s death toll

Between 50,000 and 60,000 people crossed into Ceuta in 48 hours; 67 died drowning or crushed on a breakwater. The Spanish “socialist” government’s response was a 500-meter sea wall. The event demands analysis of the social crisis in North Africa driving the movement, the collaboration between Madrid and Rabat, and the role of the PSOE and its Podemos-derived allies in administering the border regime.

4. CWA and IBEW extinguish the Verizon contract fight before it begins

Contracts covering 20,000 Verizon workers expired August 1, but through “early bargaining” the unions locked members into extensions running to 2030 — removing the expiration date as leverage and foreclosing the strike that shut the company down for seven weeks in 2016. With 24,600 Kaiser nurses facing an August expiration and a heavy 2026 contract calendar, the mechanism deserves exposure now.

5. Pakistan-administered Kashmir: mass movement criminalized under anti-terrorism law

The Joint Awami Action Committee has been banned, its leaders arrested, dozens of protesters killed since June and at least 38 people disappeared, amid internet shutdowns. The movement began over electricity and flour prices — a working-class revolt being suppressed under counterterrorism powers by a Pakistani state that is simultaneously acting as a mediator in the US-Iran negotiations.

6. The private equity raid on the World Cup and the financialization of sport

Infantino’s $20 billion plan to sell stakes in the World Cup to private investors collapsed in four days, and UEFA has declared no confidence in him. The episode exposes how thoroughly global sport has been converted into a financial asset class — and that the only force able to block the sale was a rival bureaucracy defending its own revenue, with players and supporters entirely excluded from the decision.

With 2,371 cases already exceeding all of 2025 and 93% of patients unvaccinated, the CDC’s own data point toward the loss of elimination status the US has held since 2000. This is the measurable outcome of the destruction of public health infrastructure and warrants a full accounting of the policy decisions that produced it.
