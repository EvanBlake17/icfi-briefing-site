1. Democrats take both houses of Congress in sweeping rebuke of Trump and the Iran war

The Democratic Party won control of both the House and the Senate in Tuesday’s midterm elections. Republican losses ran deep into states long counted as safe. The AP reported that Democrats crossed the 218-seat House threshold, and House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries is in line to become the first Black speaker. In the Senate, Al Jazeera reports Democrats had won 53 seats and led in three more as of early Wednesday. It is the first time since Obama’s second term that a sitting president controls neither chamber. The new Senate majority takes power on January 3.

Trump conceded the scale of the defeat but denied responsibility. “Well, I say this: congratulations to the Democrats. They did a good job,” he told Fox News. He added, “I don’t view it as a hit on me,” then acknowledged, “I think a part of it was Iran.” On Truth Social he claimed that if he had been on the ballot “we would have won IN A LANDSLIDE!” A White House official told reporters to “buckle up” for retaliation against “the Radical Left.” As Patrick Martin wrote in today’s WSWS Perspective, the vote was “an overwhelming repudiation of Donald Trump and the policies of his government.” It also revealed an electorate well to the left of the Democratic Party that will now take office.

Key facts: - Senate flips (per Al Jazeera/AP): - Texas: James Talarico beat Ken Paxton by more than 11 points, the first Democratic statewide win in Texas in 32 years. - Kansas: Adam Hamilton beat Roger Marshall, the first Democratic senator from Kansas since the 1930s. - Iowa: Josh Turek beat Ashley Hinson by about 4 points. - South Carolina: Annie Andrews beat Darline Graham, Lindsey Graham’s sister and appointed successor. - North Carolina: Roy Cooper beat Michael Whatley by nearly 20 points. - Maine: Troy Jackson beat Susan Collins by almost 7 points, ending her three decades in the seat. - Ohio: Sherrod Brown beat John Husted. More than $500 million was spent on the race, one of the costliest Senate contests in US history. - Michigan: NPR and Al Jazeera report Abdul El-Sayed defeated former Rep. Mike Rogers. He will be the first Muslim US senator. - Georgia: Democrat Jon Ossoff held his seat against Mike Collins. - AP voter survey: About 6 in 10 voters strongly disapprove of Trump. About 4 in 10 named the economy as the top issue and about 3 in 10 the state of US democracy, more than named crime or immigration. - Powers Democrats gain: They can block cabinet, ambassadorial and judicial confirmations, including any Supreme Court vacancy, and gain subpoena power. Sen. Richard Blumenthal, expected to chair the Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, said, “We will use our subpoena power, absolutely.” Democrats could also restrict Iran war funding, but Trump’s veto stands unless two-thirds of both chambers override it. - Jeffries: He hailed “the start of a great American comeback” and promised to pursue “corruption accountability,” not an end to the war.

2. US bombs Iran’s Qeshm Island as Pentagon readies post-election offensive

On Monday night, the eve of the election, US warplanes struck Qeshm Island at the mouth of the Strait of Hormuz. Trump confirmed the attack Tuesday, saying Iran had built “an anti-aircraft facility. It took them about two months.” He called it “just a minor attack. It wasn’t war,” and added: “I’m not looking to, you know, kill lots of people, but if we have to, we’ll have to.” US officials described the strikes as “defensive,” a response to Iranian fire on an American aircraft. The Wall Street Journal reported that several weeks of smaller US attacks had gone unannounced so they would not look like a return to major combat. US media, citing officials, report that Central Command has plans for strikes that could begin as early as Wednesday, once three US carriers are in the region.

The diplomatic track is still running, through Qatar. Iran has offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days if the US meets seven demands, including lifting sanctions and ending the naval blockade of Iranian ports. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, said Washington’s reply “was not convincing.” Qatari foreign ministry spokesman Majed al-Ansari said both sides “remain committed to the negotiation process.” Trump told the Financial Times the US was “getting everything we want.” Iran’s chief negotiator told NDTV that “Americans said no to war” at the polls. Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS that the timing points to an administration “preparing a major escalation” in open defiance of mass opposition to the war.

Key facts: - Netanyahu: According to AP, Netanyahu pressed Trump by phone over the weekend for a “quick but extensive air campaign” after the election. - Iranian posture: Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei reportedly ordered the military to respond to any threat “without geographical limits” and to update its target list. Iranian officials suggested that European countries and undersea cables in the Gulf could be targets. - IRGC claims: - Four oil tankers were destroyed by naval mines in a prohibited lane south of the strait, and two more vessels were detained. - An MQ-9 Reaper drone was shot down at 4:30 a.m., which the IRGC called its 56th. - UKMTO: Two projectiles hit a tanker’s engine room 14 nautical miles northeast of Limah, Oman. CBS reported a separate strike on an Indian-flagged cargo ship off Oman that killed one crew member. - Mearsheimer: Political scientist John Mearsheimer noted that the first US-Israeli campaign lasted 40 days and failed to settle the conflict.

3. Russia pounds Ukraine’s power grid and a Kharkiv hospital ahead of Abu Dhabi talks

Overnight into Tuesday, Russia launched what Zelensky called a “complex, massive attack”: 289 attack drones, 17 ballistic missiles and 59 cruise missiles. Power plants and substations in nine regions were hit, from Odesa and Vinnytsia to Chernihiv and Rivne. A strike on a Kharkiv city clinical hospital injured at least 73 people and damaged the cardiology ward. Mayor Ihor Terekhov said more than 1,000 windows were blown out. Russia’s defence ministry said it hit power facilities that supply “the Ukrainian army and military factories.” The ISW assessed that neither side advanced on the ground on November 3.

The barrage followed the end of a local truce in Kherson, the longest of the war, which Russia refused to extend. A day earlier, Russian bombs hit a village school used as an aid distribution point in Zaporizhzhia region. Ukrainian authorities first put the dead at 26; Al Jazeera later reported at least 33, and France 24 reported dozens more missing. Danielle Bell, head of the UN human rights mission, said the attack likely broke international law, because the time and place of the distribution had been “well publicised.” US envoy Steve Witkoff said US, Russian and Ukrainian officials will meet in Abu Dhabi next week, with talks reported for November 10–11.

Key facts: - Blackouts: More than 180,000 people in Zhytomyr were without power Tuesday morning, according to Governor Vitalii Bunechko. Odesa introduced scheduled outages. - Ukrainian strikes: A Ukrainian drone strike on the Yaroslavl oil refinery injured two workers. Kyiv has signalled it might pause refinery strikes in exchange for Patriot missiles. - Brussels meeting: This week US negotiators also meet France, Germany, the UK, the EU and NATO in Brussels on “security guarantees” and Europe’s post-war “security architecture.” - Aid: Al Jazeera notes that Washington has sent no significant new military aid package since Trump took office, and that Europe has filled the gap. - Hacked alert apps: Cyberattacks on both countries’ emergency-alert apps sent fake nuclear-strike warnings. Each government blamed the other.

4. French students blockade schools on the eve of a national strike as youth revolt spreads across Europe

Students blockaded dozens of high schools and universities across France on Monday and Tuesday as classes resumed after the autumn holiday. They were reviving a movement that the government had tried to defuse with concessions. Trade publications report that 51 higher-education institutions were disrupted. Unions have called a national strike for Thursday, November 5, and SNCF rail, regional RER and air services are all expected to be hit. A cruise operator has already cancelled a Le Havre call. The movement began in September at high schools in the working-class northern suburbs of Paris, over teacher shortages and dilapidated buildings. It has grown into a revolt against inequality, the cost of living and rearmament. Protesters wear high-visibility vests, consciously recalling the Yellow Vests.

The Lecornu government has answered with mass policing, gathering bans and talk of a state of emergency. On October 17, the Interior Ministry counted 550,000 marchers and the CGT 800,000. Prime Minister Lecornu reported about 1,200 arrests that day, and a teenager lost a hand after picking up a stun grenade. Macron branded the protesters “sowers of chaos.” Researchers quoted by France 24 say the youth protests now spreading across the continent share common grievances over housing, rents and job insecurity.

Key facts: - Portugal: Housing protests continued in Lisbon this week (AP). Earlier rallies drew tens of thousands. - Italy: Police used tear gas in Milan and water cannon in Bologna in October. The government has since sent 500 soldiers to Milan, according to the Khaleej Times. - Albania and Serbia: Albanian students have demanded Prime Minister Edi Rama’s resignation, and police reportedly fired rubber bullets. Serbian youth have joined the wave. - Borrowing costs: France’s borrowing costs are at their highest in more than two decades, which is driving the government’s austerity agenda. - Canada: Quebec’s new premier, Paul St-Pierre Plamondon, endorsed Macron’s crackdown. The WSWS reports he posted his “condemnation of the unrest fomented by the far left” after an October 26 call with the French president.

5. Spain’s transport minister resigns after rail collision kills 69

Spanish Transport Minister Óscar Puente resigned on Wednesday and withdrew from the coming general election. He said a minister “cannot continue in office” after two severe accidents in under a year. Last week, a high-speed train run by state-owned Renfe slammed into a stationary train run by the private operator Iryo on the Madrid–Seville line in Castilla-La Mancha, killing 69. Trains on that stretch had been ordered to stop after a signal failure, but central controllers in Madrid let the Renfe train proceed. Its speed at impact is estimated at between 60 and 200 km/h. In January, a high-speed derailment in Andalusia killed 46.

The Sánchez government’s answer is a €30 million victims’ package, completion of the long-delayed European Rail Traffic Management System (ERTMS), and an AI system to monitor communications between drivers and controllers. Puente says Spain would be the first country to deploy one. Services on the line remain reduced while the Railway Safety Agency reviews the line’s safety systems.

Key facts: - Compensation: Bereaved families receive €72,121 in direct aid plus an equal advance on compensation. - Operators: The crash involved both the state operator and a private competitor running on the liberalised high-speed network. Bourgeois coverage has not examined the role of competition and cost-cutting in signalling and control.

Europe

German government answers Gen Z protests with threats of bans and harsher sentences

On October 31, more than 120,000 young people demonstrated in over 30 German cities against education cuts, rising living costs, militarism and the return of conscription. Merz’s government responded by doubling down. Spokesman Stefan Kornelius said the chancellor felt “encouraged to continue his policies.” Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt (CSU) blamed riots at the margins of mostly peaceful marches on “violent left-wing groups and migrant troublemakers.” Hesse Interior Minister Roman Poseck (CDU) would not rule out banning the youth protests planned for November 20. The police union DPolG is demanding a one-year minimum sentence for breach of the peace, pretrial detention and systematic deportations. As Johannes Stern wrote on the WSWS, the riots are being used “to criminalize the protests as a whole.”

  • Public support: A Forsa poll published November 3 found 83 percent of respondents sympathetic to the demonstrations.
  • Defence budget: It rises by almost a third in 2027 while pensions, health care and education are cut.
  • Left Party: Co-chair Luigi Pantisano says only “strong, peaceful protests” can force Merz to back down.

European troops rehearse a post-ceasefire Ukraine deployment in Poland

The European “multinational force” being prepared to deploy in Ukraine after any ceasefire will hold its first exercise in Poland from November 9 to 22, with about 2,800 troops from 10 countries. A “Coalition of the Willing” summit follows in Berlin on November 19. Separately, Prime Minister Donald Tusk said Polish forces stopped an overnight “provocation” on the border with Russia’s Kaliningrad exclave, but gave few details. Poland’s constitutional crisis is also deepening: the European Commission has urged President Karol Nawrocki to recognise judges appointed under Tusk.

Sabotage wave hits European supermarkets and airlines

A second wave of cyberattacks hit supermarket refrigeration systems in at least eight EU countries, after a similar attack in France in October. Freezer temperatures suddenly spiked and stock spoiled. The EU called it a “deliberate attempt to disrupt” the bloc’s food supply but named no culprit. On Tuesday, bomb threats against at least 10 European airlines temporarily closed Warsaw airport and disrupted Ryanair flights. No explosives were found. These incidents, along with the hacked Ukrainian and Russian alert apps, are being folded into the official narrative of Russian “hybrid war” that is used to justify rearmament budgets.

Africa

South Africans vote in local elections as ANC support heads below 40 percent

South Africans vote today for 8 metropolitan, 44 district and 205 local councils. A record 29.2 million people are registered at 23,696 voting stations, and final results are due Sunday. Polls suggest the ANC, which lost its parliamentary majority in 2024 and now governs nationally with the Democratic Alliance, could fall below 40 percent. Turnout in 2021 was only 46 percent. Johannesburg, the main prize, has had five mayors in four years. The DA’s Helen Zille, 75, polls at 36 percent there (News24) and says the ANC is “in free fall.” Jacob Zuma’s uMkhonto weSizwe (MK) party is contesting local seats for the first time and is challenging strongly in Durban, where anti-migrant agitation is a central campaign theme. Ramaphosa conceded that “municipal failures have contributed to declining public trust.”

  • Coalition councils: As of February, 74 of 257 municipalities were run by coalitions or minority administrations.
  • Risk: KwaZulu-Natal has 165 high-risk voting stations, and police have opened 21 election-related cases since Sunday.

Global markets moved unevenly as the US election results came in. Tuesday’s selloff in Asia was led by tech and AI stocks, on investor fears that a stronger Democratic showing would mean tighter regulation of the sector. Australia’s central bank lifts rates to an 18-year high

The Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash rate by 25 basis points to 4.85 percent in a split 6–3 vote, the highest level since 2008. All four big banks passed the rise on to variable mortgages immediately. Governor Michele Bullock said: “Some pain now will prevent even worse pain down the track.” She added that she was “not worried about the pace of increase in the unemployment rate,” which has risen to 4.7 percent. The RBA attributed the inflation surge to “materially higher global energy prices as a result of the Middle East conflict” and now forecasts headline inflation of 4.5 percent by December. Analysts are already flagging another hike in February.

Washington resident dies of H5N1 after dairy exposure

A Yakima County, Washington, resident with prior health conditions has died of H5N1 avian influenza after exposure on a dairy farm with infected cattle (AP via STAT). It is the first US bird flu death this year and the first linked directly to the cattle epizootic that has circulated in US herds since 2024. The CDC’s last global summary counted 12 human infections in Bangladesh, Cambodia and India between August 2025 and June 2026, three of them fatal. Cambodia has since reported its sixth case of 2026. Gutted federal surveillance and the lack of mandatory testing of dairy workers mean the true extent of human infection is unknown.

Child dies of measles in New York as US cases pass 4,800

A child in rural northern New York has died of measles, the state’s first measles death in three decades. CDC data through October 29 show 4,852 confirmed cases nationally in 2026 across 47 jurisdictions, with 43 new outbreaks and at least three deaths, already more than double 2025’s 2,289. About 96 percent of cases are outbreak-associated. Kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.4 percent from 95.2 percent in 2019–20, below the roughly 95 percent needed to stop transmission. Greengrass’s The Uprising revives the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381

Paul Greengrass’s new film dramatises the 1381 Peasants’ Revolt in England, the uprising that followed the Black Death and a war-driven economic crisis. It was triggered by a poll tax levied to pay for wars in France. Andrew Garfield plays “Ploughman,” a fictional Essex labourer, with Cosmo Jarvis as Wat Tyler and Jamie Bell as the radical priest John Ball. Greengrass wrote the script in 2020 and struggled for years to get it made; at one stage it was marketed as a Robin Hood origin story. He has said the script became more relevant as he worked on it, and that the feeling of “we’ve had enough” reflects how many people feel today.

Paul Bond’s WSWS review credits the film with taking the side of the oppressed and with conveying “pent-up anger finally erupting.” He singles out Garfield’s performance and Bell’s Ball. His main criticism is the coda, which turns Ploughman into a Robin Hood-style lone rebel, in effect individual protest rather than collective revolt. Pandemic, war-financed taxation and a ruling class turning to repression: the parallels with today are not lost on audiences.

A documentary tops the US box office for the first time since Fahrenheit 9/11
