1. Trump administration launches global economic war on Iran

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on Monday unveiled what the administration is calling “Operation Economic Outcast,” a global secondary-sanctions campaign intended to sever Iran from the world economy nearly six months into the US-Israeli war that began with the joint air campaign of February 28. Bessent framed the operation in the language of the Normandy landings — Trump had promised an “ECONOMIC D-DAY” — and told reporters the objective is “to sever every economic lifeline that sustains this tyrannical regime until Tehran stands alone.”

The Treasury designated 60 entities, individuals and vessels spread across the United Arab Emirates, China, Hong Kong, Singapore and Switzerland, targeting five sectors: shipping, oil, digital assets, gold and aviation. Bessent said Trump is personally telephoning heads of state with “specific requests” to halt trade with Iran, and issued a blanket warning that firms handling Iranian oil “will be targeted” and that governments that do not comply risk exclusion from the US dollar clearing system. Notably, the Washington Post reported that the administration held back its most severe measure — full secondary sanctions on Chinese purchasers of Iranian crude — a sign of the limits Washington faces in a confrontation with Beijing.

Key facts:

  • Bessent: “Iran now faces a very clear choice, with only two paths before them: complete global isolation and a subsistence economy, or a path back to normalcy with an opportunity to rejoin the global economy.”
  • Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi dismissed the campaign: “They cannot think of any other solution in confronting the great Iranian people, so they repeatedly put forward the same old plans.”
  • Tehran retains effective control of the Strait of Hormuz. More than 80 percent of liquid cargoes over the past two weeks have transited via the Omani channel, a UN-authorized route Iran rejects. Iran has granted selective passage to Iraqi tankers.
  • Parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf published a list of preconditions for reopening the strait, including the lifting of oil sanctions and an end to military threats. Iran’s Assembly is advancing legislation to impose “service fees” on Hormuz transits.
  • Iran’s security chief threatened to shut all traffic through the strait if any neighboring state joins the US economic campaign.
  • US gasoline prices are up nearly a dollar per gallon year over year, with Hormuz traffic still far below normal.

The campaign follows a war that has already killed Iranian Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and other senior officials and devastated Iranian infrastructure, without producing the political capitulation Washington sought. The turn to blockade economics — starving a population of 90 million to force regime change — is the continuation of that war by other means, and it places every major trading nation on notice that neutrality will not be tolerated.

2. Zelensky rules out wartime elections as Ukraine jails antiwar socialist

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, in remarks published over the weekend and amplified across the international press Monday, definitively rejected holding elections while the war continues. “Elections right now are a tsunami for the state that will split Ukraine,” he said. “If we want to destroy the country, then during a war like this we can move toward elections.”

The immediate trigger was an August 18 video message from Mykhailo Fedorov, the defense minister Zelensky dismissed in July, who argued that Ukraine faces a “systemic crisis of governance” resolvable only through a wartime vote — the first such demand from a major Ukrainian political figure since February 2022. In the same set of remarks Zelensky disclosed that Ukraine’s stock of Patriot interceptors has fallen sharply as Western deliveries declined over three years even as Russian missile strikes intensified, and predicted Moscow will conscript an additional 300,000 troops after Russian parliamentary elections on September 18-20.

The Perspective published on the WSWS Tuesday by Johannes Stern places these statements in the context of a systematic destruction of democratic rights. Since March 2022 Zelensky has suspended eleven opposition parties by decree, roughly a dozen of which have since been banned outright by the courts, including the Socialist Party and the Communist Party; independent broadcasting has been merged into a single state telethon; and the WSWS itself has been banned in Ukraine since June 2024.

Key facts:

  • Bogdan Syrotiuk, a 27-year-old Ukrainian Trotskyist arrested in April 2024, was sentenced on August 10, 2026 to 15 years for “high treason.” The prosecution’s entire evidentiary basis was six WSWS articles and his private correspondence with WSWS editors — writings calling on “Russian and Ukrainian workers to unite” against the war.
  • A June 2026 Gallup poll found 66 percent of Ukrainians favor a negotiated peace, against 24 percent who support continuing to fight.
  • CSIS estimates put Ukrainian casualties at 500,000-600,000, including roughly 140,000 dead, against Russian casualties of 1.2 million and about 325,000 dead.
  • Elections during martial law are prohibited under Ukrainian law; analysts cite the millions of Ukrainians abroad, under occupation, or at the front.
  • More than 1,249 signatories from 65 countries, and organizations in at least 11 countries, have joined the campaign for Syrotiuk’s release since his sentencing.

3. Israeli strikes kill four in Gaza, destroy Gaza City desalination plant

Israeli airstrikes and tank fire killed at least four Palestinians in Gaza on Monday, including two children — one of them a four-year-old killed when a strike hit a building in the Bureij refugee camp in central Gaza — hospital officials said. The killings came a week after a US delegation formally asked Israel to reduce the tempo of its attacks.

Israeli forces also destroyed the water desalination plant serving Gaza City’s Sheikh Radwan neighborhood, one of the enclave’s few remaining sources of potable water, and struck a tent sheltering displaced Palestinians. The Israeli military and Shin Bet said they had targeted two Hamas weapons-storage sites located inside mosques in Shati and Deir al-Balah. Hamas described the destruction of the desalination plant and the attacks on displacement camps as a continuation of the genocide and a violation of the ceasefire framework.

Key facts:

  • The destruction of water infrastructure — desalination, wells, and pumping stations — has been a consistent feature of the campaign, and is the mechanism by which mass death continues after the formal fighting is scaled back.
  • Israeli police arrested two people on suspicion of assaulting an elderly Palestinian amputee in the West Bank, one of a rising number of settler assaults.

4. Supreme Court clears path for Trump’s mail-in ballot restrictions

The Supreme Court on Monday handed the Trump administration a preliminary victory in its drive to restrict mail-in voting before the November midterms, lifting a June injunction issued by a Massachusetts federal judge that had blocked key provisions of Trump’s March executive order in California and 22 other challenging states. The vote was 6-3, along ideological lines.

The order at issue directs the executive branch to construct federal lists of “eligible voters” and instructs the US Postal Service to deliver mail ballots only to people appearing on those lists — an unprecedented federal intervention into an area of election administration constitutionally reserved to the states. The Court did not rule on the merits; it deferred adjudication of whether the president has any constitutional or statutory authority to issue such directives at all. A separate nationwide ruling by US District Judge Indira Talwani in Boston, issued August 11, still bars the Postal Service from implementing the directive.

Key facts:

  • The decision comes roughly ten weeks before the November 2026 midterms, with mail-ballot request deadlines already approaching in several states.
  • The three liberal justices dissented.
  • Legal analysts noted the majority offered no reasoning on the merits, using the emergency docket to allow implementation while litigation continues — the same procedural pattern that has characterized dozens of administration wins over the past 20 months.

5. US-Canada trade war escalates as Trump threatens 50 percent auto tariffs

Trump announced Monday that tariffs on all Canadian cars, trucks, automotive parts and steel will rise to 50 percent on January 1, 2027, days after Canadian negotiators left Washington without a deal and Prime Minister Mark Carney suspended talks. Trump added: “We don’t need Canada.”

Carney said he walked away because Washington’s demands would “destroy” Canada’s auto, steel and aluminum industries. Canadian officials told CBC that after negotiators had agreed in principle, US negotiators altered the text in the final hours before the deadline to exclude medium- and heavy-duty trucks from tariff relief. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer blamed Ottawa, saying “they wanted more.” Carney has pledged that Canada “will match those tariffs dollar for dollar.”

Key facts:

  • New 50 percent US tariffs are already set to take effect on roughly $20 billion in Canadian goods.
  • Roughly three-quarters of Canadian exports go to the United States; the integrated North American auto supply chain crosses the border multiple times per vehicle.
  • Tens of thousands of autoworkers in Ontario, Michigan and Ohio are directly exposed. Unifor and the UAW have offered no coordinated cross-border response, instead each appealing to their respective national governments.
  • The Washington Post framed the collapse as demonstrating “the limits of Trump’s aggressive trade strategy” — the first major partner to walk away rather than concede.

6. John Deere workers reject UAW-backed contract extension

Roughly 10,000 UAW members at John Deere voted down the company’s proposal to extend the current collective bargaining agreement from October 2027 through October 2029, the union announced Sunday. The proposal offered 4 percent wage increases in 2026 and 2027 plus a $3,000 signing bonus if ratified by the end of August.

UAW President Shawn Fain said the company “failed to make an offer that addressed the issues weighing on the minds of our members, especially job security,” and that the union will “see Deere at the table in 2027.” The offer contained nothing on the outsourcing of work from unionized plants and no plan to recall approximately 1,600 laid-off workers.

Key facts:

  • The 4 percent offer sits against a 3.4 percent inflation rate — effectively a wage freeze, and one locked in for three additional years.
  • Deere reported $5.03 billion in fiscal 2025 net income and spent a combined $2.8 billion on share buybacks and dividends.
  • Axios noted the rejection comes amid a boom in Deere’s AI-enabled autonomous equipment sales — technology directly implicated in the elimination of production and dealer-service jobs.
  • WSWS reporting notes that UAW officials awarded themselves raises of $10,000-$30,000 while presenting members with a 4 percent extension.

7. Hawk Fire forces mass evacuations on the edge of Reno

The Hawk Fire, which ignited over the weekend in the hills northwest of Reno, Nevada, has burned more than 15,000 acres, destroyed 32 homes and damaged six more, and forced the evacuation of 42,000 people at its peak. Nevada’s governor declared a state of emergency. By Monday evening containment had risen from zero to 27 percent and crews had stopped the fire short of the Somersett community; evacuation orders were reduced to 23,000 people, with another 40,000 under evacuation warning.

Key facts:

  • At least four civilians and three first responders have been injured.
  • Nearly 10,000 customers lost power.
  • A preliminary investigation determined the fire was human-caused.
  • The United States has recorded more than 50,000 wildfires so far in 2026, a record pace.

The fire is the latest instance of urban-wildland communities left with no meaningful protective infrastructure — no undergrounded lines, no defensible-space enforcement, no funded evacuation capacity — while utility profits and municipal budgets are directed elsewhere.

Lockerbie bombing trial postponed days before jury selection

US District Judge Dabney Friedrich on Monday postponed the federal trial of Abu Agila Mohammad Mas’ud Kheir Al-Marimi, the former Libyan intelligence official charged with building the bomb that destroyed Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie, Scotland, on December 21, 1988, killing 270 people. Jury selection had been scheduled to begin Wednesday in Washington, DC — the first US trial arising from the bombing.

Friedrich cited “the newly discovered evidence, as well as evidence located in other countries” and “the need for the defense to determine how best to defend this case.” She set no new trial date and scheduled a hearing for September 1. Last week she had warned prosecutors to “proceed at your own peril.” The defense had requested a year-long delay. The case has been marked from the outset by questions about the provenance of Mas’ud’s confession, obtained in Libyan custody, and about the forensic evidence chain that also underpinned the contested conviction of Abdelbaset al-Megrahi.

National Guard deployment in Washington DC extended to January 2029

The Pentagon has confirmed that the federalized National Guard deployment in Washington, DC will continue until January 20, 2029 — the end of Trump’s term — “or until terminated by the President.” Approximately 2,400 Guard members remain stationed in the District, drawn from Republican-governed states including Florida, South Carolina, Georgia and Ohio.

The practical effect is the establishment of a standing federal military garrison in the national capital, staffed by units under governors politically aligned with the White House — a permanent institutional precedent regardless of the stated law-enforcement rationale.

US measles cases reach 2,777, the highest since elimination

The CDC’s latest weekly update, covering data through approximately August 20, added 211 new measles cases, bringing the 2026 US total to 2,777 — the highest annual count since measles was declared eliminated in the United States in 2000. Ninety-four percent of cases are outbreak-associated.

Pennsylvania led with 89 new cases; Lancaster County alone accounts for 48 percent of the state’s 379 total. Ohio added 73 cases, reaching 142. The proximate driver is the collapse in kindergarten vaccination coverage, which has fallen from 95.2 percent in 2019-20 to 92.4 percent in 2025-26 — well below the roughly 95 percent threshold required for herd immunity against a pathogen with an R0 of 12-18.

H5N1 tears through Pennsylvania poultry

USDA APHIS confirmed six new H5N1 detections in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania poultry facilities this week, including egg-layer operations with 2.6 million and 1.4 million birds respectively. Nationally, 51 flocks totaling 8.97 million birds have been affected in the past 30 days. The CDC reported no unusual human influenza indicators as of August 7.

Internationally, Australia recorded its first mass-mortality H5N1 event in wild birds, and New Zealand officials warned the virus may become endemic there within months — the closing of the last major geographic gap in the panzootic. The mass-culling response protects the commercial flock but does nothing about the reservoir, and each additional mammalian and avian host expansion increases the probability of a reassortment event.

COVID-19 growing in 47 states

CDC Center for Forecasting and Outbreak Analytics Rt modeling as of August 19 shows COVID-19 infections growing or likely growing in 47 states, with infections declining in no state. Test positivity rose from 2.1 percent to 2.4 percent in late July, with the sharpest increases in the West and South. A month-long summer wave is building nationally, though absolute levels remain below prior summers following an unusually long preceding lull. Federal surveillance capacity has been progressively dismantled, leaving wastewater monitoring and Rt modeling as the principal remaining indicators.

Research and technology roundup

  • Universal flu vaccine manufacturing: Researchers reported a process for manufacturing influenza vaccines in baker’s yeast — faster and cheaper than egg- or cell-based methods, and designed to confer longer-lasting protection across multiple strains. If it scales, it could displace the annual reformulated shot, a significant prospect amid H5N1 spillover risk.
  • Medications reshape the gut microbiome long-term: A cohort study of more than 2,500 people found lasting alterations in gut microbial composition associated not only with antibiotics but with antidepressants and other common medications, persisting well after the last dose — implying drug-microbiome interaction is far broader than previously appreciated, with implications for long-term prescribing.
  • AI regulation: On August 22, OpenAI publicly urged California to strengthen SB 53, calling for monitoring of frontier models during training and evaluation and tougher cybersecurity requirements across the model-development lifecycle. This reverses the company’s opposition to the bill before its 2025 passage. The shift reflects the calculation of the dominant firms that a compliance regime they help write raises barriers for smaller competitors.
  • Climate and energy: A framework published August 24 in Nature Communications improves electricity demand forecasting during heatwaves and cold waves, showing how better use of sparse data can help grids manage extreme-weather risk. Separate work published August 22 in the same journal found that across most of the Amazon, forest carbon uptake and water loss do not covary as models assume — particularly in intact, wetter forests, a finding with direct consequences for carbon-cycle projections.

The Smithsonian under political review. The White House has placed eight Smithsonian museums into Phase I of a content review under the executive order “Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History,” ahead of the America 250 celebrations: the National Museums of American History, Natural History, African American History and Culture, and the American Indian; the National Air and Space Museum; the Smithsonian American Art Museum; the National Portrait Gallery; and the Hirshhorn. School shootings in the Philippines: The violence of the state comes home — John Malvar

Two school shootings have taken place in the Philippines in 2026 — in Tacloban City in June and in Zamboanga in August. In both cases the firearms used belonged to state security personnel. The article’s wider political point is that the Marcos administration’s continuity with Duterte’s methods — the retention of the police structures built for the drug war, the ongoing red-tagging of trade unionists and left activists — means the social pathology has no institutional check. “Yes, Let’s Talk About the Cookshops of the Future” and Aaron Benanav’s companion piece on Marx’s “anti-utopian line” (August 24) argue that socialists must engage concretely with how a post-capitalist economy would function.
