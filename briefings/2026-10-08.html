1. Russian glide bomb kills 33 at Kramatorsk bus stop a day after nationwide barrage

A Russian strike on a street in Kramatorsk on Thursday morning killed at least 33 people and wounded 18, Ukrainian authorities said. It was one of the largest single attacks on civilians this year. Police said two city buses were operating at the site, which President Zelensky described as a bus stop. Police photographs showed bodies across a debris-strewn street and the burned-out shell of a bus. The Times reported a glide bomb. Donetsk regional governor Vadym Filashkin called a bus full of passengers “an attractive target” and halted public transport in Kramatorsk and Sloviansk, the two main cities of the Donetsk “fortress belt” that Moscow insists Kyiv cede before any ceasefire. Drone strikes on Kyiv the same morning killed three and wounded 14, according to Mayor Vitali Klitschko.

The attack came 24 hours after one of the largest Russian barrages of the war. Overnight into Wednesday, Russia fired about 70 missiles, many of them ballistic, plus Shahed drones, at Kyiv, Chernihiv, Poltava, Kharkiv, Dnipropetrovsk and Kirovohrad regions. At least 24 people were killed. A missile destroyed a five-storey apartment building in Pryluky, killing at least 18, including four children, and wounding about 55. Zelensky called it “one of the most vile strikes” and noted that it fell on Putin’s 74th birthday. Russia’s Defense Ministry said it had hit sites where Ukraine’s Fire Point company builds Flamingo cruise missiles and long-range drones.

Key facts:

  • Ukraine’s air force said it downed just over 80% of 46 jet-powered drones, up from 50–60% in recent weeks. Zelensky said ballistic missiles remain the main gap and that Kyiv is “searching for Patriot systems.” Global Patriot supplies are stretched by the Iran war.
  • The EU paid Ukraine a new €1.2 billion tranche on Wednesday, which Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi said will go to defense. Fire Point has used its first domestically produced ballistic missile in combat and says its FP-9 will be able to reach Moscow.
  • Ukraine says it hit two oil facilities and a training ground in the Perm, Samara and Astrakhan regions within 24 hours. Ukrainian drones also struck a Yandex data center, the first confirmed strike on Russian data infrastructure. Sochi closed its beaches after unmanned boats set an oil tanker on fire.
  • In the Black Sea, drones hit two commercial vessels on October 6, and one sank off Bulgaria. Bulgaria has ended the search, and no survivors were found. Erdoğan pressed Putin on safe navigation and renewed his offer to help broker direct talks.
  • The UN says more Ukrainian civilians were killed and wounded in the first eight months of 2026 than in all of 2025. Secretary-General Guterres condemned the latest attacks.

2. Pentagon prepares new Iran strike options as Hormuz attacks surge

NBC News reports that the US military is preparing new options for war on Iran ahead of the November 3 midterms. CNBC reports that Washington is preparing for “massive bombing,” and Trump said on Wednesday that a deal “isn’t really something that I want to do.” The war began on February 28 and is now in its eighth month. Both sides are escalating even as the diplomatic track continues. In a Reuters interview, Vice President Vance demanded a “meaningful” reduction in Iran’s enrichment capacity: “If you don’t want a nuclear weapon, then why do you need 60 percent enriched fuel?” Analysts read this as a retreat from Washington’s earlier demand that Iran give up enrichment entirely. Speaking in Athens, Secretary of State Rubio claimed Iran had “lost complete control” of the Strait of Hormuz and that its economy was in “total and complete freefall.” Trump warned the war would end “the nice way or the not-so-nice way.”

Tehran’s position has hardened. President Pezeshkian called talks “futile,” saying “every time we come to the negotiating table, war is immediately imposed upon us,” and that the US had “attacked us three times following talks.” Trump rejected Iran’s late-September UN proposal. It would have ended fighting on all fronts, including Israel’s offensive in Lebanon, lifted the US naval blockade and oil sanctions, released frozen assets, and reopened Hormuz within seven days, with nuclear talks to follow.

Key facts:

  • The UK Maritime Trade Operations agency (UKMTO) counted nine attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz in the first week of October, half of September’s total for the strait and the Gulf combined. On Tuesday, 12 crew on a Panama-flagged tanker were injured. On Wednesday, a tanker was hit by several projectiles 94 km north of Qatar’s Madinat ash Shamal, the first attack in the western Gulf in weeks.
  • Despite the attacks, Kpler data put Gulf crude flows excluding Iran at 81% of pre-war levels in September. The seven-day average of Middle East crude exports reached 18.3 million barrels per day on September 30, above the 18 million pre-war baseline.
  • The Pentagon withdrew 12 B-1 bombers from RAF Fairford on October 4, and anonymous US officials have since alleged an Iranian drone plot against the base. As Robert Stevens noted on the WSWS, no one has been charged, police found only “a quantity of petrol,” and RUSI’s Air Marshal Greg Bagwell said the Pentagon “achieved the headlines they wanted” while masking a wider redeployment.
  • The carrier USS Abraham Lincoln is heading home after a long deployment. Andre Damon writes on the WSWS that a third carrier group and up to 10,000 more troops are being sent to the region.

3. Saudi war on the Houthis widens as Pakistani and Turkish forces deploy under the Mecca pact

Houthi missile attacks on Tuesday and Wednesday hit Abha International Airport and King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing three civilians and injuring 36, according to Saudi Arabia’s civil aviation authority, GACA. All three dead were foreign nationals. At Abha, a Moroccan woman and an Algerian woman were killed and 28 people were injured. In Riyadh, a Sudanese national was killed and eight were injured. The Houthis said they hit the Riyadh airport again with a ballistic missile on Thursday morning. Coalition spokesman Major-General Turki al-Maliki said a missile was intercepted north of the capital and a launch platform in Sanaa was destroyed. Aden airport was also targeted. Cage-like fencing has gone up around Aramco fuel tanks near the Riyadh airport, and the US embassy has issued another security alert.

The war has become a multinational operation. On October 5 in Riyadh, the top committee of the Mecca pact, signed in August by Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Türkiye, agreed to “move immediately to the practical implementation of the collective defense commitments.” Pakistan’s Defense Minister Khawaja Asif said Pakistani forces are already doing reconnaissance and that Turkish and Pakistani forces are “playing a supporting role,” while combat is left to Saudi forces. Ankara has not confirmed any deployment, and Türkiye’s parliament has not ratified the pact. Iran’s Foreign Ministry warned that intervention would “complicate the situation.” A Tehran analyst said Pakistani offensive operations against the Houthis would cross a red line.

Key facts:

  • Yemen’s internationally recognized government claims 2,103 “precise targeting operations” in its “Dawn of Yemen” counteroffensive and says 1,860 Houthi fighters have been killed. It says it has retaken Mocha and areas around the Bab al-Mandeb strait. It now says its goal is Sanaa, held by the Houthis since 2014.
  • Saudi-led forces have 100 fighter jets supporting the offensive. The Houthis have struck the East-West Pipeline, which Riyadh is using to bypass Hormuz.
  • Pakistan has not said how many troops it has in the kingdom. A 1985 CIA estimate put 13,000–14,000 Pakistanis in the Saudi army. In April 2015, Pakistan’s parliament voted unanimously to stay neutral in Yemen.
  • Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman said deterrence would be activated against “all those responsible.” Analysts read the deliberately vague wording as a warning to the Houthis’ external backers.

4. French government suspends stun grenades as student revolt enters fourth week

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez told RTL on Wednesday that he had suspended police use of stun grenades at student protests. On Monday in Lens, a 15-year-old lost a hand after police fired one. In Paris, a 14-year-old lost several teeth after being hit in the face at close range by a tear gas grenade, and a police officer has been charged. On Tuesday, hundreds of thousands of students, joined by parents, teachers and union members, marched across France for school funding, more teachers and repairs to crumbling buildings. Macron called an emergency meeting with Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, who then addressed the nation on the protests and public finances. By Thursday, Reuters reported that teenagers were keeping up school blockades “despite prime minister’s pledge to act.” The New York Times reported that the protests had spread to more than 40 cities. Police fired tear gas at a barricaded Paris high school.

The government says the movement has been “hijacked” by outsiders, and Lecornu denies any order for “police repression.” France has summoned Iran’s ambassador over alleged disinformation about the protests. Amnesty International has called for a permanent ban on stun grenades. Reuters reports tensions spreading to Belgium.

Key facts:

  • The Interior Ministry reports 6,100 arrests since late September, the large majority of them minors. The Justice Ministry is prosecuting more than 1,800 people, including 1,483 minors.
  • The prime minister’s office reports at least 715 police and 215 teenagers injured. Police have opened 18 internal misconduct investigations.
  • The Parti de l’égalité socialiste (PES), the ICFI’s French section, writes in today’s WSWS Perspective that nearly half a million people marched on October 6. It describes “a European mass movement” that “is colliding with the police state and the diktat of the capitalist oligarchy.”

5. Rahul Gandhi detained three times in two days as India voter purge passes 130 million

On Wednesday, police in New Delhi detained Congress leader Rahul Gandhi and other opposition MPs as they marched to Jantar Mantar, the city’s designated protest site. It was Gandhi’s third detention in two days. The protests demand the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar over the Special Intensive Revision (SIR) of electoral rolls, which has struck more than 130 million names from voter lists. About 969 million people were eligible to vote in 2024. Millions have appealed their removal. Gandhi called those responsible “traitors” and said Kumar is “not the legitimate commissioner.” The Election Commission and the Modi government say the revision only removes duplicate and ineligible entries.

The movement began on October 2 after an Indian Express investigation estimated that the SIR had removed 130–150 million names. It draws on opposition parties, their student wings, and the “Cockroach Janta Party” youth movement, whose protests over leaked exam papers earlier this year forced Modi’s education minister to resign.

Key facts:

  • According to Kranti Kumara on the WSWS, about 9 million names, roughly 12% of the electorate, were removed in West Bengal before the state election that the BJP won for the first time. Reports say BJP leaders distributed pre-filled Form-7 deletion requests targeting Muslim voters in Karnataka and Telangana.
  • On October 2, Delhi police imposed prohibitory orders, deployed about 4,000 officers plus paramilitary forces, closed 12 Metro stations and detained more than 500 people, including former Delhi Chief Minister Atishi.
  • The Indian Express reports that the other two election commissioners lodged at least 14 formal objections to Kumar’s decisions over ten months. All were ignored.
  • In August, Trump praised India’s electoral system and Kumar personally, citing them in support of the US SAVE America Act.

6. AfD wins Saxony-Anhalt parliament presidency, a first for the far right since 1945

On Tuesday, the Saxony-Anhalt state parliament elected AfD deputy Tobias Rausch as its president in a secret ballot. It is the first time since the Nazi dictatorship that a far-right deputy has held such a post in Germany. Rausch received 48 votes. The Sahra Wagenknecht Alliance (BSW) had announced its support, and at least four more votes came from other parties, almost certainly from the CDU. Five deputies abstained. The president of the parliament represents it externally, runs its administration and holds police powers inside the building. The AfD won 43.8% in last month’s state election but has no majority. A new Insa poll puts it at 42%.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz warned that “extremism is entering German parliaments” and cautioned against cooperation with the AfD. Wagenknecht celebrated: “The BSW is ending the firewall idiocy!” The AfD claims to have supporters within the CDU group. Attention now turns to December, when a similar secret ballot could make AfD leader Ulrich Siegmund the first AfD state premier. Separately, the AfD called this week for an end to German support for Ukraine.

Key facts:

  • Rausch joined the AfD in 2013 and is a close ally of Siegmund in the party’s openly völkisch state branch. He has attacked “left-green eco-madness” and called for “special classes” for Ukrainian pupils.
  • In Heidenau, Saxony, the CDU and AfD jointly voted to stop laying new Stolpersteine for Holocaust victims. They will instead commemorate all “victims of violence,” including Wehrmacht soldiers, at a single site.
  • As Christoph Vandreier wrote on the WSWS, the AfD’s program of a 5%-of-GDP arms budget, refugee deportations and a surveillance state “has been absorbed into official policy” by the Merz–Klingbeil government.

Spanish unions call first joint general strike in 14 years as evicted pensioner dies

María del Carmen Abascal, “Maricarmen,” died in a Madrid hospital at 87. On September 23 she was carried out on a stretcher from the apartment she had lived in for seven decades, after an investment fund bought her building and raised her rent by more than 200%, to about $1,880 a month, to clear it for tourist flats. Her eviction set off encampments in Puerta del Sol and protests in dozens of cities. On October 2, parliament struck down the government’s two housing decrees. Pedro Sánchez then called a snap election for November 29. On Thursday, hundreds rallied outside parliament after her death.

The UGT and CCOO have announced a nationwide general strike over housing for November 11, subject to ratification by other unions. It would be their first joint general strike in 14 years. The date falls 18 days before the election, which suggests the strike is being shaped to serve the PSOE’s campaign. Polls show Alberto Núñez Feijóo’s Popular Party leading but needing Vox, which is positioning itself as kingmaker.

  • The Bank of Spain estimates a shortage of 750,000 homes, rising past one million by 2028.
  • Madrid Tenants’ Union spokesperson Valeria Racu: “Housing is where most of our wages go.”
  • Spain’s Supreme Court has lifted the arrest warrant for Carles Puigdemont, who will return under the amnesty law.

Kushner and Dmitriev discuss bringing a US investor into Nord Stream

Reuters, citing five sources, reports that US envoy Jared Kushner and Putin adviser Kirill Dmitriev have discussed bringing a US investor into the sabotaged Nord Stream pipelines in meetings in Moscow and New York. Kushner has spoken of “prosperity agreements” that would give leaders “something to look forward to.” The two have also discussed selling Lukoil’s European refineries and petrol stations to a consortium of the US International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) and investors from Qatar and the UAE. A White House official said any deal “must benefit United States taxpayers and companies” and said Kushner and Witkoff had secured “a substantial upfront payment and profits interest for the United States.” Berlin said a restart is “not desired.” German lawmaker Michael Kellner called it “a dirty deal” in which “Americans and Russians will share the profits.” The AfD supports a restart, and Dmitriev is due to meet its leadership early next year.

  • Gazprom owns 51% of the pipeline company. Two German firms own 31%, and a French and a Dutch firm hold the rest.
  • The EU has agreed its 22nd sanctions package, the largest yet, covering 743 individuals and 826 entities, more than half linked to missile production. It brings the total to nearly 4,650. Foreign ministers will formally approve it in Luxembourg on Monday. The EU separately lifted its Belarus-related sanctions on billionaire Mikhail Gutseriev.

France tests M51.3 missile as Moscow issues nuclear warning over Kaliningrad

On Tuesday, France test-fired an M51.3 submarine-launched ballistic missile off Brittany. Macron called it a message “sent loud and clear for wherever it must be received.” Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Moscow had “received” the message and called it evidence of Europe’s “obsession with militarization” and a “dead end.” The test followed renewed Russian warnings that it could use nuclear weapons to defend Kaliningrad if NATO tried to isolate the exclave. Denmark said on Wednesday that Russia has carried out sabotage attacks on Danish defense companies, which one analyst called a major escalation of hybrid warfare.

Former German spy chief arrested for treasonous espionage

August Hanning, head of the BND (Germany’s foreign intelligence service) from 1998 to 2005, was arrested on Tuesday on suspicion of “treasonous espionage, obtaining state secrets, attempted treason and espionage.” So was Manfred D., office manager to successive BND presidents until March 2026. Prosecutors say Hanning paid for about 2,000 documents, including senior-level chancellery briefings on Iran’s nuclear program, the Russian military and material classified by allied agencies. He allegedly used them in an analysis for an unnamed foreign intelligence service. Spiegel reports a possible Azerbaijan link, and Süddeutsche Zeitung says Russia is not involved. Lawmakers called it the worst German intelligence scandal since the Cold War. BND President Martin Jäger said “there can be no compromises on internal security.” This is the same Jäger who told the Bundestag on Monday that Russia is Germany’s “most dangerous adversary” (see WSWS coverage below).

Ethiopia captures Mekelle as Eritrean troops reported inside Tigray

Witnesses told Reuters they saw dozens of Eritrean soldiers in two trucks in Adigrat, 25 km south of the border. If confirmed, it would be the first sighting of Eritrean forces inside Ethiopia since fighting resumed on September 23 between federal forces and a seven-group armed alliance that includes the TPLF. Federal troops took Tigray’s capital, Mekelle, on Monday after the TPLF withdrew. Ethiopia closed its embassy in Asmara and expelled ten Eritrean diplomats, and Eritrea then cut diplomatic ties. Eritrea’s information minister did not deny the crossing and accused Addis Ababa of a “war of aggression.” Abiy Ahmed, sworn in for a new five-year term, repeated that landlocked Ethiopia must have access to the Red Sea. Eritrea, Somalia, Sudan and Egypt issued a joint call for a political settlement. Addis Ababa accuses all four of backing the rebels.

Gaza one year into ceasefire: no hospital fully operational

On the third anniversary of October 7, an Israeli rights group reports that no hospital in Gaza has returned to full operation a year after the ceasefire. The National reports there are no working MRI scanners in the territory. The Gaza Health Ministry’s count stands at 74,250 Palestinians killed since October 2023, including 1,467 since the October 10, 2025 ceasefire. Britain announced that its diplomats will stay at a scaled-down Jerusalem mission. In New York, Democrats criticized Mayor Zohran Mamdani for an anniversary statement that mourned the October 7 victims while condemning Israel.

On the WSWS, Andre Damon brings together recent Haaretz, New York Times and NPR reporting. The reports say the UAE’s Mohamed bin Zayed, Egyptian intelligence chief Abbas Kamel, and an “earthquake” message relayed from Yahya Sinwar all warned Netanyahu before the attack. Damon argues that this confirms the WSWS’s 2023 assessment that Israeli officials stood down to create a pretext.

PQ forms Quebec minority government with record-low vote share

The Parti Québécois won 59 of 127 seats and will form a minority government, five short of a majority. It won about 28% of the vote, the lowest share for any Quebec government. Turnout was 66.9%. The CAQ, which had governed for eight years and won 90 seats in 2022, was wiped out entirely on 13.3%. The Liberals took 40 seats, Éric Duhaime’s Conservatives 19, and Québec Solidaire fell from 12 seats to 9. CBC reports renewed calls for electoral reform. The PQ has requested a recount in Vachon, which it lost by ten votes. Premier-designate Paul St-Pierre Plamondon has promised to eliminate a $7.7 billion deficit by 2028–29, cut about 16,300 public-sector jobs, create an “efficiency” secretariat, and hold an independence referendum late in his mandate. Alberta votes on October 19 in a referendum that includes a binding independence question.

Bond market alarm

Wall Street fell from Tuesday’s records as US government bond yields rose to their highest levels in more than two decades. The 10-year Treasury traded near 5.31% and the 30-year near 5.68%. Investors are betting that war-driven oil prices will keep inflation, and therefore interest rates, high. Equities and bonds are now moving in opposite directions. The debt-financed AI investment boom keeps pushing indices up, while the bond market prices in a long war. Higher yields pass directly into mortgage, auto and credit-card rates for working families.

Christa Pike wakes six days after surviving Tennessee lethal injection

On September 30, Tennessee gave Christa Pike two doses of pentobarbital. She is believed to be the first person in the US to survive a lethal injection. Her lawyer Randy Spivey said that on Monday night her team understood she was brain dead and that life support would be withdrawn the next day. On Tuesday she woke and asked “Where am I?” and “Who are you?” She is now “angry and confused” and walking again. Executioners spent an hour looking for a vein. Experts think the drug pooled in the tissue of her arm, which remains swollen and blistered. During the attempt she kicked off her sheet and said she was in pain. Before the execution she had warned officials about her veins and asked to be executed by firing squad.

  • Nashville Judge I’Ashea Myles ordered the state to preserve the drugs, syringes, IV lines, recordings and officials’ communications. Spivey will seek commutation.
  • Governor Bill Lee halted all executions, and Corrections Commissioner Frank Strada will resign this month. In May, Tennessee had already called off Tony Carruthers’ execution after failing to find a vein for an hour. A state lawmaker is calling for public hearings.
  • Texas went ahead on Wednesday night with the pentobarbital execution of Jamaal Howard, 46, after a court rejected appeals that cited Pike’s case. It was the first US execution since hers.
  • Maya Foa of the human rights group Reprieve wrote in the Chicago Tribune that the case shows “lethal injection is breaking down.”

Nobel week rewards chirality chemistry, optogenetics and neutrino astronomy

The chemistry prize went to Henri Kagan (France, 95) and Kenso Soai (Japan, 76) “for the discovery of nonlinear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis.” Their work explains how reactions can be steered toward one mirror-image form of a molecule. That matters for drug manufacture, since the two forms of a drug can act very differently. It also offers an account of how homochirality, life’s near-exclusive use of one form, could arise spontaneously. The committee called the reactions “spectacular.” Earlier in the week, the medicine prize recognized optogenetics, the control of neurons with light, with Stanford’s Karl Deisseroth among the laureates. The physics prize recognized Francis Halzen’s work on neutrino detection, the so-called “ghost particles.”

Arts and culture

Anne Carson wins the Nobel Prize in Literature. The Swedish Academy gave the 2026 prize to the Canadian poet, essayist and classicist Anne Carson, 76, for a “bold and inventive oeuvre” that engages playfully with the classical tradition and creates new forms for contemporary literature. She is the 19th woman to win and follows László Krasznahorkai. Carson trained as a philologist, and the Academy places her work where a philologist’s interest in words meets a poet’s search for new meaning. She has said the etymology of a word is where all her work starts. Her major books are Eros the Bittersweet (1986), on desire in Greek lyric; Autobiography of Red (1998), a novel in verse that reworks Stesichorus’ fragments about Geryon and Heracles; The Beauty of the Husband (2001); Nox, an elegy for her brother built from a translation of Catullus 101; her Sappho translations; and Norma Jeane Baker of Troy (2019), which sets Marilyn Monroe beside Helen.

This is a serious choice. Carson has done more than any living English-language writer to keep the literature of antiquity in active use. She treats Sappho, Euripides and Stesichorus as contemporaries, not museum pieces. Her limits lie in the same place. Her work is inward and erudite, and history appears in it mainly as personal grief and erotic loss. She has written little about the wars and social upheavals of her lifetime. The prize recognizes a real artist, but one whose project, unlike that of a Brecht or a Neruda, does not face outward toward the world.

Eva Marie Saint dies at 102. Saint made her film debut as Edie Doyle in Elia Kazan’s On the Waterfront (1954) and won the Oscar for Best Supporting Actress. She went on to star opposite Cary Grant in Hitchcock’s North by Northwest (1959). Her performance in On the Waterfront, tender and morally alert opposite Brando, is among the finest of the decade. It sits inside a film that Kazan and screenwriter Budd Schulberg, both friendly witnesses before the House Un-American Activities Committee, built as a defense of informing, with the longshoremen’s union cast as nothing but a mob racket. Saint’s work survives the film’s politics. Her career also took in A Hatful of Rain, Exodus and The Russians Are Coming, the Russians Are Coming. She cut back her film work in the 1960s to raise her family.
