1. Israel seals the West Bank and launches a Hebron operation days before its election

On Friday, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ordered a full lockdown of the occupied West Bank and the closure of Hebron. He also announced preparations for a military operation he described as one “not carried out since” Israel conquered the territory in 1967. The trigger was an attack near the Telem settlement outside Hebron, in which two Palestinian gunmen shot five soldiers from a reserve battalion. Two of the soldiers, Staff Sgt. Ron Yonatan Roytman, 23, and Sgt. Yuval Shai Svilem, 20, died. The army said it killed both attackers after a gunfight of about 20 minutes. On Saturday Israeli forces raided at least 15 towns in the northern West Bank, including Nablus, Jenin, Tubas and Qalqilya, as well as Jericho and the Aqabat Jabr refugee camp, and detained dozens of people. The army said the Hebron operation will last “for several days.” Israel’s national election is on Tuesday, October 27.

Netanyahu vowed to destroy the neighborhood the gunmen came from and to “implement the successful and aggressive model” used in the refugee camps, going “house to house.” Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich called for a campaign to dismantle the Palestinian Authority, although nothing links the PA to the attack, and ordered the destruction of “a large swath of illegal Arab structures.” On the WSWS, Andre Damon noted that the lockdown came four days after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth visited Netanyahu. He argued that the extension of the Gaza war to the West Bank is being carried out “in alliance with the Trump administration,” in step with plans for a renewed assault on Iran.

Key facts: - Lockdown scale: nearly 1,000 checkpoints and gates are closed, affecting millions of Palestinians (Al Jazeera). - Hebron: bulldozers knocked down a three-storey building and gave the families five minutes to collect their belongings. Hundreds of families in the Ein Deir Bahha area were ordered out of their homes, and troops raided the al-Jalda neighborhood. - Katz: residents of “every clan neighborhood” in Hebron linked to the attack will be evacuated, “Just like in Gaza …” - Ben-Gvir: the national security minister called for full Israeli control of the West Bank and said of Palestinians, “Their movements on the road should be stopped.” - Mustafa Barghouti (Palestinian National Initiative) said the closure turns every town into “a small prison” and that collective punishment “will lead to explosions.” - Settler violence: UN OCHA counted “at least 160” settler attacks linked to the olive harvest in the first 14 days of October. Over two weeks, troops and settlers killed five Palestinians and injured more than 240. On Wednesday Israeli forces briefly detained 80 rabbis, volunteers and activists accompanying Palestinian farmers, among them New York congressional candidate Brad Lander. On Thursday they detained 11 foreign activists. - Gaza: Israeli forces killed four Palestinians on Thursday. The Gaza Health Ministry counts more than 74,450 dead since October 2023. - Washington: Senate Minority Leader Schumer and House Minority Leader Jeffries issued no statement on the lockdown (WSWS).

2. US envoy says Washington could “absolutely” strike Iran this weekend as Brent tops $117

On Friday, foreign ministers from the UK, France, Germany and Italy met in Istanbul with the “R4” group of Türkiye, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Egypt to discuss the Strait of Hormuz. Al Jazeera described it as a “first-of-its-kind” meeting. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said none of the participants approved of another military attack on Iran. Hours later, Tom Barrack, the US ambassador to Türkiye, said Washington could “absolutely” strike Iran this weekend. He added that any action would not aim at regime change. UN Ambassador Mike Waltz told the Financial Times that the war could last another year. Trump said “We are not negotiating with” Iran and that “Something one way or another will happen, fairly soon.”

On Saturday Iran’s armed forces chief of staff, Maj. Gen. Ali Abdollahi, said US military vessels would not be allowed to enter the Gulf “under any circumstances.” Brent crude rose to $117.30 a barrel, extending four-year highs. A senior Iranian official told Drop Site News that Tehran’s intelligence points to a high likelihood of a large-scale US-Israeli bombing campaign before Tuesday’s Israeli election. That claim rests on a single anonymous source. Netanyahu said this week, “We are committed to completing the elimination of the Iranian regime,” and has made regime change a theme of his campaign.

Key facts: - Barrot (France): further escalation “would create nothing but losers” and would hit hydrocarbon prices hard. - Tajani (Italy): the problem “cannot be solved from a purely military standpoint.” - R4 joint statement: Israeli occupation “constitutes the fundamental source of instability in the region and beyond.” The statement also condemned the Houthis’ escalation and called for the full reopening of Hormuz. - Yemen: the Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted a Houthi ballistic missile aimed at Riyadh. The Yemen war, dormant for years, reignited in July. - The war’s timeline: the US and Israel began attacking Iran on February 28. A US-Iran memorandum of understanding was signed on June 17, before strikes resumed. - Egypt: President Sisi said Egypt would “carry out any measure to protect” Saudi Arabia. Egypt has not decided whether to join the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement between Türkiye, Saudi Arabia and Pakistan.

3. France deploys 70,000 officers against banned Saturday protests as a gunman kills one in Trappes

Macron has banned what he called “illegitimate” demonstrations, meaning any protest not organized by a trade union, and many of Saturday’s planned nationwide protests have been banned. Against them the government is deploying 70,000 officers, helicopters, armored vehicles and water cannon. Jean-Didier Berger, a minister delegate at the Interior Ministry, said police on the streets will increase by 20 percent. Paris is under a partial curfew, and protests are banned in Marseille and Lyon. Shops in Paris, Lyon and Orléans were boarded up on Friday. The main student union said, “Macron is trampling upon the freedom to protest in our country.” Students still rallied in Paris on Thursday after last-minute approval. On Friday Macron held his first talks with high school student representatives since the movement began in mid-September.

Also on Friday, a 39-year-old gunman killed one person and wounded five in Trappes and La Verrière, west of Paris. He fired at a children’s playground, a fuel station and a medical center. The Élysée called it a “racist, terrorist attack,” and antiterrorism prosecutors opened a murder investigation “in connection with a terrorist enterprise.”

Key facts: - Trappes victims: the person killed was of African origin. Two of the wounded, cleaners at a school, were also of African origin, and one critically wounded victim was of North African origin. A 12-year-old girl was shot eight times and is seriously wounded but stable. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said the weapon appeared to be “a military-grade weapon, or at least an automatic weapon.” The suspect had booby-trapped his flat with gas canisters wired to a timer. - Trappes Mayor Ali Rabeh: the attack had “racist, likely Islamophobic, overtones.” - Student demands: a €1.5 billion emergency plan for school buildings, an overhaul of the Parcoursup university admissions system, €10 billion more for education and 250,000 more university places. Some classrooms hold more than 40 students. - Government offer: 3,000 more substitute teachers, an online consultation and an extra €1.1 billion for education in the 2027 budget. The higher education minister calls abolishing Parcoursup “pure demagogy.” - Fiscal constraint: public debt is projected to reach 121.7 percent of GDP in 2027. Macron ended the 2018 yellow vest movement with €10 billion in tax cuts. Al Jazeera notes that a similar response may be harder this time. - Union calendar: a pupils’ day of action on November 3, then a one-day national strike on November 5 called by the CGT, CFDT, FO, UNSA and FSU.

4. Brazil votes Sunday in a runoff Flávio Bolsonaro still narrowly leads

On Sunday, October 25, Brazil holds its presidential runoff between incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, 80, of the Workers Party (PT), and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, of the Liberal Party (PL). Flávio’s father, Jair Bolsonaro, is serving a 27-year sentence for his role in a coup plot. In the first round on October 4, Bolsonaro took about 47 percent to Lula’s 45.2 percent. Polls show Lula narrowing the gap but still behind within the margin of error in most surveys.

Flávio is promising a militarized approach to crime, a debt ceiling and a constitutional reform to “redemocratise” Brazil, and he has pledged to fight for his father’s release. According to Tomas Castanheira on the WSWS, his economic plan is a Milei-style “tesouraço”: a fiscal adjustment of 1.5 percent of GDP, the abolition of 10 ministries and a constitutional austerity amendment. Lula, for his part, has backed lowering the age of criminal responsibility to 16 for heinous crimes. His campaign broadcasts the slogan “If you’re on the left, vote 13; if you’re on the right, vote 13.”

Key facts: - Polls: AtlasIntel/Bloomberg, Monday: Bolsonaro 49.8 percent, Lula 47.6 percent (it was 51.1 to 45.7 on October 9). Nexus: 46.5 to 45.9. Quaest, October 21: 45 to 44. - Lula’s pitches: credit for a constitutional amendment cutting the six-day work week, and free weekend public transport. - Meta: internal conversations reported by Folha de S.Paulo reportedly acknowledged that Lula’s account was restricted more than Flávio’s. The PT has asked the electoral court to annul Flávio’s candidacy for “algorithmic manipulation.” Meta calls it a “technical glitch.” - Vorcaro scandal: a corruption case around banker Daniel Vorcaro has implicated Bolsonaro and several Supreme Court justices. - Milei: on October 20, Argentina’s president called Lula a “thief” and suggested the election could be stolen. - Public finances: the nominal deficit reached 9.99 percent of GDP in the 12 months to June, the highest since the pandemic. Interest payments came to 1.16 trillion reais, or 8.8 percent of GDP (WSWS).

5. Serbia votes Sunday after 150,000 march against Vučić

About 150,000 people marched in Belgrade against Aleksandar Vučić ahead of Sunday’s parliamentary elections. The vote will decide whether his ruling party keeps control after nearly two years of student-led protests over corruption. Vučić has resigned as president so that he can remain in power as prime minister. The student movement is running 250 candidates on its own list. Berta López Domènech, an analyst at the European Policy Centre, told Euronews she expects the result to be “very tight.” Protester Bogdan Pavković attacked a system “which cheats on us, which spies on us, which beats us.”

Key facts: - Context: Serbia is one of nine European countries with mass, mostly youth-initiated protests this autumn. The others are France, Albania, Spain, Italy, Greece, Belgium, Portugal and Ireland. - WSWS assessment: Peter Schwarz criticized the Student List for offering “no answers” to social questions, despite the depth of popular hostility to the regime.

6. Russia hits central Kyiv with cluster warheads as Witkoff brokers November 10 talks

On October 22, Russia fired three ballistic missiles with cluster warheads into central Kyiv. Serhiy Beskrestnov, a senior adviser at Ukraine’s Defense Ministry, said each warhead releases 45 submunitions over an area of 400 by 400 meters. One landed between the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra cathedral and a monastery. Zelenskyy said there “was not even theoretically any military object nearby.” Russia said it targeted an assembly site for FP-5 drones and FP-9 missiles. Since Trump declared an “energy ceasefire” on October 11, Zelenskyy has counted more than 170 Russian strikes on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure. Ukraine, for its part, has resumed strikes on Russian energy targets, hitting the St Petersburg oil terminal on October 18.

US envoy Steve Witkoff met Zelenskyy in Oslo on October 20 and Putin in St Petersburg on October 21. Putin aide Yuri Ushakov said Russia agreed “in principle” to a meeting on November 10, which the Kremlin says will be held in Abu Dhabi. The EU is seeking a bigger role in the talks to “influence US thinking.” The White House says Melania Trump has secured a localized ceasefire in Russian-occupied Oleshky, with timing still to be set.

Key facts: - Front line: the Institute for the Study of War reports a net Ukrainian gain of 241 sq km since September 1. Ukraine’s Operation Vivaldi is credited with retaking about 5,500 sq km of Donetsk region. - Casualties (Ukrainian estimates): Russian losses were 46,230 in September, the year’s highest month. Robert Brovdi, commander of Ukraine’s Unmanned Systems Forces, put losses on October 21 at 2,250, the war’s highest single day. - US leverage: the US cut early warning of ballistic missile launches on October 8, the second time this year; Zelenskyy says it was restored October 20. Patriot interceptors that Europe has paid for have not been released. Defense officials say Starlink and Starshield restrictions have not been lifted, despite Trump’s claim on October 22 that they were about to be. - Diesel deal: Trump waived sanctions on Russian oil exports by executive order on October 16 to bring down US diesel prices. Putin says the first 40,000-tonne shipment to the US leaves within two or three days.

Gunmen kill seven at a church funeral in Jordan

On Friday, masked gunmen attacked a funeral procession at a Greek Orthodox church in Hamoud, a village in Karak governorate about 120 km south of Amman. They killed at least seven people and wounded 34. Survivors heard an explosion as the procession began, and then the attackers entered the church and fired for several minutes. State broadcaster Al-Mamlaka reported two suspects who got out of a vehicle and opened fire. No group has claimed responsibility.

  • Interior Minister Mazen al-Faraya called the attack “terrorist” and “unprecedented.” King Abdullah II pledged “the harshest penalties” and “an iron fist.”
  • About 300,000 of Jordan’s roughly 11.5 million people are Christian. The last comparable attack was ISIS’s 2016 assault on Karak castle, which killed 10.
  • UN spokesman Stéphane Dujarric called an attack during a funeral “especially heinous.”

Houthi siege pushes Taiz hospitals toward collapse

The Houthis’ siege of government-held Taiz began in the first week of October, after Houthi advances in the surrounding countryside. Hospitals in the city are now running out of fuel and medicines. The Republican Hospital’s dialysis center serves 270 regular patients. Its director, Dr. Essam Mahyoub Dahan, has warned that it could shut completely within days, and sessions have already been cut from four hours to two and a half. The Ministry of Health’s October delivery of dialysis solution never arrived.

  • Across the governorate, 1,110 kidney-failure patients depend on dialysis services.
  • Hospitals run on diesel generators because much of the city’s power grid was destroyed. Diesel now comes from the black market at about five times its pre-siege price. Laboratory reagents are down to 10–15 percent of previous levels, and about half of normal medicines are out of stock.
  • On October 22, WHO warned that 42 health facilities in Taiz city face severe shortages, and that hospitals and ambulances need at least 156,000 liters of fuel a month. Amnesty International has urged the Houthis to reopen the main Taiz–Aden road.

Albania’s protest movement widens as parliament descends into chaos

Protests in Albania are escalating. They began against a luxury resort project linked to Jared Kushner and the Trump family, and on Thursday MPs clashed in parliament over them. The movement, known as the Flamingo Movement after its original target, has broadened into opposition to living costs, school conditions, corruption and Prime Minister Edi Rama. The Albanian Students’ Union has called for indefinite boycotts, and there have been violent clashes in Tirana and other cities. Bourgeois press coverage today offers headline-level detail only, with no crowd figures.

Indonesian students protest unemployment, fuel prices and forest fires

Student-led protests continued across Indonesia over unemployment, rising fuel costs and the forest fires blanketing parts of Kalimantan in haze. On Thursday, hundreds of students rallied in Banjarmasin, a city covered in smoke. “Our anger has reached its peak,” a student named Rizky told reporters. These protests belong to a wider Asian wave over fuel and living costs. The WSWS Perspective lists fuel-price blockades and transport strikes in the Philippines alongside similar struggles in Central and South America.

Argentine judge drops the Libra crypto fraud case against Milei

Federal judge Marcelo Martínez de Giorgi dismissed the criminal investigation into President Javier Milei and his sister Karina over the collapse of the $Libra token, which Milei promoted on X in February 2025. “The reported events do not constitute a criminal offence,” the judge wrote. He held that investor losses were not enough to establish fraud. He also dropped the investigation into US businessman Hayden Davis, the token’s self-described “launch adviser,” who admitted holding nearly $100 million after the crash. The presidential office called the investigation “a failed attempt at an institutional coup.” The ruling comes two days before Brazil’s runoff, in which Milei has openly intervened on Bolsonaro’s side.

Cuba accuses Washington of coercing votes on the UN embargo resolution

Cuban Foreign Minister Bruno Rodríguez Parrilla says the Trump administration is threatening countries ahead of the annual General Assembly vote demanding an end to the US embargo. The threats he listed include punitive tariffs, suspended cooperation programs, visa denials for officials and their families, and “the threat of encouraging overthrow or subversion.” A large majority of the General Assembly has voted against the embargo every year for decades.

Oil above $100 and a widening tech sell-off sink the Dow 1.2 percent

Wall Street ended Friday, October 23, lower as an AI-led tech sell-off spread across sectors and US crude stayed above $100 on fears that the US-Iran war will flare up again before or right after the midterms. The Dow fell 652.82 points (−1.23 percent) to 52,215.16. The S&P 500 lost 0.52 percent to 7,623.68, and the Nasdaq Composite slipped 0.21 percent to 25,955.34. For the week the S&P was down 0.5 percent, the Nasdaq 0.7 percent, while the Dow gained 0.4 percent.

The war is now the main factor in workers’ cost of living. AAA puts the national average gasoline price at $4.27 a gallon, up from under $3 before February 28. Diesel is at record highs, and Senator Chris Murphy says it is above $6 a gallon in Detroit. In a September IGA survey, 81 percent of respondents agreed the war has raised the cost of living.

Europe enters winter with record-low gas storage

IEA chief Fatih Birol told Al Jazeera that “Life will not be easy for Europe this year and also next year.” EU gas storage is 72 percent full against a usual level of about 90 percent. Analysts say the shortfall equals the energy needed to power 10 to 12 million homes. Gas prices are up 140 percent since February. Before the war, nearly a tenth of Europe’s LNG came through Hormuz, and about a third of Russian diesel deliveries have stopped because of Ukrainian strikes on refineries. EU energy ministers are reportedly considering asking households and businesses to ration energy. Birol said energy “is not currently considered a business but a political leverage.” He also said populist parties in France and Germany are exploiting public anger over energy.

Trump claims the right to a third term at a Sioux City rally

At a campaign rally in Sioux City, Iowa, on October 22, Trump said he has the right to seek a third presidential term. The 22nd Amendment bars it. Coverage so far is limited to the remark itself. It came in the last stretch of a midterm campaign in which the administration is suing states to use unverified DHS citizenship lists and bringing prosecutions against political opponents (see below).

Pentagon authorizes testing a “heat ray” on migrants at the border

CNN reports that Defense Secretary Hegseth signed a memo last month allowing the Department of Homeland Security to test the Active Denial System on migrants at the US-Mexico border. The weapon fires an invisible millimeter-wave beam that causes a burning sensation in the skin, and it has a range of more than half a mile. The military deployed it to Afghanistan nearly 20 years ago but never used it in combat.

ICE agents filmed trying to pull toddlers from their father’s arms in Providence

A viral video from Providence, Rhode Island, shows two ICE agents repeatedly trying to pull two toddlers from the arms of a Guatemalan father, who identified himself only as Sebastian. He was taking the children to a babysitter before going to his construction job. WHO starts the first vaccine trial in Congo’s worst-ever Ebola outbreak

WHO Director-General Tedros announced on Friday that the first participants in an Ebola vaccine trial were vaccinated in Bunia, the capital of Ituri province, where the outbreak is centered. The Democratic Republic of Congo declared this outbreak, its 17th and worst ever, in May. It has since recorded more than 9,700 cases and over 4,600 deaths, and it has spread to four African countries in eastern, central and western Africa, the widest spread of any Ebola outbreak to date. It is caused by the Bundibugyo species, for which no vaccine is licensed. The trial will first test Merck’s Ervebo, which is approved for the Zaire strain, for cross-protection. Two Bundibugyo-specific candidates, Oxford’s ChAdOx1 vector vaccine and a Moderna mRNA vaccine, both funded by CEPI, are expected to join “shortly.”

  • Design: the “Tumaini” trial uses ring vaccination of high-risk contacts and includes pregnant women and children aged 12 and older, groups usually excluded from trials.
  • Spread: Nigeria has reported its first Ebola case in 12 years, and the Congolese outbreak has reached an eighth province. Kenya reports a second death but no community spread.

US measles cases reach 4,588 as Pennsylvania records a sixth death

The CDC’s October 23 update lists 4,588 confirmed measles cases in 47 jurisdictions in 2026, plus 18 among international visitors, against 2,289 for all of 2025.   • “Key challenges facing the movement in France” (Socialist Worker, October 23). The article quotes Tony Cliff calling the union bureaucracy “a distinct, basically conservative, social formation” and a “major obstacle,” and it calls the November 5 strike “a bureaucratic attempt… to take control back of the movement.” But it then calls for working “with and against” the bureaucracy and credits Mélenchon with “consistent support” for the movement. Its only criticism of him is that he refuses to lead. Its anti-fascist strategy is local campaigning “in every marketplace… before the election,” in other words an electoral front against the RN.
  • “The centre—not rioters—opens the door to French fascism” (Socialist Worker, October 22). The article correctly rejects the idea that the left should back the “centre” against Le Pen, and it blames Macron’s austerity for the RN’s growth. But it defends LFI only as “the only major party” to back the students and does not examine LFI’s record of channeling movements back into parliament, including in the 2024 New Popular Front.
  • “‘Muslim women are showing there’s opposition to Reform UK’” (Socialist Worker). This piece frames protests against a proposed burqa ban in the Senedd as the front line against Reform, within identity-based “anti-racism.”

Assessment: left phrases about the bureaucracy and “revolt,” while the movement is kept in orbit around Mélenchon and the union calendar. Its stated goal, “a revolt powerful enough to defeat both,” comes with no program for workers’ power.

Socialist Appeal / RCP

  • “After 10 October – the next steps for the RCP” (communist.red, Khaled Malachi). Malachi reports that police attacked the RCP’s 700-strong bloc at the national Palestine demonstration on October 10 and arrested 11 members. He claims 56 recruits in one week and almost 200 since September, and announces that “phase two” is the Revolution Festival on November 13–15. Neither Labour, the trade unions nor the Burnham government’s war policy is mentioned.
  • “The 1956 Hungarian Revolution: A struggle for genuine socialism” (communist.red, Ben Gliniecki). This is an extract from Gliniecki’s new introduction to Peter Fryer’s Hungarian Tragedy, presenting 1956 as an anti-Stalinist struggle for workers’ democracy. It does not acknowledge that Fryer broke with the Communist Party over Hungary and went on to work with Gerry Healy’s Socialist Labour League, the British section of the ICFI. The IMT’s own tradition descends from the Grant tendency, which opposed the ICFI’s struggle against Pabloism.

Assessment: revolutionary-sounding enthusiasm that substitutes membership growth and events for a program. The RCP’s silence on Labour and the union bureaucracy in the middle of a European strike wave is the clearest sign of its political orientation.
