### 1. Israel's caretaker cabinet orders war preparations against Iran as Tehran threatens bases from West Asia to Europe

On Wednesday evening Israeli media reported that the security cabinet, chaired by Benjamin Netanyahu, ordered the military to step up preparations for a possible new attack on Iran. Public broadcaster KAN said the directives "could lead it to carry out activities that could trigger a war." Netanyahu holds office only as caretaker. His coalition lost its majority in October, and President Herzog has asked Gadi Eisenkot to form a government. The order became public after Eisenkot urged Netanyahu not to use the military "without a direct and immediate threat" until a new government is in place. Fox News and Ynet report a heated phone call between Trump and Netanyahu over the war, which Ynet called a "shouting match from hell."

Tehran answered with threats of a wider war. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, wrote that if Washington starts another war, "every military base involved in the aggression" will be a legitimate target "from West Asia to Europe," adding: "No safe distance. No safe haven. No immunity." Al Jazeera reports that Tehran is debating whether to replace parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf with Rezaei as chief negotiator, and that some officials favour pre-emptive action if the stalemate continues. In an exclusive interview with Al Jazeera, Ghalibaf said Iran and Oman have agreed to jointly oversee the Strait of Hormuz. He said the 1982 UN Law of the Sea convention does not apply there, that "there are no free waters" in the strait, and that "the ball is now in the American court." He also said US officials had asked Vladimir Putin, during a visit to Moscow, to broker direct talks with Tehran. Iran has rejected a US proposal to move its stockpile of 60% enriched uranium to a third country.

Washington is keeping its options open. Trump says he will resume the war if the Qatari mediation track does not produce a deal. Secretary of State Rubio says the economic pressure is working well enough that large-scale strikes are unnecessary, and Central Command says it is enforcing an "ironclad" naval blockade. Al Jazeera's Alan Fisher reports that the White House wants to be able to declare victory.

Key facts:
- **Attacks on shipping:** The International Maritime Organization recorded 131 confirmed incidents near Hormuz between Feb. 28 and Nov. 9, including 35 in October, the highest monthly total of the war. Reuters columnist Ron Bousso says attacks on ships are now "an almost daily occurrence." Reuters sources say Iran has attacked shipping navigation systems on Didamar island, which belongs to Oman.
- **Oil flows:** About 10.5 million barrels a day now pass through the strait, against 13.5 million before the war (Kpler). Freight and insurance costs are about 10 times their pre-war level. The US EIA expects Brent to stay near $100 through the end of the year.
- **Oil prices:** On Wednesday Brent rose $1.27 to $92.76 and WTI rose 49 cents to $86.53, after Iran denied it was talking to Washington and rejected the uranium proposal.
- **Missile output:** Bloomberg reports that Iran is producing about 50 missiles a month despite damage to its industry, and the IRGC has unveiled an underground "missile city."

### 3. Spain's general strike ends in Barcelona clashes, with rent taking 98.7% of young people's income

The first general strike in Spain in 14 years ended Wednesday night with protests in cities across the country. DW reports that hundreds of thousands joined them after the 24-hour stoppage, and the BBC and Al Jazeera report clashes in Barcelona after dark. The two main federations, CCOO and UGT, reported 90% stoppages in the car industry and the ports. The employers' federation CEOE said participation was "low" in most regions and sectors. Rail, metro and road traffic were disrupted throughout the day.

The strike has turned into an argument about the November 29 snap election. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez told *La Vanguardia* that "this is not a strike against anyone," presenting it as support for his housing package, which parliament rejected. People's Party leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo called the strike a "serious mistake" and warned that the unions would be linked to Sánchez "forever." Vox also attacked the strike. Al Jazeera reports that Spain's protests began with a five-week occupation of Madrid's Puerta del Sol, with hundreds of tents and copies in other cities.

Key facts:
- **Rent:** The average monthly rent for a 50-square-metre flat in Madrid was €1,170 in October (Idealista). The average gross monthly wage is about €2,460.
- **Youth:** The Youth Council of Spain says that rent takes 98.7% of the average young person's income. Average rents across the country have nearly doubled in ten years.
- **Testimony:** One protester told Al Jazeera, "I pay $500 for 5 square metres."

### 4. USDA moves to cut 6.3 million people off food stamps a week after the midterms

On Tuesday the Agriculture Department proposed a rule that would cut 6.3 million people in 3.2 million households from SNAP, nearly one in five recipients. It would end "broad-based categorical eligibility," which 46 states use to enroll households that receive TANF-funded services such as childcare help or job training. Thirty-seven states have raised the gross income limit above 130% of the poverty line, and 41 have waived the federal asset test. Under the rule, only households receiving TANF cash assistance would remain automatically eligible. Trump first proposed the measure in 2019, Biden withdrew it in 2021, and Project 2025 called for bringing it back. A 30-day comment period is open.

As Kate Randall writes on the WSWS, the rule hits two groups: working families earning just over 130% of the poverty line (about $35,500 a year for a family of three) and seniors, disabled people and laid-off workers with modest savings. A USDA spokesman said categorical eligibility had "bloated the welfare rolls for decades." Rep. Angie Craig, the ranking Democrat on the Agriculture Committee, called the rule "heartless and cruel." Incoming Speaker Hakeem Jeffries said Democrats are "ready, willing and able to find bipartisan common ground."

Key facts:
- **Federal savings:** USDA projects $44.65 billion in SNAP cuts over fiscal years 2027–2031. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities estimates about $15 billion a year once fully in effect, more than 15% of the program.
- **Children:** About 1.78 million children would lose automatic free school meals. By USDA's own count, 42,000 would lose summer food benefits and 5,500 women, infants and children would lose WIC.
- **Cost to states:** States would have to verify applicants' assets at an extra cost of $928.5 million while federal administrative funding is being cut.
- **Enrollment already falling:** Under the 2025 "One Big Beautiful Bill Act," SNAP enrollment fell by 5.5 million (13%) between July 2025 and July 2026 to 36.1 million, the lowest in 17 years (CBPP).

### 5. Abu Dhabi talks end with a prisoner swap as Kushner and Witkoff head to Putin

Two days of US-mediated talks between Russia and Ukraine ended in Abu Dhabi on Wednesday with agreement on a large prisoner exchange and no breakthrough on ending the war, which is approaching its fifth anniversary. Russia's chief negotiator Vladimir Medinsky reported "mutual understanding" on some other issues. TASS says the swap will take place within two to three weeks, and Jared Kushner said the number of prisoners would be settled on Thursday. Kushner called it "a very tough meeting but a productive one." He said territory would be the hardest question but that progress had been made on a post-war security framework. He and Steve Witkoff are flying to Moscow to brief Putin and then to Dublin to meet Zelenskyy.

The European powers are pushing to join the next round. Ukrainian negotiator David Arakhamia wants multilateral talks that include Europe, and Zelenskyy said European security cannot be decided "without Europe." Medinsky said Moscow does not object to EU participation but asked what there would be to discuss, given that Europe has made no proposals. Politico Europe reports that Washington now also wants Europe present, calling it "the only way to get this done." Inside the EU there is a fight over who pays. Four member states are resisting Chancellor Merz's demand to write Ukraine aid into the EU budget, and Slovakia's prime minister accused Merz of "blackmail."

Key facts:
- **Strikes continue:** Russia said it intercepted 146 drones over the Leningrad region, and the Baltic states issued drone alerts (Al Jazeera).
- **Front line:** A Wall Street Journal analysis, cited by the Institute for the Study of War, finds that Russian land gains are shrinking. Its headline is "Ten months of war to gain 46 square miles."
- **Past swaps:** Each side freed 200 prisoners in March. A larger swap than the 1,000-for-1,000 exchange of May 2025 would set a record.
- **Arakhamia** claims there is "good momentum" toward a settlement before the end of the year.

### 6. Israel strikes Nasser hospital's intensive care, killing patients awaiting surgery

Early Thursday an Israeli air strike hit the Nasser Medical Complex in Khan Younis, killing at least six people and wounding 20, according to Gaza's health ministry and civil defence. Al Jazeera's Hind Khoudary reported that most of the dead and wounded were patients, some due for surgery the next day. She said it was at least the seventh Israeli strike on Nasser in two years. It is the only hospital still functioning in Khan Younis, serving at least 500,000 people. The Israeli military said it was targeting a Hamas member. Hamas called the strike a "war crime" and asked the US and the mediators to make Israel observe the ceasefire. Earlier the same day, Israeli strikes killed seven other Palestinians across the territory. Among them were two brothers in their tent and a man filling a water tank. Israeli forces also killed Mahmoud Kamal Abdel-Malek, a 20-year-old goalkeeper for Gaza Sports Club.

The killing has gone on through the "ceasefire" in effect since October 2025. Gaza's health ministry counts at least 1,600 dead and 5,878 injured since then, and 74,653 dead since October 7, 2023. UN humanitarian chief Tom Fletcher said some donor governments are holding back winter aid until a new Israeli government is formed, which he called "not an acceptable excuse."

Key facts:
- **Floods:** Gaza's media office says weekend rains flooded at least 70,000 tents, and UNICEF says at least 106,000 people were directly affected as latrines overflowed into the camps.
- **Tents:** The UN deputy spokesman said Israeli permits are needed before tents can be brought in.
- **Diplomacy:** At COP31, Turkish President Erdoğan called the war in Gaza an "ecocide." Israel called this a "campaign of lies" (AP).

### Ebola passes 11,000 cases with no vaccine for the strain

Government data show 11,042 confirmed Ebola cases and at least 5,387 deaths in the Democratic Republic of Congo, the largest outbreak in the country's history and the second-largest ever after West Africa in 2014–16. It is caused by the rare Bundibugyo strain, which kills about half of those infected and has no specific vaccine or treatment. WHO approved trials of two candidate vaccines this month, with results expected in early 2027. Cases have been reported in East, Central and West Africa, the widest spread of any Ebola outbreak. Many deaths occur outside treatment centres. MSF says the response is struggling despite nearly $1 billion in funding (Bloomberg). The IOM has found 71 cases along travel routes and warns that a funding gap could leave the outbreak "out of control." Kenya discharged its last patient and has started the countdown to being declared Ebola-free (BBC).
