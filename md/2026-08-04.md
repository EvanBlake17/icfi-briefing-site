### 1. Israel kills dozens in Gaza as Trump threatens Iran with "decapitation"

Israeli forces killed at least 19 Palestinians on Sunday and eight more on Saturday in strikes across Gaza City, Khan Younis and Deir el-Balah, including a pregnant woman and children. Israeli warplanes struck medicine warehouses attached to the Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital in Deir el-Balah, destroying two facilities. Key facts:

- At least 1,222 Palestinians killed and 4,053 injured since the October 2025 ceasefire took effect, per local officials.
- More than 58,000 children in Gaza have lost one or both parents over the course of the war, according to humanitarian estimates.
- Two medicine warehouses attached to Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital destroyed in Deir el-Balah — a continuation of the systematic targeting of medical infrastructure.
- A poll cited by the WSWS found 64% of Americans oppose continuation of the conflict.

### 2. Trump calls off Iran strike, gives Tehran "last chance" as talks resume

Trump announced that negotiations with Iran resumed Monday, August 3, after Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates and Qatar asked Washington to call off a planned military strike — described by administration officials as the largest planned US attack since the Second World War — because "they think there's a deal." Trump described the talks as Iran's "last chance" to abandon its nuclear program, while stating that US forces remain "locked and loaded."

Tehran gave a markedly different account. Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman said the government is focused solely on an Omani-mediated arrangement covering shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and accused the United States of having "violated all of its commitments" when the June ceasefire collapsed.

Key facts:

- Trump: "There's a deal on [the Strait of] Hormuz, and then there will be a deal on the nuclear... denuclearization of Iran."
- The announcement drove the single largest market move of the day: WTI crude fell roughly 5.6–6% to about $79.60–79.94, Brent to roughly $83.85.
- Crude nonetheless remains about 20% above pre-conflict levels, reflecting continued risk premium on Hormuz transit.
- Trump's FY2027 request includes $1.5 trillion in military spending, framed substantially around war with Iran.

### 3. Spokane wildfire complex forces 65,000 to flee in worst fire in Washington history

The Spokane Complex — comprising the Old Trails, Autumn Lane and Fairview fires, ignited August 1 and driven by 35–45 mph winds — has forced the evacuation of roughly 60,000–65,000 people, approximately one in four residents of Spokane, Washington. As of August 3 the complex had burned about 8,000 acres (roughly 12 square miles) at 0% containment, destroying 600–700 homes and businesses. The Mann-Grandstaff VA Medical Center was evacuated and closed; more than 400 people are sheltering at the Spokane Convention Center.

Mayor Lisa Brown called it "the worst natural disaster our region has faced" and said officials are "still assessing the potential for lives to be lost." Governor Bob Ferguson declared a state of emergency and a statewide burn ban through September 30. Incident Commander Tom Clemo told the *Spokesman-Review*: "Probably the largest, most destructive fire in Washington's history occurred yesterday afternoon."

Key facts:

- Air quality in Spokane reached 218 ("unhealthy") and is forecast to exceed 300 ("hazardous"); smoke has spread to Nevada, Minneapolis and into Canada.
- Washington is in its fourth consecutive year of drought; the National Weather Service issued its first-ever "Particularly Dangerous Situation" Red Flag warning for Eastern Washington.
- More than 1,000 fires have burned roughly 450,000 acres statewide in 2026, the worst season since 2021, with 15–17 major fires active on 250,000–262,000 acres.
- The Kaiser Canyon Fire on the Colville Reservation has burned over 129,000 acres, destroying homes, some 120 archaeological sites and a Shaker Church.
- The Sinlahekin Fire tripled in size and cut power to roughly half of Okanogan County.

Working-class neighborhoods including Indian Trail and Balbo bore the brunt. Today's WSWS Perspective by Bryan Dyne contrasts the scale of the disaster with the concentration of wealth in the same state — Amazon, Microsoft, Starbucks and Boeing, and three individual fortunes above $100 billion — and advances demands for full compensation of every family that lost a home regardless of insurance status.

### 5. US and Japan mount first joint yen intervention since 1998

The United States and Japan confirmed a coordinated currency intervention after the yen fell to 40-year lows against the dollar, the first joint US-Japan operation to buy yen since 1998. Japan conducted its largest single-day intervention on record, spending roughly $52.8 billion; the US Treasury, through the New York Fed, sold euros to buy yen, deploying approximately $26.3 billion. USD/JPY, which had traded above 163, fell toward 157 and closed Monday around 156.34, a roughly 1% single-day drop.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the operation countered "disorderly" trading and left room for further action. Trump described it as a friendly assist to an ally. Analysts quoted by *Fortune* called the US participation "weird" and "unwise," warning that "the last thing you want is to give markets any kind of reason to ask questions."

Key facts:

- US federal debt has risen from $27.7 trillion in 2020 to $39.5 trillion.
- Japan's import-dependent economy has been hit by imported inflation from the weak yen; the BOJ has held rates at ultra-low levels.
- On the WSWS, Nick Beams argues the intervention's real object is protecting US Treasury bond prices from a yen carry-trade unwind, not aiding Japanese consumers.

### Ceuta death toll reaches 88 as EU prepares emergency migration talks

Spanish officials said Monday that at least 88 people died during the mass crossing into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta from Morocco in late July, after an estimated 60,000 people — predominantly young men — breached the border. Many drowned swimming around the breakwater; others were crushed attempting to climb the border fence. Morocco's interior ministry issued a drastically lower count of 11 deaths, 10 of them drownings. Spanish authorities have conducted summary forced returns without legal hearings. EU governments are preparing emergency talks framed around "uncontrolled crossings" rather than the causes of the deaths. More than 32,000 people have died attempting Mediterranean crossings since 2014.

### Japan launches its first centralized intelligence agency since World War II

Japan formally launched the National Intelligence Bureau on July 31, its first centralized national intelligence body since 1945, created by upgrading the Cabinet Intelligence and Research Office. The bureau has roughly 730 staff and is headed by veteran police official Kazuya Hara, 58. A National Intelligence Council chaired by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi held its first meeting the same day, with the bureau serving as its secretariat. A CIA-style foreign intelligence arm is planned by 2028.

The bureau has a statutory mandate to integrate material across ministries and set intelligence priorities independently of other agencies' policy agendas. It accompanies Japanese military spending rising to 2% of GDP and a broader dismantling of postwar constitutional constraints, oriented against China.

### Porsche and IG Metall agree to 5,000 more job cuts in Germany

Porsche announced the elimination of a further 5,000 jobs in Stuttgart by 2035, on top of 3,900 cut last year. - Workers under collective agreement receive a one-off €1,500 "transformation bonus" in August; IG Metall members get €1,911 plus an extra leave day and a €200 voucher.
- The cuts form part of a Volkswagen group-wide plan the WSWS puts at roughly 100,000 jobs.

### Selective Service moves to wargame a national draft

### US measles cases pass 2025 total, highest since 1991

The CDC's latest count puts 2026 US measles cases at roughly 2,300–2,400 (reports range from 2,318 to 2,371 depending on update date), already exceeding the full-year 2025 total of 2,289 and the highest annual figure since 1991. Approximately 93% of cases are in people who are unvaccinated or whose vaccination status is unknown; 7% have required hospitalization. There have been 37 distinct outbreaks this year, with 94% of all cases outbreak-associated. The United States is on track to lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000 when the Pan American Health Organization reviews transmission data later this year — the direct consequence of the anti-vaccination policy now installed at the top of the federal public health apparatus.

### DRC Ebola epidemic becomes the fastest-growing on record

The Bundibugyo virus outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo reached 3,605 confirmed cases and 1,587 deaths as of July 30 — a 44% case fatality ratio — making it the largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded in the DRC and the second largest anywhere. The WHO declared it a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on May 17. Transmission is sustained across five provinces (Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu, Haut-Uélé and Tshopo) and 49 health zones. Writing on the WSWS, Benjamin Mateus links the trajectory to the collapse of US global health aid — from $1.2 billion in 2024 to $67 million in the fourth quarter of 2025 — and calls the outcome social murder.

### Australia records first H5N1 mass-mortality event

Officials confirmed on August 2 that H5N1 killed 49 greater crested terns, with 35 more sick, off Cape Jaffa in South Australia — the country's first confirmed mass-mortality event from the virus. Australia now has 53 confirmed wild-bird detections across five states, with no poultry farm spread yet reported. Australia was the last continent without H5N1 in its wildlife; officials say broader spread through seabird colonies is now expected.

### Roundup

- **Oral GLP-1 pill produces 11.2% weight loss in Phase 3 trial.** *NEJM* published the full ATTAIN-1 results for Eli Lilly's orforglipron: in 3,127 patients, the 36 mg dose produced average weight loss of 11.2% at 72 weeks (versus 7.5% and 8.4% at lower doses), with 36% of patients losing at least 15% of body weight and improvement in cardiometabolic markers. It is the first pivotal data for a once-daily oral GLP-1 for obesity — significant for access, given the manufacturing constraints and pricing of injectables.

XFG.1.1 is now dominant at 32% of sequenced cases, followed by XFG (13%) and XFG.14.1 (8%). The FDA has recommended fall 2026 vaccines target the JN.1-lineage XFG variant.

## Arts and culture

**Record box office weekend underscores the concentration of film capital.** The weekend of July 31–August 2 produced roughly $430 million in North American ticket sales — the largest collective weekend in box office history — driven by *Spider-Man: Brand New Day* and Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey*, and achieved without the Chinese market. Nolan's adaptation of Homer took $50 million domestically in its second weekend and is tracking toward a $1.4 billion global total.

The result deserves more than industry-page treatment. That the single largest weekend on record was produced by a superhero franchise instalment and an adaptation of the founding epic of Western literature says something about the present state of cinema: the studio system now sustains itself almost entirely on pre-sold intellectual property and event-scale spectacle, while mid-budget filmmaking about contemporary social life has been effectively liquidated. Nolan's film is a genuine attempt at serious material on a scale the industry rarely permits — the *Odyssey* is, after all, a poem about a soldier's decade-long inability to come home from a war, and about what the war did to everyone who waited. Whether the adaptation engages that content or merely renders it as spectacle is the question worth asking of it. The commercial verdict is unambiguous: audiences will turn out in enormous numbers when offered work of ambition, contradicting a decade of studio insistence that the theatrical audience has evaporated.

**Museum censorship intensifies under conditions of federal defunding.** The American Alliance of Museums, which represents 35,000 institutions, published new guidance on August 3 on how museums can resist censorship — an unusual step for a trade body, and an index of how severe the pressure has become. The AAM's own recent report documents that US museums have been hit hard by Trump-ordered funding cuts. The pattern of the past year: Amy Sherald's cancellation of her *American Sublime* exhibition at the Smithsonian's National Portrait Gallery over the removal of LGBTQ content; the Art Museum of the Americas in Washington cancelling *Nature's Wild With Andil Gosine* weeks before opening without explanation.

The *Art Newspaper* has separately documented that a growing number of US artists are now expected to fundraise for their own museum exhibitions as institutional budgets shrink — the combination of federal and state defunding, legislative attacks on diversity programming, and housing costs in cultural centres producing conditions in which, as the paper puts it, artists' material needs have become indistinguishable from their artistic needs. This is not merely a question of "culture war" targets. The defunding of public art institutions and the proletarianization of artists proceed from the same source as the destruction of public education and health care, and the artistic response so far has been fragmented and largely confined to liberal-institutional appeals.

**Deaths.** Vincent Pastore, the American character actor best known as Salvatore "Big Pussy" Bonpensiero in HBO's *The Sopranos*, died August 1. Pastore came to acting late, after years running a nightclub in New Rochelle, and brought to the role a lived familiarity with the milieu that gave the series its documentary weight — a naturalism largely absent from the prestige television that followed it. Japanese actor and impressionist Ryotaro Shimizu died August 1 at 37. Horse trainer and author Monty Roberts, who popularized non-coercive "natural horsemanship" methods against a century of orthodoxy, also died August 1.

Breen argues that the American Revolution was made by ordinary people organized through local Committees of Public Safety rather than by the elite Founders of conventional historiography. The interview is a significant contribution to the WSWS's long campaign against the New York Times' 1619 Project and the broader racialist falsification of American history: Breen's evidence is that the revolution's driving force was a mass democratic mobilization, not a conspiracy of slaveholders. ## Pseudo-left press review

- [Jacobin / DSA](#jacobin--dsa) — treats DSA's fusion with the Democratic ballot line as a purely organizational question; frames Gaza through First Amendment litigation
- [Left Voice](#left-voice) — most consistent anti-war line of the day on Ukraine, but its Ceuta programme is a list of democratic demands addressed to the bourgeois state
- [Liberation News / PSL](#liberation-news--psl) — calls for an independent revolutionary party while declining to condemn the Mamdani/DSA strategy directly
- [Socialist Alternative](#socialist-alternative) — makes independent electoral candidacies the strategic centre; treats Mamdani as an ally to be pressured
- [Socialist Worker / SWP UK](#socialist-worker--swp-uk) — opposes Burnham's arms spending on Keynesian redistributive grounds rather than anti-imperialist ones
- [Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)](#socialist-appeal--rcp-imt) — sharpest anti-Labour rhetoric of the day, expended largely on sectarian differentiation from the SWP

### Jacobin / DSA

**"Socialists Are Out-Organizing the Democratic Establishment"** credits NYC-DSA's electoral wins to volunteer-based organizing against a consultant-driven establishment. The entire critique is organizational and tactical; the class character of the Democratic Party is never posed. DSA's operation on the Democratic ballot line is treated as an unproblematic given, and "socialism" is redefined as a superior method of Democratic campaign management.

**"Centrist Democrats Keep Making Awful Health Reform Proposals"** attacks a moderate Democratic health plan as policy-incoherent and argues Medicare for All is technically superior. This is reformism arguing with reformism on technocratic terrain — the question of which class can implement any health programme, and against whose resistance, does not arise.

**"Mahmoud Khalil Is Suing the Trump Administration"** is a sympathetic interview that names Gaza a genocide but channels the political content almost entirely into US civil-liberties litigation. The First Amendment frame substitutes a domestic constitutional grievance for opposition to Zionism and US imperialism as state projects.

*Assessment:* Jacobin's output today is the clearest expression of the DSA's function — furnishing a socialist vocabulary for a politics that remains wholly within the Democratic Party and the courts. Notably, Jacobin has published nothing on Mamdani's move against the paraprofessional bonus.

### Left Voice

**"Zelenskyy's Wartime Regime Faces Political Crises and Simmering Protests against Conscription"** takes an explicitly defeatist position toward both camps, rejecting Ukrainian nationalism and Russian expansionism alike, casting Zelensky as a servant of European capital, and calling for Ukrainian-Russian working-class unity to end the war independently. No campism in either direction — the most consistent anti-imperialist line in today's pseudo-left press.

**"Ceuta: Against Militarization, Deportations, and 'Fortress Europe'"** frames the enclave deaths as a product of imperialist plunder and demands open borders, legalization, and the return of Ceuta and Melilla to Morocco. The programme is a list of democratic and humanitarian demands presented as achievable through pressure — the national-restitution demand in particular substitutes a territorial-sovereignty claim for the unification of Moroccan and Spanish workers.

**"Argentina: Beyond the World Cup Backlash"** attacks Milei as a subordinate of the US and the IMF and calls for Pan-American working-class solidarity.

*Assessment:* Left Voice's formal positions on war are the closest of any tendency reviewed to a Trotskyist line, but the programmatic content remains propagandistic — the PTS's actual practice of electoral blocs within Argentina's bourgeois state framework is never subjected to the same criticism it applies elsewhere.

### Liberation News / PSL

**"The Machine Strikes Back: Illinois Democrats against democracy and socialism"** argues that the Democratic machine tolerates only socialists who operate inside the party — citing Mamdani — while crushing independents, and calls for building an independent revolutionary party. It stops short of condemning the DSA's inside-the-Democrats strategy as such, treating it as a tolerated position rather than a betrayal.

**"Six months later, the lessons of January in Minnesota cannot be forgotten"** warns against channelling street militancy into Democratic votes as occurred in 2020, calling instead for strikes and mass mobilization — unusually anti-electoralist for the PSL.

**"Study, fast, train, fight: The roots of Black August"** ties the tradition to George Jackson and explicitly rejects Black Democratic officials as any part of the answer to mass incarceration.

*Assessment:* The PSL is running to the left of the DSA rhetorically while maintaining the Stalinist framework — national liberation and racial oppression as autonomous questions rather than as expressions of class relations, and no accounting for its own repeated electoral accommodations.

### Socialist Alternative

**"Support Left Independent Candidates—Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties"** rejects both major parties but makes independent candidacies (Sawant, PSL) the strategic vehicle toward a "new mass workers' party." Electoral activity substitutes for a programme of class struggle, and the "mass workers' party" formulation is deliberately empty of revolutionary content.

**"Zohran Says Netanyahu Not Welcome In NYC: Kick Him Out With Mass Action!"** calls Israel's conduct genocide but treats Mamdani as a pressurable ally rather than a Democratic mayor to be politically opposed — precisely the adaptation the paraprofessional bonus reversal exposes.

**"Justice For Corey Ruiz—Fight Police Brutality With A One-Day Citywide Shutdown"** calls for a political strike and "democratic community control" of police, the most militant framing in their output, though community control is a reformist illusion about an instrument of class rule.

### Socialist Worker / SWP UK

**"Burnham commits to increase defence spending by billions"** attacks the Labour government's arms pledge and its link to BAE Systems and Israel, but argues chiefly that the money should be spent on welfare instead — a Keynesian counter-budget rather than a demand for the dismantling of the arms industry and the defeat of one's own imperialism.

**"Scottish anti-racists: 'we don't trust the state to beat the far right'"** argues correctly against relying on state bans and police to fight fascism, distancing from the SNP, Labour and Liberal Democrats.

**"'Everything explodes and nothing changes': Anton Jäger's Hyperpolitics"** rejects the book's nostalgia for rebuilding mass reformist parties in favour of "movements and working-class self-activity" — the classic IST substitution of movementism for a party programme.

### Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

**"Another government, same system – Britain needs a revolution!"** attacks Burnham's government as continuous with Starmer on austerity, NHS privatization and support for NATO and Israel, naming Labour "a steadfast defender of British imperialism" and rejecting electoralism.

**"Stand Up to Opportunism – Why the SWP gets everything wrong on racism"** attacks the SWP's Stand Up To Racism as a class-collaborationist popular front allying with "middle-class elements and Zionists." The criticism has substance but functions primarily as product differentiation against a rival group rather than as the elaboration of an independent programme.

**"Britain First sent packing in Norwich thanks to communist-led counter-demo"** reports an RCP counter-mobilization against a far-right march.

*Cross-cutting:* No tendency reviewed took an openly pro-war or one-sidedly campist position on Gaza. None referenced the ICFI or WSWS directly. The common thread — from Jacobin's Democratic organizing through Socialist Alternative's independent candidacies to the RCP's abstract revolutionary appeals — is the absence of any orientation to the actual industrial struggles now under way, and silence on the union apparatuses' role in them.

## What the WSWS should cover today

### 1. Eighty-eight dead at Ceuta as 60,000 breach the Morocco-Spain border

The scale of the late-July crossing — an estimated 60,000 people, overwhelmingly young men, against a death toll Spain now puts at 88 and Morocco at 11 — makes this the largest single border event in the history of the Spanish enclaves and one of the deadliest. The gap between the two official counts is itself a story, as is the EU's response: emergency talks framed around "uncontrolled crossings" rather than the deaths. This warrants follow-up to the August 3 article, particularly on the economic collapse in the Sahel and Morocco driving the movement and on the role of Spain's PSOE-led government in conducting summary returns.

### 2. Verizon and Kaiser contracts expire as US labour fights converge in August

Twenty thousand Verizon workers in the Northeast and mid-Atlantic are already past contract expiry with retiree health care unresolved, and 24,600 Kaiser Permanente nurses face expiry this month — alongside the WestJet strike in Canada and the ongoing Salit Steel fight. This is a concentrated moment requiring coordinated coverage and an explicit call for rank-and-file committees linking the disputes across employers and borders, against the CWA, NNU and CUPE apparatuses that will otherwise isolate each one.

### 3. Trump's August 7 tariffs will cost US households $900 a year

The tariff rates taking effect August 7 on Canada, Vietnam and South Africa, and the Section 338 levies on August 19, push the effective US tariff rate to 15.8% from 2.3% at the end of 2024 — a regressive consumption tax of roughly $900 per household annually with no measurable effect on the trade deficit. The WSWS has analysed the trade war strategically; a direct treatment of the household cost and its relation to the wage stagnation now driving contract fights would connect the two.

### 4. US measles cases hit the highest level since 1991 as elimination status nears collapse

With roughly 2,300–2,400 cases already surpassing the 2025 total and 93% of infections among the unvaccinated or those of unknown status, the United States is on course to lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000 when PAHO reviews the data later this year. This is the measurable consequence of the anti-scientific policy now installed at the head of US public health, and it belongs alongside the WSWS's Ebola and COVID coverage as a single indictment of the destruction of public health infrastructure.

### 5. Sudan's drone war and the imperialist powers arming both sides

More than 1,000 civilians killed by drone strikes in Sudan in five months, 24.6 million facing acute hunger, and Sunday's massacre of 35 people at an open-air tribal court — with UAE backing for the RSF and rival external sponsorship of the army — make this the largest humanitarian catastrophe on earth and among the least covered. A substantive article on the drone-supply chains and the competition for Red Sea access and Sudanese gold would fill a real gap.

### 6. Museum censorship and the defunding of US public art institutions

The American Alliance of Museums, representing 35,000 institutions, has been driven to issue formal guidance on resisting censorship, while artists are increasingly expected to fundraise for their own museum exhibitions as federal and state support collapses. The WSWS's arts coverage would be strengthened by a treatment connecting the defunding of cultural institutions to the broader assault on public provision — and by a critical assessment of the liberal-institutional response, which has so far confined itself to appeals to the same state carrying out the cuts.

### 7. DRC's M23 occupation and the minerals behind it

Despite a US-brokered agreement and Qatar-mediated talks, Rwanda-backed M23 forces continue to occupy Goma and Bukavu, with documented summary executions and rapes during the occupation of Uvira. Some 4.3 million people remain displaced. The conflict is inseparable from control over coltan and other minerals feeding global technology supply chains, and it is unfolding in the same provinces as the Ebola epidemic — a connection that has gone almost entirely unexamined.
