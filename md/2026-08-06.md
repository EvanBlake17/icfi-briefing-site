### 1. Hiroshima marks 81st anniversary of the atomic bombing as nuclear powers rearm

More than 50,000 people gathered at Hiroshima's Peace Memorial Park on Thursday morning for the 81st anniversary of the US atomic bombing, observing a moment of silence at 8:15 a.m., the instant of detonation on August 6, 1945. Representatives of some 120 countries and regions attended, including both the United States and Iran — the latter now four months into a shooting war with Washington. The United States was represented by Deputy Chief of Mission Aaron Snipe; Ambassador George Glass did not attend.

Mayor Kazumi Matsui used the Peace Declaration to attack the great powers directly, warning that "downplaying the inhumanity of nuclear weapons and accepting the use of force to pursue one's own prosperity risk cycles of violent retaliation that could ultimately result in another Hiroshima or Nagasaki," and telling the assembled dignitaries: "Policymakers of the world, civil society demands the immediate abolition of nuclear weapons." Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi attended for the first time as head of government, presiding over a rearmament program that has taken Japanese military spending to levels unseen since 1945.

Key facts:

- Roughly **91,000 hibakusha** remain alive — about **8,000 fewer than a year ago** — with an average age above 86, according to Japan's Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare.
- **4,393 names** of victims whose deaths were confirmed in the past year were added to the cenotaph registry, bringing the total to **353,639**.
- Survivor Amano Kokichi, 87, who witnessed the bombing from about a mile away, said communities must take responsibility for preserving testimony as the generation of eyewitnesses disappears.
- Two sixth-grade students delivered the children's commitment to peace: "Peace is not something that is handed to us. It is something that all of us around the world must build together."
- Japan, sheltering under the US nuclear umbrella, remains a non-signatory to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons.

The anniversary falls in the middle of a US-Iran war in which the Pentagon has burned through the bulk of its interceptor inventory, a NATO-Russia proxy war, and open US preparations for conflict with China. The mayor's warning about "another Hiroshima or Nagasaki" is not rhetoric.

### Taiwan launches Han Kuang war games with 20,000 reservists

Taiwan's annual Han Kuang exercises entered their live-fire phase on August 5, running through August 14 after an April phase of computer simulations. Active-duty troops were mobilized alongside approximately **20,000 reservists** in the largest drills ever held on the island.

- Reservists from the Taipei City Reserve Brigade conducted rifle and mortar drills at a Taipei high school as part of a full-brigade mobilization. On the northwest coast, reservists conducted live-fire training with US-made M4A1 rifles on a beach identified as a likely amphibious landing site.
- The Defence Ministry said drills test "combat readiness across all branches of the military, rapid deployment, anti-amphibious landing operations and prolonged warfare." Activities include fighter dispersal, naval deployment and the conversion of civilian factories to military production.
- Civilian mobilization measures include air raid drills and a deliberate temporary slowdown of mobile internet speeds.
- The US maintains approximately **500 military trainers** on Taiwan and has supplied drones, HIMARS and anti-tank missiles. Recent arms packages total **$11.4 billion**, with a proposed **$14 billion** deal awaiting Congressional approval. Taiwan plans to procure **100,000 drones by 2027** under the "Taiwan Dome" initiative.

### EU escalates migration crackdown after Ceuta catastrophe

The European Union is using the mass crossing at the Spanish enclave of Ceuta to accelerate an already sweeping fortification of its borders. Since late July, an estimated 60,000 to 80,000 people — overwhelmingly young Moroccan men — breached the Ceuta border by land and sea. Death tolls vary: Spanish and Moroccan authorities confirmed at least 72 by August 2, local officials cited 86, and the WSWS reports at least 99.

- Many drowned attempting to swim around the border barrier; others were crushed in a stampede at the Tarajal beach breakwater fence. Spain closed shops and restricted access to water and food in the enclave; most of those who crossed were returned to Morocco within a day. Spain has since installed a 500-metre floating containment barrier.
- Commission President von der Leyen declared: "We cannot allow anyone to enter our Union without complying with our rules." **Twenty-two European leaders** signed an open letter criticizing Spain's handling and threatening to suspend the Schengen Agreement; **Italy suspended Schengen** outright.
- A five-point plan expands Frontex operations and border enforcement. The new Common European Asylum System permits the outsourcing of asylum procedures to external borders.
- Irregular crossings had already fallen **55% over two years** and 40% this year — the crackdown is not a response to rising numbers.
- Spain regularized roughly **500,000** irregular migrants who applied for asylum before the end of 2025, and its external border sees half the irregular entries of Italy's — which is precisely why it has been singled out for attack by the European right.

### Record European heat and wildfires, nuclear plants forced to cut output

Italy's Health Ministry placed **all 27 cities** in its monitoring network under *bollino rosso* — the highest heat alert — simultaneously, the first time this has occurred in recorded Italian meteorological history. It is Italy's fourth heatwave of the summer. France's national weather office confirmed July 2026 was the hottest month since record-keeping began in 1900. Poland faces what may be its worst drought in years.

- By July 29 the EU had mapped **457,459 hectares burned** across 1,407 detected fires, releasing approximately **17.98 million tonnes of CO2** — nearly a third more than the same period in 2025.
- The Gironde fire west of Bordeaux burned **42,000 hectares**, France's largest forest fire since 1949, displacing over 220,000 people. The western Attica fire in Greece burned about 13,290 hectares and destroyed over 100 homes; 42% of Attica's forested area has burned in the past decade.
- Nuclear generation is failing under the heat: Hungary's **Paks** plant, which supplies nearly half the country's electricity, nearly shut down completely on August 4 due to record-low Danube water levels. A reactor at Romania's **Cernavoda** went offline July 28, threatening 20% of national electricity production. A July 29 fire came within three miles of Britain's **Sizewell B**.

### DR Congo's largest-ever Ebola outbreak spreads to five provinces

As of August 4, the Democratic Republic of Congo has recorded **3,874 confirmed Ebola cases and 1,751 deaths**, with **717 patients** currently hospitalized in isolation. It is the largest outbreak in the country's history.

- The outbreak is caused by **Bundibugyo ebolavirus**, distinct from the more common Zaire strain. Existing monoclonal antibody therapeutics were certified against the Zaire species, leaving clinicians without validated treatments.
- It has spread from Ituri Province to North Kivu, South Kivu, Haut-Uélé and Tshopo — **49 health zones** in total. Ituri remains worst hit with 3,375 cases and 1,417 deaths.
- WHO reports that trials of the first Bundibugyo-specific vaccine candidate began in the UK on **July 24**, with a second trial starting in Canada. Two candidates are now in phase 1.
- CIDRAP's assessment is blunt: the response is being **outpaced by the spread of the virus**. The delay in developing a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine — the species has been known since 2007 — is a direct consequence of pharmaceutical development being governed by markets rather than need.

### SpaceX booster slams into the Moon as lunar debris tops 209 tons

A discarded 4-tonne SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage struck the Moon near Einstein Crater on the far side at **2:35 a.m. EDT on August 5**, travelling at approximately **8,700 km/h (5,400 mph)**. The impact released energy equivalent to about **3 tons of TNT**; NASA calculates a resulting crater roughly **18 metres wide and 4 metres deep**.

The stage had delivered the Blue Ghost-1 and Hakuto-R Mission 2 lunar rovers in 2024 and was subsequently abandoned in space. The impact was **unplanned**. The European Southern Observatory's Very Large Telescope detected spectral lines of sodium and lithium in the debris plume for five to ten minutes after impact. Human-made debris on the lunar surface now exceeds **209 tons** — an unregulated accumulation driven by the commercialization of spaceflight with no liability regime and no scientific oversight.

UPS eliminated the Midnight shift at its 43rd Street Manhattan hub in April 2026, replacing it with split shifts: a four-hour Twilight shift ending around 10:30 p.m., then **five to six unpaid hours of waiting**, then a four-hour Preload shift beginning around 3:30–4:00 a.m. UPS thereby controls 13 to 14 hours of a worker's day while paying for eight.

Workers described the consequences: "Some people wander the city. Some people stay here. Some people sleep in the cafeteria. A lot of people are getting exhausted." Another said, "I was on the Midnight shift, and now there are easily over 40 people sleeping in the cafeteria every night." A third: "We are not getting eight hours of sleep. This can cause safety problems and accidents."

The restructuring is national in scope: UPS cut **48,000 operational positions in 2025** and plans **30,000 more in 2026**, while closing 93 buildings, 85 permanently. The company returned **$6.4 billion to shareholders in 2025**. Similar conditions were imposed at the Maspeth, Queens facility after its Midnight closure in May 2024, affecting roughly 120 workers. Teamsters officials told workers at meetings that grievances would not help and that strikes were "off the table."

### Canada's Liberal government prevails on CUPE to short-circuit militant strike of 4,400 WestJet flight attendants — Carl Bronski

Approximately **4,400 WestJet flight attendants** struck on Sunday, August 3, demanding an end to unpaid ground duties, better pay and improved scheduling. CUPE shut the strike down after just over a day, announcing a tentative agreement early Monday and ordering an immediate return to work.

The central grievance is WestJet's "credit hours" system, under which cabin crew are not paid for boarding, deplaning or pre-flight work. Union research found members average **35 unpaid hours per month** and earn below the federal minimum wage once ground time is counted.

Jobs Minister Patty Hajdu acknowledged the federal government's active role in pressuring both sides toward rapid settlement, with officials meeting separately with WestJet and CUPE throughout. CUPE president Alia Hussain called the deal "meaningful progress" that would "evolve the flight credit system," while withholding the actual contract terms from the membership for more than two days. Bronski's assessment is that CUPE functioned as a strikebreaker to pre-empt government intervention under Section 107 of the Canada Labour Code, which permits imposed binding arbitration without a parliamentary vote — the union apparatus enforcing the state's authority in advance of its exercise.

### Canada's ruling class exploits "anti-scab" legislation hailed by union bureaucracy to break strikes — Carl Bronski

Bill C-58, enacted in June 2025 and celebrated by the unions and the NDP as historic protection for workers, contains a decisive loophole: employers may legally deploy managers and supervisors to perform strikers' duties. Rogers Communications has used precisely this to circumvent the law.

Enforcement has been non-existent. The Canada Industrial Relations Board has issued only verbal reprimands despite violations by major employers; the law permits fines of up to $100,000, and **not a single fine has been levied**. The Bank of Canada illegally hired replacement security guards twice within weeks despite CIRB rulings against the practice. Canadian Pacific Kansas City continues to deploy scab contractors during an ongoing signals workers' strike, with multiple complaints filed. The WSWS warned at the time of passage that the bill would strengthen state control over bargaining rather than prevent strikebreaking; the record now confirms it.

### Australian military think tank strategises building "social licence" for war — Taylor Bennington

### Cost of living rises in New Zealand — Chris Ross

Annual inflation reached **4.1%** in the June quarter, the highest in over two years. Petrol is up **27.5%** and diesel **71%** year on year; electricity is up 12%; fruit 6%, meat 7.5%, bread 4.7%.

Real wages fell **6.4% between 2021 and Q1 2026 — the largest drop in the OECD**. Unemployment stands at an 11-year high of 5.6%, with youth unemployment at **17.3%**, a 30-year record. Welfare beneficiaries spend 8% more per week than they receive. **Four in ten households** borrow to cover daily expenses; 28% cannot absorb a $500 unexpected cost without debt; **450,000 adults (10.6% of the population)** fall behind on debt payments monthly. Material hardship among children has risen to **169,300 — one in seven**, rising to 38% among children in renting households receiving accommodation assistance.
