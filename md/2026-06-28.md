### 1. US-Iran war escalates as both sides strike military bases

The war between the United States and Iran sharply escalated over the weekend as both powers traded direct strikes on military installations, effectively ending a one-week-old ceasefire. On June 26, US Central Command struck Iranian missile and drone storage facilities and coastal radar sites near the port of Sirik in the Strait of Hormuz — the third US strike on Iran in three weeks — citing an Iranian drone attack on the cargo ship *Ever Lovely*. On June 27, Iran's Revolutionary Guard (IRGC) retaliated with drones and missiles against the Ali Al-Salem Air Base in Kuwait and the US Navy's Fifth Fleet base in Bahrain. No US casualties have been confirmed.

The proximate trigger is Iran's demand that all commercial vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz use an Iranian-designated northern route and obtain Iranian permission — a de facto blockade of a waterway carrying roughly 20% of global oil trade. Washington has rejected the demand. As Kevin Reed noted on the WSWS, the ceasefire's ambiguous language about Iran making "best efforts" on safe passage was "political theater from the outset," and Iranian parliamentary official Ebrahim Azizi accused the US of "attacking in the middle of negotiations once again."

Key facts:
- Trump warned Tehran: "There may come a point when we are no longer able to be reasonable," threatening to "complete the job"; Vice President Vance said "violence will be met with violence."
- Iran declared it had "neutralized" the US strikes and pledged further retaliation.
- Trump accused Iran of a "foolish violation" of the ceasefire after the *Ever Lovely* strike.
- The Hormuz standoff represents a potential chokepoint crisis unseen since the 1980s tanker wars.

The escalation marks the unraveling of a ceasefire that Washington itself had presented, only weeks ago, as a diplomatic triumph. The war the Trump administration launched in late February has already killed more than 3,000 Iranians by most estimates and triggered a global food and energy shock. Each cycle of strike and counter-strike now draws the Gulf monarchies — host to the very US bases Iran is targeting — deeper into a conflict their populations overwhelmingly oppose, while the danger of a miscalculation that pulls in additional powers grows. The targeting of US installations in Kuwait and Bahrain, even without casualties, represents a qualitative widening of the war beyond Iranian and Israeli territory and onto the soil of nominally neutral states.

### 2. Supreme Court strips 1.3 million of protected status, blocks asylum at border

The US Supreme Court's six-justice conservative majority issued two rulings, both authored by Justice Samuel Alito, that revoke judicial review of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) decisions and reinstate Customs and Border Protection's practice of turning away asylum seekers before they can formally "arrive" at the border. The TPS ruling strips legal status from an estimated 1.3 million people — Haitians, Syrians, Venezuelans, Somalis, Ethiopians, Salvadorans, and Ukrainians.

Justice Sonia Sotomayor read her dissent from the bench, invoking the MS St. Louis, the ship of Jewish refugees turned away from US shores before the Holocaust, and warning bluntly: "More people will die." As John Burton wrote on the WSWS, Alito dismissed Trump's racially inflammatory statements about Haiti as merely "heated language," underscoring the Court's role as an instrument of ruling-class reaction.

Key facts:
- The asylum ruling allows CBP to reject claims at the physical border, gutting a core protection under both US and international refugee law.
- Roughly 1.3 million people now face deportation to countries including earthquake-stricken Venezuela and war-torn Syria.
- The decisions arrive amid the administration's broader mass-deportation campaign and its assault on the courts that have ruled against it.

The TPS program, created by Congress in 1990, was designed precisely to shield nationals of countries devastated by war or natural disaster from deportation into danger. By removing judicial review, the Court has handed the executive unchecked discretion over the fate of more than a million long-settled residents — many of whom have lived and worked in the United States for a decade or more, raising US-citizen children. The asylum ruling, layered on top, effectively reconstructs the "Remain in Mexico" and metering regimes by judicial fiat, ensuring that those fleeing persecution can be turned back before any claim is heard. The two decisions together mark the most sweeping rollback of refugee protections in modern US history and a further consolidation of the Court as the instrument through which the financial oligarchy strips the working class — citizen and immigrant alike — of legal rights.

### 3. Venezuela earthquake death toll reaches 920 as rescue window closes

Twin earthquakes of magnitudes 7.2 and 7.5 — the strongest to strike Venezuela in 125 years — devastated the northern coast on June 24, leveling La Guaira and Catia la Mar. At least 920 are confirmed dead and more than 3,300 injured, with tens of thousands reported missing; UN reports suggest as many as 50,000 unaccounted for. Only 243 people have been rescued alive as of Friday, and the 48–72 hour survival window for trapped victims has now expired.

The United States deployed a 73-person Los Angeles County Fire team, rescue units from Virginia and Florida, the warships USS Fort Lauderdale and USS Billings, transport aircraft, and Marine Corps Major General Kevin J. Jarrard to direct operations, pledging $150 million in aid. As Andrea Lobo argued on the WSWS, this constitutes not humanitarian relief but military occupation under cover of disaster — noting that US sanctions had previously contracted Venezuela's economy by 80% over a decade and displaced 8 million people, with no comparable aid offered then.

Key facts:
- 383 buildings destroyed; up to 6.76 million people potentially affected.
- Survivors lacked heavy machinery, water, and rescue specialists; hospitals and morgues were overwhelmed.
- Residents reported a critically insufficient state rescue presence in the hardest-hit zones.

The disaster lands on a society already hollowed out by a decade of US sanctions, economic collapse, and the mismanagement of both Chavista and opposition factions. The absence of heavy rescue equipment, the overwhelmed hospitals, and the reliance on bare hands to dig through rubble are not natural features of an earthquake but the predictable result of an infrastructure starved of investment and a state apparatus oriented to repression rather than social provision. That Washington — which spent a decade strangling the Venezuelan economy and offered no aid as 8 million people fled — now arrives with warships and a Marine general underscores the cynicism of "humanitarian" intervention as a vehicle for geostrategic positioning in a country sitting atop the world's largest proven oil reserves.

### 4. South Africa migrant expulsion deadline arrives Tuesday

A nativist movement calling itself "March and March" has issued an ultimatum demanding that all undocumented immigrants leave South Africa by June 30 — a deadline now two days away. At least five Mozambican nationals have been confirmed killed, with further deaths reported across Malawian, Nigerian, and Ghanaian communities. More than 3,000 Malawian nationals, including hundreds of children, have been driven from their homes and are sheltering in an open field in Durban.

The crisis reflects the failure of post-apartheid capitalism: the attacks fall on the lowest-paid workers in agriculture and services, and the ANC-led government has not banned the "March and March" group despite the killings. Nigeria, Ghana, Mozambique, and Malawi have all dispatched aircraft to repatriate nationals; Nigeria moved 260 people on Thursday with more flights planned. South African police have tightened security ahead of the deadline.

That the government has tolerated, rather than proscribed, a movement openly issuing ethnic-cleansing ultimatums points to the deeper logic of the crisis. With official unemployment above 30% and youth joblessness far higher, the ANC has for years deflected mass anger over its failure to deliver the social transformation promised in 1994 by scapegoating migrant workers. The "March and March" campaign — echoing the xenophobic pogroms of 2008 and 2015 — channels the desperation of impoverished South Africans against fellow workers from across the region, dividing a class that shares common exploitation in the mines, farms, and service sectors of the continent's most industrialized economy.

### 5. John Bolton pleads guilty to retaining classified intelligence

Former national security adviser John Bolton pleaded guilty on June 26 in a Maryland federal court to one count of retaining national defense information, down from the 18 charges originally filed last October. The count concerned intelligence Bolton unlawfully retained about an adversary's plans for an attack on US forces — material containing human intelligence sources and methods and details of a covert action program. He shared more than 1,000 pages of diary-style notes about his duties, including classified content, with family members while writing his memoir.

Bolton agreed to pay a $2.25 million fine; sentencing is set for October 28, with a maximum prison term of 60 months under the plea deal. A prominent Trump critic, Bolton was prosecuted for essentially the same conduct for which Trump's own classified-documents case was dismissed — a contrast Democrats noted but on which they have largely declined to mount a defense. The selective application of the Espionage Act is significant beyond the figure of Bolton, a hawkish architect of US wars whose own record is one of advocating regime change and military aggression. The principle at stake — that the national-security state can deploy the most serious secrecy statutes against a critic while shielding the president from the identical charge — establishes a precedent that can and will be turned against left-wing dissidents, journalists, and whistleblowers. The political class's near-silence reflects a bipartisan consensus on expanding the powers of the secret state, with the criminalization of "leaks" and the retention of documents serving as a flexible weapon against opponents of either party.

### 6. Utah wildfires top 70,000 acres amid first-ever "particularly dangerous situation" warning

The Cottonwood Fire in Beaver County has grown past 70,000 acres at 0% containment and is poised to become the most destructive wildfire in Utah state history. The National Weather Service issued Utah's first-ever "Particularly Dangerous Situation" (PDS) red flag warning for the fire area. Across the state, 353 separate wildfires are burning, consuming more than 220 square miles. Every Utah county is in severe drought and 23 are in extreme drought.

Key facts:
- The Iron Fire has burned over 37,000 acres (23% contained); the town of Eureka (pop. ~600) has been fully evacuated.
- Utah has declared a state of emergency and restricted fireworks statewide ahead of the July 4 holiday.
- The fires underscore the accelerating climate emergency that capitalist governments continue to subordinate to military and corporate priorities.

The scale of this year's fire season — more than 220 square miles already consumed before the peak summer months — reflects the compounding effects of prolonged drought, record heat, and the systematic underfunding of fire-prevention and emergency-response capacity. Rural working-class towns such as Eureka, evacuated wholesale, bear the brunt while federal climate policy is rolled back and disaster agencies are gutted. The "particularly dangerous situation" designation, the first of its kind in Utah, is a meteorological acknowledgment of conditions that climate scientists have warned for years would become the new normal across the American West.

### 7. Ukraine and Russia trade heaviest drone barrages in weeks

Ukraine launched a major overnight attack on June 26–27, striking more than a dozen Russian regions, Russian-held Crimea, and surrounding seas. Russia responded with 129 drones against Ukraine, of which Ukrainian air defenses intercepted or neutralized 113. The exchange marked the heaviest aerial barrages in weeks as the war grinds into its fifth year.

Key facts:
- Ukrainian forces struck a Pantsir-S1 anti-aircraft system in Feodosia and targeted the car ferry *Petropavlovsk* near the Kerch Strait.
- Russian strikes injured 9 in Zaporizhzhia (including two children) and 13 in Sumy (including two children); dual drone strikes hit gas stations in Kharkiv's Derhachi district.
- Russia launched 57 ground attacks in a single day, with the heaviest fighting in the Huliaipole, Pokrovsk, and Sloviansk sectors.

The intensified exchanges come as Western military aid pipelines strain and NATO governments — preoccupied with the Middle East and their own rearmament drives — face mounting domestic opposition to open-ended war spending. The recurring pattern of civilian casualties, including children wounded in Zaporizhzhia and Sumy, exposes the human toll of a proxy conflict that the imperialist powers continue to escalate while ruling out any settlement that would halt the slaughter. For the WSWS, the war remains a catastrophe driven by the US-NATO drive to subordinate Russia and, ultimately, to encircle China — a trajectory that can be arrested only by the unified anti-war action of the international working class.

### Peru election deadlocked as Sánchez alleges fraud

With 99.7% of votes counted from the June 7 runoff, Keiko Fujimori leads Roberto Sánchez 50.11% to 49.89% — a margin of roughly 40,687 votes out of more than 18 million cast. Sánchez has formally petitioned the National Jury of Elections to nullify results from 119 consular offices abroad, alleging systematic manipulation of overseas ballots, and has declared: "I will not recognise a Fujimori presidency." International observers from the OAS and EU reported the election as smooth overall, undermining the fraud claims but not resolving the legal dispute. Fujimori, daughter of imprisoned former dictator Alberto Fujimori, would mark a hard-right turn in a country still reckoning with his legacy of forced sterilizations and repression. The standoff revives memories of Peru's 2021 constitutional crisis, when a similarly narrow result triggered months of legal warfare and right-wing mobilization. A Fujimori presidency would likely bring labor-law rollbacks, accelerated resource extraction in the Amazon, and intensified repression of Indigenous and campesino communities — continuing the dynasty's record under a constitutional veneer. Whichever faction prevails, the Peruvian working class confronts a political establishment thoroughly subordinated to mining capital and international finance, with no section of the bourgeoisie offering a way out of the country's chronic instability.

A board member called it "an effort to use federal law enforcement to intimidate and halt voter registration and organizing efforts." Civil-liberties advocates describe it as among the most direct uses of federal law enforcement against civic organizing since COINTELPRO, concentrated in majority-Black urban areas.

## Science, technology, and public health

- [US measles outbreak reaches 2,134 cases in 41 states](#science-technology-and-public-health) — elimination status, held since 2000, faces a November review as kindergarten MMR coverage slips to 92.5%
- First NEJM-reported human case of H5N5 avian influenza documented, a subtype never before seen in humans
- COVID-19 activity at "very low" nationally, with ~870 projected weekly hospital admissions
- 78 AI chatbot bills active across 27 states as Big Tech's AI valuations correct

**Measles surges toward loss of US elimination status.** As of June 25, the CDC counts 2,134 confirmed measles cases across 41 jurisdictions in 30 active outbreaks, with roughly 92% of cases in unvaccinated or unknown-status individuals. South Carolina (~700) and Utah (~520) account for more than half of all 2026 cases. National kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.5%, below the 95% herd-immunity threshold, and the US could lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000 when authorities conduct a formal review in November. The resurgence tracks the collapse of childhood vaccination rates amplified by official anti-vaccine rhetoric and the gutting of public-health infrastructure — a preventable epidemic that falls hardest on poor and rural communities with the least access to care. Three measles deaths have already been recorded this year, the first US measles fatalities in nearly a decade.

**Novel avian flu subtype infects a human for the first time.** The *New England Journal of Medicine* published a case report documenting the first human infection with highly pathogenic avian influenza A(H5N5), a subtype never previously reported in humans. Viral load was substantially higher in lower-respiratory-tract specimens than in upper-respiratory ones, complicating early detection. Separately, a University of Pittsburgh study explained why H5N1-infected dairy cows shed heavily in milk with few respiratory symptoms: the virus's preferred cell-surface receptors are concentrated in bovine mammary tissue rather than lung tissue — a finding with immediate implications for farm biosecurity and spillover risk to farmworkers.

Separately, researchers identified stem cells appearing in aging tissue that dramatically accelerate visceral fat formation, a candidate target against middle-age metabolic disease.

## Arts and culture

The most politically charged cultural development of the day is the Trump administration's "Great American State Fair," analyzed by David Walsh on the WSWS as empty patriotic spectacle — state pavilions offering shallow "sensory experiences" in place of genuine cultural expression. Walsh uses the fair as a lens onto the administration's broader assault on arts funding: the attempted elimination of the National Endowment for the Arts ($207 million in 2026), the National Endowment for the Humanities, and the Institute of Museum and Library Services, with Congress preserving funding only at 2025 levels under threat. Walsh calculates that the NEA's budget is 4,879 times smaller than military spending and notes that even funded arts organizations now self-censor under threat of defunding. Invoking Eisenhower's 1953 warning that arms spending diverts resources from education and culture, he argues the spectacle reflects capitalism's "ruthless and barbaric character" in a period of war and austerity.

The episode is emblematic of a wider pattern in which the cultural superstructure is increasingly subordinated to militarism and nationalism. As the United States approaches the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, the official commemorations promise pageantry stripped of the revolutionary-democratic content the WSWS insists must be reclaimed by the working class rather than ceded to a ruling class busy dismantling democratic rights.

The contrast is instructive: while the state mounts hollow festivals celebrating national greatness, the genuine cultural institutions that serve working people — libraries, museums, community arts programs, public-broadcasting affiliates — are starved of the modest sums that sustain them. The Institute of Museum and Library Services alone underwrites library services in thousands of communities that have no commercial alternative. The targeting of these bodies, whose combined budgets are a rounding error against the Pentagon's, reveals that the aim is not fiscal savings but the deliberate impoverishment of independent cultural and intellectual life.

Roundup:
- **Defunding as cultural policy:** The NEA/NEH/IMLS threats function less as a budgetary measure — the sums are trivial against the federal budget — than as a disciplining mechanism over independent cultural institutions, museums, and libraries.
- **Self-censorship:** Walsh's reporting that even funded organizations preemptively soften programming illustrates how the threat of defunding produces a chilling effect well beyond the institutions directly targeted.
- **Historical memory:** The contest over the meaning of 1776 — and the parallel polemic against the 1619 Project's framing of the Revolution as a counter-revolution for slavery — is itself a cultural-political struggle over how the past is mobilized in the present.

For ICFI readers, the through-line is the WSWS framework: art and culture are not entertainment commodities or lifestyle content but expressions of definite social relations. The defunding of public arts, the nationalist repackaging of history, and the criminalization of dissent (see the Ulm 5 and Prairieland cases below) are facets of a single process — a ruling class stripping society of both material resources and critical cultural memory as it prepares for war abroad and class conflict at home.

### WSWS webinar on the American Revolution: The historical significance and contemporary relevance of 1776 — Joseph Kishore

WSWS national secretary Joseph Kishore reports on a webinar, led by WSWS editorial board chairman David North, examining the American Revolution's meaning as the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence approaches "amid an escalating assault on democratic rights." The argument is that the Declaration's universal assertion of human equality abolished "a world of ascribed status" and set a standard by which all later social movements — above all abolitionism — would judge society. The webinar's chief polemical target is the 1619 Project, which cast the Revolution as a counter-revolution to preserve slavery; North countered that "nobody, including its enemies, thought it was anything but a revolution" at the time, and characterized the "petty-bourgeois view of history" that "substitutes race for class" as having "no explanatory power." The contemporary application is direct: panelists drew parallels between Trump's actions and the Declaration's charges against George III — military supremacy over civil authority, transporting people for "pretended offenses" — concluding that the democratic heritage of 1776 can be defended only by the working class on an international and socialist basis. Kishore's closing argument is that the defense of democratic rights — free speech, due process, the right to political opposition — is inseparable from the struggle against capitalism and imperialist war: the same ruling class that invokes the Founders is dismantling the rights they proclaimed, and only a movement rooted in the actual social content of 1776's universalism, carried through to socialist transformation, can carry that heritage forward. The polemic against the 1619 Project is thus not an academic dispute but a fight over whether the working class will be armed with, or disarmed of, the revolutionary-democratic traditions of the past.
