### 1. US halts Iran bombing for a fourth night as Trump claims talks are underway

Trump directed the US military not to conduct new strikes on Iran on Friday, breaking a nearly two-week streak of nightly attacks. The pause has now held for a fourth consecutive night, with Iran reciprocating by suspending its retaliatory strikes. Administration officials attributed the decision to two factors: dwindling munitions stockpiles, and the assessment by Admiral Brad Cooper, commander of US Central Command, that the bombing campaign around the Strait of Hormuz had reached the limit of its effectiveness.

Trump told reporters: "We're talking to [the Iranians] right now. I think they're getting more and more serious as the days go by. We're locked and loaded and ready to go, but we're talking to them." Iran's Foreign Ministry disputed the claim outright, stating that no negotiations are currently underway. UN Ambassador Mike Waltz said Trump was giving the talks "some space" while weighing further escalation.

**Key facts:**

- Since the war was launched in February, **18 US troops have been killed and 624 wounded**, according to figures compiled from Pentagon reporting. The Pentagon has moved casualties incurred since July 7 to a separate "overseas operations casualties" page, requiring anyone seeking a full accounting to combine multiple sources.
- Ship traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains a fraction of prewar levels — **fewer than 10 commodity vessels passed through as of Monday**, against roughly 100 on a typical pre-war day.
- Qatar and Pakistan are carrying indirect messages between Washington and Tehran; regional officials describe the informal channel as "working" to sustain the pause.
- Senior administration officials conceded that renewed bombing was unlikely to force Iran back to the table, with one noting the strikes had the opposite of their intended effect — keeping Iran's population cohesive and allowing the leadership to focus attention on an external enemy.
- Trump hosts Netanyahu at the White House Tuesday to discuss Iran, the Lebanon negotiations, and expansion of the Abraham Accords. The next round of US-brokered Israel-Lebanon talks is scheduled for Rome next week. The administration also announced steps last week toward a civil nuclear partnership with Saudi Arabia.

### 4. Europe's "firewave": 360,000 evacuated in France and Spain amid record heat

More than 360,000 people have been evacuated as wildfires swept southwestern France and northern Spain — among the largest evacuations in French history. Authorities deployed 2,500 firefighters, 1,500 military personnel and nearly 1,200 police. An incoming heat wave threatens temperatures above 40°C (104°F) across the affected zone.

The WSWS Perspective column by Thomas Scripps today reports that evacuation of Bordeaux itself — a city of 250,000, with 850,000 in the metropolitan area — was under active consideration, and that some 400,000 people in total have been displaced by the fires. A spokesperson for France's National Firefighters Federation described the resource situation as "a David-versus-Goliath scenario."

**Key facts:**

- Estimates of heat-related deaths across Europe this summer range from **10,000 to 25,000**, with some 277,000 workers injured in heat-related incidents.
- Britain is experiencing its fourth heatwave of the year.
- In East Africa, drought has left 1.5 million Ugandans affected, with 19 confirmed starvation deaths, while **40 million people across the Horn of Africa** face acute food insecurity.
- Simultaneous extreme-rainfall disasters struck the United States, Bangladesh (more than 50 deaths) and Chile in the same period.

### 5. Ukraine war internationalizes: Zelensky in Portsmouth, 30,000 more North Korean troops sought

Zelensky met new UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham aboard the carrier HMS *Queen Elizabeth* in Portsmouth on Monday — the first foreign-leader visit since Burnham took office a week earlier. Burnham called it a sign of Britain's "unwavering" support for Ukraine; the two discussed joint drone production and Ukraine's urgent need for ballistic-missile interceptors ahead of winter. Zelensky meets Trump at the White House Tuesday.

Zelensky also said Russia has requested **an additional 30,000 troops and missile launchers from North Korea** — more than double the number of North Koreans currently believed deployed. Preparations for the deployment have reportedly been underway in Russia's Voronezh region, near the Ukrainian border, since June.

**Key facts:**

- An estimated 14,000 North Korean troops were deployed to Russia's Kursk region in 2024; South Korean intelligence estimates roughly **2,000 North Korean soldiers have since been killed**.
- Russia launched more than **1,560 drones and 56 missiles** across Ukraine in a recent 24-hour period, killing at least 11 people. Strikes reached the Slovakian border, prompting Slovakia to close a crossing. A separate overnight attack on July 26 used seven ballistic missiles, one Kh-59/69 cruise missile and 136 attack drones.
- On Saturday, July 26, a Ukrainian long-range drone struck the Iranian dry cargo ship *Ana* in the Caspian Sea, killing one sailor and injuring another. Kyiv claimed the vessel carried "drones and missiles from Iran to Russia"; Russia and Iran called it a civilian cargo ship. It had departed from Astrakhan.
- Iran's Foreign Ministry called the strike "an act of aggression" and vowed to "defend its national interests and security." Ukraine's foreign minister replied that "the regime in Tehran is a direct accomplice to Russian aggression."
- As Kevin Reed writes on the WSWS, the Caspian strike marks the point at which the Ukraine and Iran wars have become a single, converging conflict — with Ukrainian drone strikes having damaged roughly one-third of Russian refining capacity while the Gulf chokepoints are simultaneously disrupted.

### 6. Asian chip stocks collapse: KOSPI falls 10.8 percent, triggering circuit breaker

South Korea's KOSPI index closed Tuesday at 6,023.63, down **10.84 percent** — a decline steep enough to trigger a market circuit breaker — as the semiconductor selloff that began last week turned into a rout. Markets read the deal not as validating demand but as evidence of circular vendor financing propping up the AI buildout.

**Key facts:**

- Samsung Electronics fell more than 7 percent; Kioxia plunged roughly 18 percent; SoftBank fell over 6 percent; Advantest and Tokyo Electron each dropped about 10 percent. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 2.2 percent.
- The KOSPI has now fallen roughly 30 percent over the course of July.
- A 30-day stock dispersion gauge hit an **all-time high in July**, eclipsing the previous record set in April 2025 during Trump's global tariff rollout.
- Tyler Richey of Sevens Report called the sector divergence "a measurable market warning signal." BTIG's Jonathan Krinsky warned that historically low stock correlations alongside near-record index prices leave "only one way for them to move."
- The Hang Seng bucked the regional selloff, closing up 0.10 percent; the Shanghai Composite fell 0.40 percent.

### 7. UN finds "hallmarks of genocide" in Sudan as drone deaths jump 600 percent

A UN fact-finding mission has concluded that the Rapid Support Forces' siege and capture of Al-Fashir bore "the hallmarks of genocide." Famine has now been formally declared both there and in Kadugli, the capital of South Kordofan. The war has killed at least 59,000 people and displaced roughly 13 million since fighting began.

**Key facts:**

- Drone strikes killed **more than 1,000 civilians in the first five months of 2026 alone** — an 81 percent increase in the number of drone attacks and a **600 percent jump in drone-related deaths** compared with 2024-25, according to the UN human rights office.
- More than **300 children** have been killed or injured in the past six months, UNICEF reports. A strike on Al Musalamiya village in North Kordofan killed at least 16 civilians.
- The proliferation of armed drones — supplied through the UAE and other external backers of the RSF, and through Egypt, Turkey and Iran on the army's side — has transformed the conflict into a remote-killing war against the civilian population.

### US measles cases hit a 35-year high

As of July 23, the CDC has confirmed **2,318 measles cases** nationwide in 2026, already surpassing the 2,289 recorded for all of 2025 — the highest annual total since measles was declared eliminated in the United States in 2000. Seven states account for over 84 percent of cases, led by South Carolina (670) and Utah (522). **146 people (6 percent) have been hospitalized**, and only 4 percent of cases occurred in fully vaccinated individuals.

The driver is straightforward: kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.5 percent, below the 95 percent herd-immunity threshold. This is the direct product of the anti-vaccine campaign now institutionalized at the top of the federal public health apparatus, combined with the gutting of local public health departments.

### Inherited genetics shape cancer evolution after DNA damage (*Nature*)

Researchers led by Aitken et al., published July 22 in *Nature*, exposed four genetically distinct mouse strains to a single identical dose of the carcinogen diethylnitrosamine, then applied whole-genome sequencing, transcriptomics and histopathology. They found that **inherited genetic background — not merely acquired mutations — determines the evolutionary trajectory of liver tumors**. This provides direct experimental evidence for why individuals with comparable carcinogen exposure (smoking, UV radiation) develop radically different cancer outcomes, and strengthens the case for genetically stratified screening. The University of Cambridge, which led the work, framed it as explaining "why DNA damage from smoking and UV rays may cause cancer in some people but not others."

## Arts and culture

### Bill Oddie, 1941-2026

Bill Oddie, the British comedian, musician, writer and naturalist, died Saturday at 85. Oddie belonged to the generation of Cambridge Footlights performers whose satire — beginning with the *I'm Sorry, I'll Read That Again* radio company and reaching a mass audience with *The Goodies* (1970-82) — reflected the sharp erosion of deference in postwar British life. *The Goodies* was frequently dismissed as children's slapstick, but its best episodes were pointed lampoons of the British establishment, the tabloid press, and the emergent racist right; the 1975 episode "South Africa," on apartheid, was more explicitly political than anything then permitted in comparable US network comedy. Oddie wrote most of the series' songs and scored a number of chart singles.

His second career, as a birder and wildlife broadcaster on *Springwatch* and *Birding with Bill Oddie*, was of a piece with the first. Chris Packham recalled that Oddie "was invariably funny and occasionally furious. He loved birds and loathed those who harmed them." Co-star Graeme Garden remembered him as "a bit of a rebel" who would "challenge the status quo." Oddie also spoke publicly about his clinical depression at a time when few figures in British broadcasting did.

### The Lalique museum heist and the security of public collections

French investigators are still assessing losses from the July 5 burglary at the Musée Lalique in Wingen-sur-Moder, Alsace, where three masked men smashed six display cases and removed 27 pieces of jewelry and art in an eleven-minute operation. Losses are estimated at €4.5 million (roughly $5 million). Among the stolen works is René Lalique's *Femme libellule ailes ouvertes* (Dragonfly Woman with Open Wings, 1898-1900), one of the defining objects of Art Nouveau — a work whose fusion of gold, enamel and glass deliberately refused the hierarchy separating "precious" materials from craft materials.

The museum's vice-chairman disclosed that the alarm sounded but the contracted private security company failed to intervene immediately and did not alert the gendarmerie. Coming nine months after the Louvre jewel theft, the pattern points to a structural condition rather than a run of bad luck: publicly held collections outsourced to low-bid private security firms, in a period when art has become a preferred store of value for the wealthy and a target for organized theft. The immediate victims are the public, who lose access to works held in trust for them.

This is the essential function of Jacobin's Mamdani coverage: to present the occupation of a municipal executive office within the Democratic Party as the concrete content of "socialism."

**"Socialists Aren't Naive About Human Nature"** by Matt McManus answers the perennial conservative objection by arguing that socialists "want a system that guards against tendencies to greed and domination." The formulation is revealing: socialism is presented as a superior institutional design for managing fixed human dispositions, rather than as the resolution of a specific historical contradiction between socialized production and private appropriation. This is the language of liberal political philosophy, not Marxism, and it dovetails with the journal's practical orientation toward reform of the existing state.

**"DOGE May Have Given You Diarrhea"** by Hayley Brown links the administration's assault on farmworkers and federal employees to the record cyclosporiasis outbreak — a genuinely useful connection, though the article's remedy is restored agency staffing rather than any mobilization of the workers involved.

Also notable: **"In Morocco, Royalist Authorities Silence Critical Rap Lyrics"** by Rona Lorimer documents the arrest of rapper Mehdi Black Wind, and Ben Burgis's **"Mamdani Is Right. Netanyahu Belongs Behind Bars"** (July 25), which endorses the mayor's arrest rhetoric.

**Assessment:** The day's output consolidates the line that the Mamdani administration is the model for American socialism, with theoretical material tailored to make that identification philosophically respectable.

### Left Voice

**"Stand against the State's Attack on The People's Forum and BreakThrough News"** (July 23) responds to House Ways and Means Committee subpoenas issued July 22 demanding internal communications and financial records from both organizations. Left Voice's opposition is unequivocal and correct. But the article frames the attack as an assault on free speech and civil liberties by a "MAGA-led" committee, rather than situating it within the bipartisan construction of a state apparatus for suppressing anti-war opposition — an apparatus built up under Democratic as well as Republican administrations, and one whose most consequential recent applications have been against opponents of the Gaza genocide.

A companion piece by Emma Lee and M. Carlstad (July 24) examines Vietnam's military-industrial cooperation with Israel alongside its rhetorical support for the Palestinian struggle — a genuinely worthwhile subject, treated as an exposure of hypocrisy rather than as a demonstration of what national-bourgeois "anti-imperialism" amounts to in the era of globalized production.

**Assessment:** More willing than most of the American pseudo-left to take up state repression directly, but consistently frames these as democratic-rights questions detachable from the war drive that generates them.

### Socialist Alternative

**"Zohran Says Netanyahu Not Welcome In NYC: Kick Him Out With Mass Action!"** (July 23) addresses Mamdani's campaign pledge to arrest Netanyahu, who faces ICC charges, should he visit New York for September's UN General Assembly. Mamdani has now announced that his administration explored legal options and concluded the city lacks the authority. Socialist Alternative's response is to accept this without criticism and substitute a call for "mass action" to make Netanyahu unwelcome. The organization has thereby converted a broken promise into an occasion for a mobilization it does not intend to lead in political opposition to the administration that broke it.

**"The Democrats Won't Save Us — The Case For A New Party"** (July 8) argues for a new party of the left. The proposal is not accompanied by any assessment of the program such a party would fight for, or of the record of the DSA-Democratic Party fusion that Socialist Alternative has itself accommodated to in New York.

**Assessment:** The organization is attempting to maintain formal independence from the Democrats while functioning as a left flank of the Mamdani administration.

### Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

Socialist Worker's coverage of the Burnham premiership runs across three weeks and one register: hopeful uncertainty. Judy Cox's **"Burnham promises change, but can he deliver?"** (July 17) reports his promise of "the biggest change in 40 years." Alex Callinicos's **"Burnham will need more than words"** (July 20) allows that Burnham is "a much more skilful politician" than Starmer. **"Burnham cabinet is a Starmer tribute act"** (July 22) and **"Will Burnham drive Labour in a new direction?"** (July 23) continue posing as an open question what the composition of the cabinet has already answered.

**Assessment:** Three weeks of asking whether a Labour prime minister installed by the same apparatus that installed Starmer, retaining his cabinet and his commitments to NATO rearmament, will deliver change. The persistence of the question is the political line: the SWP's orientation requires that Labour remain a potential vehicle.

### Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

**"The many faces of Andy Burnham — how long until the mask slips?"** and **"Burnham's 'Number 10 North' and the myth behind 'Manchesterism'"** are considerably sharper than the SWP's treatment, dismissing Burnham's regional devolution record as insufficient reform and predicting rapid crisis. **"Farage's sham resignation: bin all the liars, crooks, and racists!"** treats the Clacton manoeuvre. The weakness is that the analysis terminates in the prediction that workers will be disappointed and then turn to the RCP — a syndicalist-flavoured substitute for a worked-out revolutionary program and any accounting of the Labour Party's role in the imperialist war drive.

### Liberation News / PSL

The PSL's July output centers on its independent congressional campaign in the Bronx — Andre Easton, a teacher, standing against Ritchie Torres in November — and on opposition to National Guard deployments and ICE raids in Los Angeles. The campaign is presented as the concrete alternative to the Democrats. What is absent is any programmatic content beyond a list of demands, or any perspective for the international unification of the working class; the orientation remains toward pressure politics in a single district.
