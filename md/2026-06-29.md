### 1. US-Iran ceasefire collapses as IRGC strikes US bases in Kuwait and Bahrain

The June 17–18 ceasefire memorandum between Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian has collapsed. After fresh US airstrikes on Iranian military sites at Sirik and Qeshm — the second consecutive day of US strikes near the Strait of Hormuz — the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps launched a joint Navy-Aerospace operation between 2:00 and 3:00 a.m. local time on June 28, targeting eight US military installations across the Gulf. The war, which began February 28, 2026 when the US and Israel launched an air war against Iran, has now killed over 3,400 Iranians.

As Andre Damon noted on the WSWS, both Republican and Democratic establishment factions oppose the ceasefire "as a sign of weakness" and are pushing for further escalation despite demonstrated Iranian military capability and broad popular opposition.

Key facts:
- Confirmed targets: Ali Al Salem Airbase in Kuwait and the US Fifth Fleet headquarters at Salman Port in Bahrain, struck with ballistic missiles and drones; the IRGC claims all were "destroyed."
- The Fifth Fleet headquarters has been rendered unusable, with a reported $400 million required to rebuild; more than 20 US regional bases have been struck and 13 US troops killed.
- Bahrain called the attack "a dangerous escalation"; Kuwait condemned a "flagrant violation" of its sovereignty. A Qatari national was killed by shrapnel from an Iranian drone.
- Iranian casualties include 376 children; the Pentagon has run up $29 billion in costs, with an $87.6 billion White House supplemental spending request.
- Trump warned Iran "will no longer exist" if strikes continue; Tehran threatened a "complete halt" to negotiations.

### 2. Iran reasserts sole control of Strait of Hormuz as 14 million barrels a day stay offline

The Strait of Hormuz crisis has re-intensified alongside the ceasefire collapse. On June 29, Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi warned against "any attempt to adopt new or separate arrangements" for the waterway, declaring that Iran holds "sole control." An adviser to Supreme Leader Khamenei separately warned Oman against aligning with Gulf states on Hormuz transit arrangements.

The strait — through which roughly 25% of world seaborne oil and 20% of global LNG normally transits — has been largely blocked since February 28. The IMF warns the war has "halted momentum in global growth" and risks a "major energy crisis."

Key facts:
- Global oil supply has been cut by an estimated 14 million barrels per day since the closure began.
- Oil prices surged more than 50% at the height of the conflict before retreating on mid-June ceasefire hopes.
- On June 27, the US Navy's Joint Maritime Information Center announced a widened navigational corridor near Oman.

### 3. Venezuela twin earthquakes kill more than 1,450 as US troops deploy

Two powerful earthquakes struck Venezuela's coast Wednesday evening in rapid succession — a magnitude 7.2 followed seconds later by a 7.5 — leaving at least 1,450 dead and roughly 3,150 injured, with "tens of thousands" reported missing. The capital Caracas, the main port region of La Guaira state, and the town of Caraballeda suffered severe building collapses. Venezuela is not historically earthquake-prone, catching residents unprepared.

The WSWS Perspective by Andrea Lobo frames the catastrophe as "a crime of US imperialism" rooted in decades of sanctions and a January 2026 US military invasion (see WSWS coverage below).

Key facts:
- Rescue teams from Bolivia, Ecuador and El Salvador are on the ground; a 60-year-old woman was extracted alive after 86 hours trapped.
- 100 US Air Force personnel arrived to repair Simón Bolívar International Airport; 130 US Marines deployed to reopen La Guaira port for aid delivery.
- Infrastructure recovery: La Guaira electricity 75% restored, water 68%, roads 90%.

### 6. Sudan civil war: 1,000 civilians killed by drones in five months as 33 million face starvation

Sudan's civil war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the RSF paramilitary has entered its fourth year. Drone strikes killed more than 1,000 Sudanese civilians in just the first five months of 2026 — a 600% increase over 2024–2025. More than 33 million Sudanese are now in what aid agencies call the "world's largest hunger crisis."

In May, Sudan formally accused Ethiopia and the UAE of orchestrating drone attacks on Khartoum International Airport, alleging drones launched from Bahir Dar in Ethiopia and military support routed through the UAE to the RSF. Bloomberg's June 26 report highlights Western energy companies scouting Sudanese oil fields even amid active fighting — underscoring the resource dimension of the conflict.

Key facts:
- The SAF has retaken Khartoum; the RSF controls Darfur and parts of the west.
- Chad shut its eastern border with Sudan indefinitely in February over cross-border incursions.

### Trump religion commission attacks church-state separation

A 224-page draft report from Trump's Religious Liberty Commission — chaired by Texas Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick with Vice Chairman Ben Carson, and including Franklin Graham, Cardinal Timothy Dolan and Dr. Phil — urges replacing the separation of church and state with the building of "bridges" between them, casting the doctrine as a misapplied invention rather than a founding principle. Recommendations include DOJ guidance narrowing the Establishment Clause, a DOJ religious-liberty litigation task force, reporting portals for alleged violations, repeal of the Johnson Amendment barring churches from political activity, and changes to military religious-accommodation procedures. As Patrick Martin notes on the WSWS, the commission ignores that most early American states eventually disestablished their official churches.

### UAW monitor finds Fain abused office, threatens sanctions

Federal Monitor Neil Barofsky's report, filed June 25 in the Eastern District of Michigan, found UAW President Shawn Fain exhibited a "recurring pattern of retaliation" and "abused the authority of his office" in matters involving his fiancée and her sister. Fain allegedly sought a bonus for his fiancée, a non-UAW employee at the Stellantis National Training Center, and intervened to let her injured sister stay home. Fain called the report "false" and suggested it was retaliation for the UAW's support of a Gaza ceasefire. As Shannon Jones argues on the WSWS, the findings confirm the bureaucracy cannot be reformed from within.

### US measles cases top 2,100 as vaccination rates fall

The US recorded 2,134 confirmed measles cases for 2026 as of June 25 — among the highest annual totals in decades — as childhood vaccination rates continue to slide. Public-health specialists warn that the erosion of measles-mumps-rubella coverage, compounded by the Trump administration's hostility to vaccine mandates (now reflected in the Religious Liberty Commission's call for expanded vaccination exemptions), is reopening the door to diseases that were effectively eliminated. The outbreak is a direct consequence of the defunding and political delegitimization of public-health infrastructure.

### Lancet study finds HPV vaccine prevented cervical-cancer deaths

A study in The Lancet led by Queen Mary University of London and funded by Cancer Research UK estimates the HPV vaccine has prevented around 200 deaths from cervical cancer in England, finding that no women aged 20–24 died of the disease between 2020 and 2024 — the first such period on record. The result is a striking demonstration of what socialized, publicly funded preventive medicine can achieve, in sharp contrast to the market-driven and increasingly anti-science direction of US health policy.

### China drafts $295 billion AI data-center grid

Other developments across science and technology:

- **AI infrastructure:** China has drafted a $295 billion plan to build a national AI data-center grid running on 80% domestically produced silicon, targeting a 2028 timeline — a direct response to US chip export controls. Qualcomm unveiled its Dragonfly data-center CPU and AI300 accelerator, signing Meta as a major customer.
- **Chip bottlenecks:** Industry analysts project 30–50% of planned 2026 US data-center capacity will slip to 2028 amid power-grid interconnection queues exceeding total grid capacity. March missile and drone strikes on Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG hub removed 20% of global LNG supply, spiking electricity costs for fabs in Taiwan and South Korea — a direct line from the Gulf war to the tech supply chain.
- **Astrophysics:** University of Copenhagen researchers published a Nature paper explaining "little red dots" in early-universe imaging as young, relatively small supermassive black holes shrouded in dense ionized gas.
- **H5N1:** The CDC has moved to streamline H5N1 avian-flu reporting as surveillance of dairy-cattle infections continues — a change critics warn could reduce transparency.

## Arts and culture

The most significant cultural event documented this cycle is the 2026 World Press Photo exhibition at De Nieuwe Kerk in Amsterdam, reviewed for the WSWS by Daniel Woreck. The exhibition's prize-winning works confront the central catastrophes of the present: Carol Guzy's Photo of the Year, "Separated by ICE," depicts an ICE detention; Saber Nuraldin's first-finalist image captures Palestinians struggling for food aid in Gaza; and Saher Alghorra's series documents a family breaking the Ramadan fast in the ruins of their home. The WSWS argues that while these images document real suffering with genuine artistic power, the World Press Photo organization claims a false neutrality — partnering with imperialist-aligned institutions such as Bellingcat while presenting crises as isolated tragedies rather than systemic products of capitalism and war. The contradiction is instructive: photographers risk their lives to capture the human cost of imperialist violence, while the institutional framing strips that violence of its political authorship.

The exhibition's themes — forced separation under ICE, famine engineered as a weapon in Gaza — dovetail directly with the political content of this briefing, a reminder that the most serious documentary art of the period is inseparable from the wars and repression dominating world events.

### The earthquake disaster in Venezuela: A crime of US imperialism — Andrea Lobo

Beyond the casualty figures reported above, the Perspective advances a distinct historical and political analysis. It traces US interference in Venezuela from Theodore Roosevelt's dispatch of warships during the 1902–1903 Venezuela Crisis, through Washington's backing of the dictators Juan Vicente Gómez and Marcos Pérez Jiménez, down to the present — a century-long record that, Lobo argues, systematically stripped the country of the capacity to build resilient infrastructure.

The article's pivotal claim is that in January 2026 the Trump administration militarily invaded Venezuela, abducted President Nicolás Maduro, and installed a puppet government under Delcy Rodríguez; the ensuing six months of "chaos and disorganization" crippled the state's disaster-response capacity. Lobo cites UN Special Rapporteurs who found US sanctions "produced mass suffering and contributed to over 100,000 excess deaths" before the quake. Drawing an explicit analogy to Haiti after 2010, the piece argues the US military deployment serves to consolidate imperial control, not deliver aid. The programmatic conclusion calls on Venezuelan workers to "organize independent rank-and-file relief committees" outside both the US-installed government and the Maduro-era bureaucracy, and demands expropriation of US corporate holdings in the oil sector as the basis for reconstruction.

### Rank-and-file candidate Will Lehman issues statement to Nexteer workers — Our reporters

This WSWS-only labor story warrants attention. Lehman's statement follows the June 26 ratification of a Nexteer Automotive contract that passed narrowly (54–46% among production workers) after three prior rejections by margins of 96%, 73% and 55%. Lehman argues the vote is "null and void" due to voting irregularities, use of company property for balloting, and the firing of dissident worker Antwiane Sanders. He demands Sanders's reinstatement, recall of the bargaining committee, and rank-and-file committees independent of the UAW apparatus. The contract preserves the same $27/hour top rate as 2005, even as inflation has eroded real wages. The dispute is a concrete application of the analysis in the Fain monitor story above: the same apparatus implicated in Barofsky's report is here pushing through a contract over repeated rank-and-file rejection.

### New Zealand Greens advance fraudulent wealth tax policy — Tom Peters

Tom Peters critiques the NZ Green Party's proposed 2.5% annual wealth tax on assets over $10 million, 33% inheritance tax on transfers over $1 million, and increases to the top income (39%→45%) and corporate (28%→33%) rates.
