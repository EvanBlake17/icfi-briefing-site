### 1. Trump cancels Iran strikes, claims imminent "peace deal" as Iran stays cautious

President Trump announced Thursday that he had cancelled scheduled strikes on Iran, writing that he acted "based on discussions with the Islamic Republic of Iran brought to the highest level of Iranian leadership and approved." Hours later he declared the war "ended" and claimed a memorandum of understanding was nearly finalized, with a peace signing possible "within days." But the sequence was characteristically chaotic: that same Thursday morning Trump had threatened on Truth Social to invade Iran and seize Kharg Island, its main oil export terminal, before reversing course by early afternoon.

Tehran poured cold water on the claims. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said reports of a deal were "speculative" and that "nothing has been finalised," though a separate MFA official said an MOU was "under consideration." The US naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz remains in force pending any agreement. The US-Israel war on Iran began February 28, 2026 and is now past its 100th day. As Andre Damon noted in the WSWS Perspective, by CNN's count Trump has declared a deal "imminent" at least 38 times since March — each followed by renewed bombing — a pattern Damon called "the mafia principle: Do as we demand, or we will murder you."

Key facts:
- Trump, listing eleven governments from Israel and Saudi Arabia to Egypt as parties to the supposed deal, conspicuously omitted Iran from the list
- Brent crude fell roughly 1% to below $89.50/barrel on ceasefire hopes; global equities surged
- Seymour Hersh reported Trump raised the possible use of low-yield nuclear weapons against Iran's underground missile factories at a White House meeting; the *Wall Street Journal* editorial board likened his choice to the one George W. Bush faced over Iraq
- Ken Klippenstein reported an April 7 order dispatched 82nd Airborne paratroopers to Israel under plans "completed since February, for seizing Kharg Island"

### 2. US strikes kill three Indian merchant sailors off Oman

US forces killed three Indian merchant sailors aboard the Palau-flagged MT Settebello on June 10, part of a four-day campaign of strikes on commercial vessels accused of carrying Iranian oil in violation of the US blockade. India confirmed the deaths of deck cadet Aditya Sharma, engine fitter Shivanand Chaurasiya and Chief Engineer Patnala Suresh, and summoned the US Embassy's deputy chief of mission, Jason Meeks, to lodge "a strong protest."

CENTCOM justified the strikes as enforcement of the naval blockade. Three ships were hit in four days: the MT Marivex (June 8, 24 Indian crew rescued by Oman), the MT Settebello (June 10, three killed), and the MT Jalveer (June 11, when a US aircraft fired two Hellfire missiles into the engine room, with all 20 Indian crew surviving). The strikes underscore how maritime workers from the Global South are bearing the human cost of the US war on Iran.

Key facts:
- India's Shipping Minister Sarbananda Sonowal called the deaths "deeply unfortunate"; New Delhi demanded "immediate de-escalation"
- India described the incidents as "a direct result of the ongoing conflict in the region"
- Indian nationals crew a large share of the world's merchant fleet, placing them disproportionately in the blockade's line of fire

### 3. World Bank slashes global growth forecast to 2.5%, blames Iran war

The World Bank cut its 2026 global growth forecast to 2.5% — the lowest since the Covid pandemic and down from 2.9% in 2025 — attributing the downgrade directly to the energy shock from the Iran war. The bank warned of a worst-case scenario of just 1.3% growth if energy disruptions worsen and financial markets seize. Developing economies were marked down to 3.6% from 4.4% in 2025, with the Middle East/North Africa/Afghanistan/Pakistan region cut by 2.7 percentage points to just 1.6%.

The forecast lands the cost of the war squarely on workers and the poor, particularly in the worst-hit developing nations where food insecurity exceeds 61%. World Bank chief economist Indermit Gill cited slower population growth, falling private and public investment, rising debt and slower trade.

Key facts:
- Brent crude is projected to average $94/barrel in 2026 — 36% higher than 2025 and 50% above January 2026 projections
- Headline global inflation is running at roughly 4%
- President Ajay Banga said the bank made $60 billion immediately available to developing nations, with potential for $100 billion over 15 months: "We are providing liquidity where it is needed now"

### 5. Sudan's civil war enters fourth year with 33.7 million needing aid

Sudan's civil war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces entered its fourth year with an estimated 33.7 million people — roughly two-thirds of the population — requiring humanitarian assistance in 2026. - 61.7% of the population is acutely food insecure; families report one meal per day or less, with some eating animal feed or boiling leaves
- Over one-third of health facilities are non-functional; the health system is on the edge of collapse

### 7. Supreme Court blocks Alabama nitrogen gas execution

The US Supreme Court ruled Thursday that Alabama cannot execute Jeffrey Lee using nitrogen hypoxia gas, upholding a lower court order that the method violates the Eighth Amendment's ban on cruel and unusual punishment. Lee, convicted of a 1998 double murder during a pawnshop robbery, argued that nitrogen gas causes prolonged, painful suffocation and proposed a firing squad as an alternative. He had been scheduled to die July 11.

Key facts:
- Justices Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito and Neil Gorsuch would have allowed the execution but issued no written dissent
- Alabama has carried out eight nitrogen executions since the court approved the method in 2024 — seven of them in Alabama
- Lee's case — a poor defendant convicted of a robbery-murder — is emblematic of the class and racial dimensions of who faces execution in the US

### Chilean students protest education cuts and criminalization of youth

Thousands of students, teachers and activists clashed with police across Chile in renewed marches against President José Antonio Kast's education cuts and the criminalization of youth, with the WSWS's Mauricio Saavedra documenting how the Broad Front and Communist Party administered the same market-based education system they once opposed before Kast took office. ### Trump SNAP cuts drive millions off food assistance

The "One Big Beautiful Bill Act" is enacting a $187 billion assault on SNAP through 2034 and has already stripped at least 3.5 million people from food assistance between enactment and February 2026, as Kate Randall reported for the WSWS. Average benefits of roughly $6/day ($2/meal) are framed as deliberately inadequate. The measure expanded work requirements from ages 18-54 to 18-64 and narrowed the definition of a dependent child from under-18 to under-14, cutting off parents of teenagers. Before the cuts, 47.9 million Americans lived in food-insecure households; food insecurity affects 24.4% of Black households versus 10.1% of white households. The USDA terminated its annual Household Food Security Report in September 2025, eliminating the data infrastructure needed to track the damage.

## Science, technology, and public health

- [US measles cases hit 2,030 across 40 jurisdictions](#science-section) — worst year in decades as kindergarten MMR coverage falls to 92.5%
- [H5N1 spreads in poultry across 12 states](#science-section) — over 100 million birds culled cumulatively; 71 US human cases since 2024
- [Three journals simultaneously expand finerenone to non-diabetic kidney disease](#science-section) — NEJM, JAMA and Lancet studies could add millions of eligible patients
- [AI policy clash](#science-section) — Anthropic's CEO demands government blocking authority days after a deregulatory White House order

**Measles and H5N1.** The CDC confirmed 2,030 measles cases this year across 40 jurisdictions as of June 4, with 30 active outbreaks accounting for ~93% of cases — putting the US on pace for its worst measles year in decades. Kindergarten MMR coverage has fallen to 92.5% for the 2024-25 school year, below the 95% herd-immunity threshold. Separately, the USDA confirmed active H5N1 detections in commercial poultry across 12 states, triggering depopulations that have surpassed 100 million birds cumulatively this cycle. The CDC reports 71 total US human cases since February 2024; NIOSH has relaunched its dairy-worker safety campaign targeting roughly 500,000 agricultural workers at elevated occupational risk.

**Finerenone studies.** Three studies published simultaneously the week of June 5 in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, *JAMA* and *The Lancet* — presented at the 2026 European Renal Association Congress in Glasgow — show that finerenone, a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist previously approved only for diabetic chronic kidney disease, significantly protects the hearts and kidneys of patients with non-diabetic CKD, including glomerular disease. It is the most concentrated simultaneous release of finerenone evidence to date and could expand the eligible patient population by millions globally.

Roundup:
- **AI regulation clash:** In a June 10 essay, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei argued voluntary transparency is no longer sufficient and that governments should have standing legal authority — with revenue-based civil penalties — to block AI deployments that fail mandatory third-party safety testing. The call followed scrutiny of Anthropic's "Mythos" model, initially found capable of exploiting critical software vulnerabilities.
- **White House AI order:** A June executive order requires CISA to issue critical-infrastructure cybersecurity guidance within 30 days and directs Treasury, NSA and CISA to build a voluntary framework within 60 days to classify "covered frontier models" — but explicitly prohibits any mandatory government pre-clearance or licensing for AI releases, setting up a direct tension with Amodei's proposal.
- **EU AI Act:** On June 10 the European Commission published its Code of Practice for marking and labeling AI-generated content, ahead of binding enforcement in August 2026.
- **COVID-19:** Transmission is declining or likely declining in 41 of 50 states. Ensemble models forecast ~1,000 new hospital admissions for the week ending June 13; COVID ED visits are at ~0.087% of all visits, down from 0.11% the prior week — a low summer baseline with no surge underway.

## Arts and culture

The most significant cultural news of the day was the loss, confirmed earlier this month, of **Marjane Satrapi**, the Iranian-French cartoonist and filmmaker, who died June 4 at age 56. Satrapi won worldwide acclaim for *Persepolis*, her graphic-novel bildungsroman of growing up during and after the 1979 Iranian Revolution and the Iran-Iraq war, later adapted into an Oscar-nominated 2007 animated film. Her death lands with particular weight as the US-Israel war devastates the Iranian population: *Persepolis* remains one of the most humane and widely read accounts of how ordinary Iranians lived through revolution, theocratic repression and imperialist-fueled conflict — a corrective to the dehumanizing war propaganda now saturating Western media. Satrapi's work insisted on the universality of the Iranian people's experience against both the clerical regime and Western militarism.

In literature, novelist **Dave Eggers** discussed his new novel *Contrapposto*, a project he says has been on his mind for nearly two decades. Eggers's fiction has long engaged questions of surveillance, technology and the alienating structures of corporate capitalism (*The Circle*), and the new work continues his interest in the social pressures bearing on the individual.

Roundup:
- **Documentary:** *American Pachuco: The Legend of Luiz Valdez*, David Alvarado's film on the Chicano theater pioneer, screened June 11 as part of a Latino Arts Festival collaboration after winning the 2026 Sundance Audience Award for US Documentary. Luis Valdez's El Teatro Campesino emerged directly from the 1965 Delano grape strike and farmworker organizing — a reminder of the historical bond between cultural production and the labor movement.
- **Music obituaries:** Avant-garde guitarist **James Blood Ulmer** died June 3 at 86; his "harmolodic" fusion of jazz, funk and blues, developed in collaboration with Ornette Coleman, was among the most original American musical voices of the late 20th century. R&B vocalist **Peabo Bryson**, a two-time Grammy winner, died June 2 at 75.
- **Stage and screen:** Actor **Anthony Head**, known to a generation for *Buffy the Vampire Slayer* and *Ted Lasso*, died June 1 at 72. British character actor **Patrick Godfrey** died June 4 at 93.
- **History:** **Gordon S. Wood**, the influential historian of the American Revolution, died June 7 at 92 after being struck by a car; his scholarship reshaped understanding of the social forces behind the founding of the United States.

The concentration of deaths of older artists and intellectuals is a reminder of a generation formed in the postwar decades now passing — figures whose work, at its best, treated art as a means of grappling with history and society rather than as disposable content.
