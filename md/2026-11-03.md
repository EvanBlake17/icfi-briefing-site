### 1. Americans vote in midterms with a record 57 million ballots already cast

Polls open today for all 435 House seats and 35 of the 100 Senate seats. The vote is a referendum on an 80-year-old president whose approval stood at **32%** in a poll published last week. The University of Florida's Election Lab counted a record **57 million** early votes by Monday afternoon. That is well above the 45 million cast in the entire 2022 midterm, and it reflects mass anger over the Iran war, prices and the administration's attacks on democratic rights. Republicans hold the Senate 53–47.

Trump closed with rallies in Iowa and Kansas on Sunday. The White House says he held events in 20 states over the past 32 days. Reuters reports that his last major pre-vote speech dwelt for more than 20 minutes on renovations to the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. On Fox's *Hannity*, he told Republicans to vote as if he were on the ballot: "If our people vote, we win in a landslide." If Democrats win the Senate, they could block his judicial nominees, including any Supreme Court vacancy.

Key facts:

- **Polling:** Reuters/Ipsos has 46% of registered voters backing Democrats in congressional races and 38% backing Republicans. CNN's Harry Enten gives Democrats a 92% chance of winning the House and 71% of winning the Senate. Reuters calls the Senate too close to call.
- **What both parties left out:** Neither party campaigned on the war with Iran that both expect to resume after the vote. In his UC Berkeley lecture on Monday, published today as the WSWS Perspective, David North called this "the threatened renewed war against Iran," which "has barely figured" in the campaign even though it should dominate the election.
- **The Democrats' role:** North argued the DSA functions as a left wing of the Democratic Party that keeps young people and workers inside it. Its July 2026 program, *Workers Deserve More*, does not mention the Iran war, nuclear weapons or imperialism.

### 2. Federal courts block Justice Department moves against election officials

In a series of rulings in the final 72 hours before the vote, federal courts rebuffed the administration's attempt to use the Justice Department and Homeland Security to interfere with how elections are run and how results are certified.

In California, US District Judge Trina Thompson ordered DOJ and DHS to stop demanding that states segregate ballots, purge voters or halt ballot processing. She also barred threats of criminal prosecution against election officials. The administration's conduct, she wrote, is "likely to have the effect of interfering with election administration." An appeals panel then refused the DOJ's request to set aside about **20,000** mail ballots it claimed were sent to noncitizens, saying any new procedures were a matter for Congress. Reuters reports that the Supreme Court also quickly rejected the administration's bid to temporarily block those ballots from being counted. California Attorney General Rob Bonta called the fraud claims "baseless."

In Alaska, US District Judge Sharon Gleason dismissed a DOJ lawsuit that sought to block certification of the Senate race if Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan narrowly loses to Democrat Mary Peltola. DOJ had argued that a second candidate on the ballot, Daniel J. Sullivan Jr., was confusing voters, and asked for his ballots to be set aside. Gleason wrote that the government "offers no rebuttal to the concerns regarding preservation of the secrecy of voting."

Key facts:

- **FBI in Alaska:** FBI agents questioned a Sullivan Jr. supporter, Barry Bracken, about a $500 donation. Bracken said he gave it deliberately. The administration is pursuing a criminal investigation, and the FBI would not comment.
- **The spoiler claim:** The *New York Times* reported that Peltola's campaign helped recruit Sullivan Jr. Peltola and Sullivan Jr. both deny it. Sen. Sullivan said he would accept the result and called Republicans "the firewall for freedom for America right now."
- **Prosecutions:** Democracy Now reports that the administration has subpoenaed county election workers and charged 28 people under federal election laws. Earlier reports gave a higher national count, and the figures have not been reconciled.
- **What comes next:** The rulings are temporary obstacles, not a resolution. An apparatus built to contest certification, including federal suits, FBI interviews and prosecutions, is in place before a single vote has been counted.

### 3. Trump signals Iran diplomacy as war preparations continue

After a weekend phone call in which Netanyahu pressed him to resume bombing Iran as soon as the polls close, Trump has publicly put diplomacy first. He called the conversation "a very good talk." An Israeli source described it as "tough" and a US source as "direct." Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Washington prefers a peaceful resolution. Markets treated this as a real signal: oil fell sharply on Monday (see World economy).

Tehran says it has given Washington a "firm, rational" response and that the "ball" is now in America's court to prevent war. Defense Minister Mehrdad Akhlaghi Ketabchi said Iran is strengthening its armed forces so they can respond "without geographical limits." Separately, a Saudi-led coalition struck Houthi missile and drone assets in Yemen (see International).

Key facts:

- **Reported target list:** In his Berkeley lecture, North cited *Wall Street Journal* reporting that the planned campaign would hit civilian infrastructure, including factories, refineries and power plants. He compared this to the 1991 destruction of Iraq's electrical grid. Middle East Watch estimated 2,500–3,000 direct deaths then, and far more people died afterward from disease and the lack of clean water.
- **Nuclear rhetoric:** North recalled Trump's April threat that "a whole civilization will die tonight, never to be brought back again." In October Trump said he "wouldn't use nuclear."
- **The WSWS assessment:** North invoked the 1948 Genocide Convention to describe deliberate plans to destroy the infrastructure a civilian population needs to survive.
- **The Democrats:** The leading Democratic critic of the plan, Sen. Chris Murphy, was hospitalized after a stroke on Saturday (see United States). No other prominent Democrat has made the war an election issue.

### 4. German police arrest more than 200 as youth protests against conscription grow

Estimates of the weekend's youth protests in Germany have risen to about **182,000** people in more than 30 cities. Initial counts were about 120,000. The protests were organized by the "Gen Z Regelt" campaign. Demonstrators demanded more money for schools and relief from the cost of living, and opposed the planned return of compulsory military service. Spokesperson Lina Friedrich said the movement is fighting for "peace and freedom."

The state responded with force. Police arrested **more than 200** people after clashes in Hamburg, Frankfurt and Berlin, using water cannons and special units. Anadolu, following police accounts, attributes the violence to "radical groups" who put up barricades and threw stones, bottles and firecrackers. It also notes that the marches as a whole were largely peaceful.

In France, the third straight weekend of cost-of-living protests drew about 320,000 people, including high school students, trade unionists and yellow-vest activists. Authorities reported **565 arrests** and five security officers injured in Paris. Students plan further school blockades before the union-led national strike on **Thursday, November 5**. Saint-Denis Mayor Bally Bagayoko said students "have the right to demand true equality."

Key facts:

- **The IYSSE's line:** IYSSE speakers addressed rallies in Berlin, Erfurt, Stuttgart and Nuremberg. They argued that "Anyone fighting against social cuts must also fight against war—and vice versa," and that "Capitalism has nothing left to offer but war, fascism and misery." In Stuttgart, Florian Hasek called the current program "the largest military buildup since the Second World War."
- **The Left Party:** Speakers pointed out that Die Linke backs social cuts and voted for trillion-euro war credits in the Bundesrat, so the youth movement has no parliamentary representative.
- **Repression in Nuremberg:** Joshua Seubert described heavy police preparations and a Young Union (CDU youth) call for the Bundeswehr to be involved.

### 6. Zelenskyy claims record Russian losses as Kremlin signals openness to talks

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy claimed that Russia lost **54,400** troops killed or seriously wounded in October, the most in any month of the war. According to Anadolu, the Oleshky truce mediated by Melania Trump ended late Sunday with no violations reported.

- **Moscow threat:** The FSB detained a minor after the US Embassy in Moscow warned of a "credible threat" of a mass-casualty event at the Tsaritsyno Museum-Reserve.
- **Sanctions leak:** A Russian deputy prime minister said Russian diesel sometimes reaches Europe under other countries' labels despite the EU import ban.
- **Context:** Neither side has stopped fighting while the talk of negotiations continues. A record loss claim helps Kyiv argue for more Western arms. Bulgaria has just elected a president who campaigned against sending them (see International).

### Yotova wins Bulgarian presidency by a wide margin

Acting President Iliana Yotova won Sunday's runoff by a wider margin than the exit polls had suggested. Parallel counts by five polling agencies gave her **56.6–58.2%**, against **38.6–40.6%** for Andrey Gyurov, a banker backed by the pro-Western opposition. - Yotova campaigned against sending more arms to Ukraine and for a negotiated peace, while supporting continued EU and NATO membership.
- The result is another sign of mass hostility in Eastern Europe to the NATO war in Ukraine. Bulgaria's ruling parties channel that hostility toward nationalist figures who stay inside the alliance.

### August Wilson's "Seven Guitars" returns

Lincoln Center Theater begins previews of August Wilson's *Seven Guitars* on November 5. The play is part of Wilson's ten-play cycle on Black life in 20th-century Pittsburgh. It is set in the Hill District in 1948 and follows a blues guitarist who is trying to escape poverty and police harassment for a recording career in Chicago. Wilson is one of the few major American dramatists who placed the lives of Black workers within the history of migration, wage labor and the broken promises of the postwar period. A revival during today's social crisis is significant. The new Broadway musical *Wanted* opened Sunday at the James Earl Jones Theatre.

### Jacobin and DSA

- <a href="https://jacobin.com/2026/11/state-elections-capital-unions-mamdani" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><strong>"Winning Office Is Not the Same as Winning Power"</strong></a> (Vivek Chibber, Nov 2). Chibber argues that electoral gains will not change the balance between labor and capital without strong unions, because capital can retaliate through investment strikes and the bond market. On the Democrats, his position is in two stages. In the short run the left must work through the party's ballot line, and in the long run it needs its own party. He admits that "The Democratic Party will never do that because it will never help you build working-class organizations," and yet still tells the left to work inside it. He correctly diagnoses capital's leverage over the state and then concludes that the left should keep building inside a party of capital. The workers' party is postponed indefinitely.
- <a href="https://jacobin.com/2026/11/midterms-us-politics-elections-reform" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><strong>"The US Electorate Is Permanently Mobilized and Unable to Move"</strong></a> (Corey Robin, Nov 2). Robin describes the electorate's "stuckness" and recommends Weber's "slow boring of hard boards," meaning patient institution-building through elections or "non-reformist reforms." He treats the dead end of bourgeois politics as a problem of organizational patience. He never mentions the war that will follow the vote, and he never mentions the working class as an independent social force.
- <a href="https://jacobin.com/2026/11/patrick-stewart-socialism-picard-sci-fi" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><strong>"Captain Picard Was a Socialist, and So Was Patrick Stewart"</strong></a> (Alexander Deley, Nov 2). A sympathetic tribute that traces Stewart's politics to his childhood poverty and to what his mother endured at work. "Socialism" here means moral fairness, without any connection to a program.

**Assessment:** On the day of the vote, Jacobin is discussing tactics for working inside the Democratic Party. In North's lecture (WSWS coverage), the DSA program it promotes leaves out the Iran war entirely. Jacobin's output today confirms that.

### Left Voice
