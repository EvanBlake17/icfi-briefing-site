### 1. Hot US inflation report fuels rate-hike fears three weeks before midterms

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said Wednesday that consumer prices rose 0.6 percent in September and 3.7 percent over the year, up from 3.4 percent in August and above the 3.6 percent consensus. Gasoline rose 9.1 percent in the month and made up more than half the increase. The report revived expectations that the Federal Reserve will raise rates again. It also set off a tech sell-off on Wall Street that spread to Asia overnight (see World economy).

The political effects are direct. The midterms are November 3, and a Reuters/Ipsos poll finds about 78 percent of Americans blame Trump's policies for rising prices. In the WSWS Perspective, Patrick Martin argues that workers "pay for the war twice": at the pump, and through interest payments and social cuts that send money to bondholders.

Key facts:
- **Energy:** Gasoline is up 34.2 percent year on year, energy overall 20.4 percent and fuel oil 74.2 percent. The fuel oil rise hits Northeastern households just as the heating season starts (BLS via WSWS).
- **Pump prices (AAA):** Gasoline averages $4.33 a gallon and diesel $6.29. According to GasBuddy's Patrick DeHaan, 2026 has already had 158 days with average gas above $4, more than any previous year.
- **Real wages:** Down 0.5 percent in one month and 0.7 percent over the year.
- **Other components:** Shelter rose 0.4 percent in the month. Food was flat overall. Computer software and accessories are up 28 percent over the year, which Parabellum's Rami Cassis attributes to the AI boom.
- **The Fed:** It raised rates by 25 basis points in September to 3.75–4.00 percent, its first hike in three years. CME FedWatch puts the odds of holding at 52.9 percent and of another quarter-point hike at 47.1 percent.
- **Household strain (WSWS Perspective):** The 30-year mortgage rate is 7.4 percent, up from 6.3 percent. Credit card rates average 22 percent. Household debt totals $18.8 trillion.
- **War cost:** Moody's Analytics estimates the Iran war has cost the average household $2,000. Its chief economist said: "No wonder the war is so unpopular."
- **Coming data:** Retail sales and producer prices are due today.

### 2. Trump weighs direct US entry into Saudi war on Yemen as ships sink near Hormuz

The Saudi–Houthi war has become a front of the wider US–Israeli war on Iran, and Washington is now openly considering joining it. Asked on Saturday whether the US would join Saudi strikes on the Houthis, Trump said "We may. We're gonna look at it." On Tuesday he declined to say. Secretary of State Rubio said on October 5 that Washington would honor its defense agreement with Riyadh. According to NBC, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman urged Trump in September to strike the Houthis.

Fighting around the Gulf grew in the past 24 hours. Al Jazeera reports that two vessels sank near the Strait of Hormuz, one hit by a projectile and the other by a sea mine. As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, US intelligence, targeting support and arms sales already make Washington "a direct participant" in Saudi operations, as it has been under three administrations.

### 4. Administration escalates pre-election offensive with Omar citizenship probe and California ballot ultimatum

Seizing a sitting lawmaker's phone would normally also trigger the Constitution's "speech or debate" protections.

On Tuesday, Acting Deputy Attorney General R. Trent McCotter gave California Secretary of State Shirley Weber and Los Angeles County Registrar Dean Logan 48 hours to review a federal list of "likely noncitizens." He demanded that their mailed ballots be "segregated" and not counted, and threatened "emergency relief" if the state refused. As Barry Grey wrote on the WSWS, these measures are preparation for disputing or overturning an election the administration expects to lose. A Reuters/Ipsos poll puts Trump's approval at a record low of 32 percent.

Key facts:
- **The Omar case:** Investigators are examining whether Omar or her late father changed her age on immigration papers so that she was recorded as 17, not 18. HSI looked at a similar allegation under Biden and dropped it for lack of evidence. A criminal case would face a five-year statute of limitations, and denaturalization would require court proceedings.

She wrote that the program "has removed United States citizens from the voter rolls." Reuters reports that the DOJ has lost 26 of its voter-roll lawsuits against states and won none.
- **Election Day:** The DOJ plans to send 1,000 staff to polling places, compared with 714 in 2024 and 289 in 2022. The NAACP has sued for records on the plan.

### 5. China opens Laos air-power base as North Korea tests submarine-launched cruise missiles

According to an AP analysis of satellite imagery, China's new Ban Keun airfield, about 30 miles north of Vientiane near the Thai border, is its first overseas military facility built around air power. The site is run jointly with Laos and was opened at a September ceremony, though construction is still going on. It has a runway of about 6,560 feet, a parallel taxiway and an apron for about two dozen aircraft. There is room to extend the runway by at least 3,280 feet, enough to take bombers. Laos, a country of 8 million, is deeply in debt to China for a railway and other infrastructure.

North Korean state media said the military fired two kinds of "strategic" cruise missiles designed for submarine launch on Wednesday in its eastern waters. It did not say whether they were fired from a submarine or a test platform. The launches follow hypersonic missile tests on September 20 and October 3.

Key facts:
- **Beijing's position:** The Chinese Defense Ministry says the facility will train Lao pilots and is "not directed against any third party."
- **Strategic role:** The Institute for the Study of War says the base "would give the PLA alternate routes to access disputed areas of the South China Sea." The IISS's Evan Laksmana asked: "Does this mean the U.S. would have to attack a joint facility in Southeast Asia?"
- **Thailand:** Its Defense Ministry does not see a threat but will keep monitoring the site. Vietnam, Cambodia and Myanmar have not commented.
- **North Korea:** Kim Jong Un calls South Korea a "permanent enemy" and has rejected Trump's calls to restart talks. Submarine-launched missiles are harder for neighbors to detect in advance.

### 6. UN warns of Asian and African food crisis as Hormuz fertiliser plan stalls

UN Under-Secretary-General Jorge Moreira da Silva told Reuters at the IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok that it is "absolutely critical" for Washington and Tehran to approve a UN fertiliser corridor through the Strait of Hormuz within weeks. If planting seasons are missed, he said, "the productivity of agriculture would significantly drop" and food prices would rise across parts of Asia and Africa. The plan was drawn up in March and could be running within 7–10 days of approval. It has been held up by the wider US–Iran deadlock, since Washington insists on addressing Iran's nuclear program first.

Key facts:
- **Before the war:** About one-third of global urea shipments and nearly half of seaborne sulphur went through the strait. Urea is the world's most widely used nitrogen fertiliser.
- **The mechanism:** A UN-staffed center in Oman would register vessels, verify cargo at Gulf ports and track ships through the strait. Five vessels a day would be enough to keep supplies moving to the countries most in need.
- **Warnings:** The FAO warned in May that fertiliser shortages could tighten food supplies from late 2026 into 2027. Farmers in South Asia are already struggling with costs, and Spanish farmers protested rising production costs on Wednesday.
- **Background:** The June interim US–Iran ceasefire collapsed, and the two sides now exchange proposals only through intermediaries.

### Faber pulls Sally Rooney's books from UK sale over Palestine Action support

Faber has confirmed it will stop supplying Sally Rooney's novels in the United Kingdom in any format. Rooney, 35, is the author of *Normal People* and *Intermezzo*. Faber says Britain's ban on Palestine Action means it "cannot pass on U.K. earnings to Sally Rooney for her work." In August 2025 Rooney wrote in the *Irish Times*, "I too support Palestine Action," and said she would give income from her novels and TV adaptations to the group. She says the withdrawal was made "with my full agreement" and that she is "wholly prepared to accept the consequences."

Rooney is one of the most widely read novelists writing in English today. Her work has taken the class structure of contemporary Irish society and the emotional life of precarious young people seriously, and its disappearance from British bookshops is a significant act of cultural suppression. The mechanism matters. No court has banned her books. Instead, terrorism legislation aimed at a protest group has made it legally dangerous for a publisher to pay one of its leading authors. More than 3,000 people have been arrested and over 1,200 charged under the Terrorism Act since the Labour government's ban, without a single conviction so far. Palestine Action's lawyers go to the UK Supreme Court next month. The case shows how "counterterrorism" powers brought in to suppress opposition to the Gaza genocide now reach into literature itself.

### IMF report details a financial system with rising risks and vulnerabilities — Nick Beams

The report's main figures are in the World economy section. Beams adds the IMF's analysis of "circular financing," in which AI firms invest in their own customers. This creates "wrong-way risk," because distress at one key company hits both sales and the value of the investment at once. An IMF review of 14 such deals found about $300 billion in equity and $650 billion in debt committed since 2023, against expected future revenue of $1.4 trillion. Beams doubts that revenue will ever arrive. He points to the March 2020 Treasury "dash for cash" and the October 2022 UK gilt crisis as precedents for the forced sales the IMF now fears from pension funds and insurers. The report treats the next crisis as a given and argues only over how to manage it. Beams notes that it never mentions Trump administration policy, and calls "AI-led correction" a euphemism for the bursting of a speculative bubble. Read with his October 14 article on sovereign debt, it raises the question of who will pay for the next bailout.

### New Zealand university and polytechnic staff vote to strike — John Braddock

About 9,500 lecturers, tutors, librarians and other staff at six universities and 11 polytechnics have voted to strike in October. It is the first time the two sectors have voted together. They are being offered raises below inflation of 4.1 percent. The Tertiary Education Union first sought 6 percent but left that figure out of its October 2 strike-vote statement. Half of all tertiary staff are not permanent. Polytechnic employers have refused to negotiate a multi-employer agreement since February, and the TEU has taken the dispute to the Employment Relations Authority. Braddock calls this a step toward a sellout. Background: real salaries fell 10–17 percent at Otago, Canterbury and Auckland between 2007 and 2021, and in 2025 the unions accepted 2.5 percent settlements after the 100,000-strong "mega strike." About 8,700 public sector jobs are being cut. TEU secretary Amy Ross is calling for "a comprehensive long term investment plan," which Braddock describes as a channel for votes to Labour in the November 7 election, with both major parties polling under 30 percent. The article criticizes the pseudo-left International Socialist Organisation, whose member Dougal McNeill, a TEU branch president, called a 2023 Labour funding boost "a first step." The Socialist Equality Group calls for rank-and-file committees independent of the unions.
