### 1. US prepares new strikes on Iran as tankers burn in Hormuz

On the eve of the US midterm elections, Washington and Tel Aviv are openly discussing when to resume the war on Iran. Axios reports that Netanyahu wants strikes immediately after Tuesday's vote, while Trump prefers to let Qatar-mediated talks run a little longer. The two spoke by phone Saturday. MS NOW, citing three senior Gulf Arab officials, reports that the administration told Gulf governments to prepare for strikes as early as November 4. The *Wall Street Journal* reported a possible large attack on nuclear sites and infrastructure.

The Strait of Hormuz remains the flashpoint. On Monday the IRGC said two crude tankers caught fire after hitting mines in the strait. At least four tankers were reportedly hit on Friday and Saturday, and Gulf states condemned an attack on a Qatari LNG carrier. Iran attacks vessels that refuse to coordinate with its "Persian Gulf Strait Authority." Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said talks on a two-day plan to reopen the strait are under way and that he hopes the US has "enough sense to choose diplomacy." As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, the plan to bomb "the day after" the vote shows the war's timing is set by the US political calendar, with the Democrats keeping silent.

Key facts:
- **Trump:** "We still have to take care of, I would say one or two sites, but we can do that in 10 minutes." He said the war will end "very soon, one way or the other," that Iran will either make a deal or "we'll just end it," and, at a Halloween party, "The only reason why I'm not finished with Iran is I want to be nice." At an Iowa rally he spoke of giving Tehran "the hammer."
- **Iran's posture:** Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei called for "maximum deterrence" and an immediate response "without geographical restriction." IRGC spokesperson Hossein Mohebbi said: "In a conflict with America, there are no geographical limitations." Army spokesperson Mohammad Akraminia said a new war "could last longer than the previous conflict." Tehran has also warned that seabed cables and regional infrastructure could be targeted.
- **Iran's conditions:** Security chief Mohsen Rezaei listed what Tehran wants before normal transit resumes: an end to the US naval blockade, oil sanctions waivers, unfrozen assets, a halt to fighting on all fronts including Lebanon, and recognition of an Iran-Oman arrangement for Hormuz. He called a letter from Vice President JD Vance a "positive" change in tone.
- **US military buildup:** The USS *Theodore Roosevelt*, carrying about 9,000 Marines and sailors, is due in late November as the third carrier in the region. More Patriot batteries have reportedly been sent to Qatar and Saudi Arabia.
- **Economic siege:** A US Treasury report on "Operation Economic Outcast," publicized by Axios, found no new oil loaded at Iran's terminals since August 25. It put Iranian inflation near 90% year on year in September and described the economy as "crippled" but not collapsing.
- **Civil defense:** Iranian officials have identified 14 electricity-dependent sectors that would need support if the war resumes, and they are weighing "network islanding" of the power grid.
- **Analysts:** "Each side wants the other to act first" (Negar Mortazavi, Center for International Policy). Tehran aims to make another attack "so costly and unpredictable" that Washington reconsiders (Hamidreza Azizi, International Crisis Group).

### 2. Mass youth revolt spreads across Germany and France

The youth protest movement that began in French high schools in late September is now a Europe-wide phenomenon. In Germany on Saturday, October 31, the organizer "GenZ regelt!" ("Gen Z has got this!") counted about 120,000 marchers in more than 30 cities. Their demands were education funding, relief from the cost of living, and opposition to soaring military spending and a new military service model that many fear will lead to conscription. Signs read "Time for resistance" and "Merz must go." The daytime marches were largely peaceful, but police used water cannon and pepper spray in Frankfurt, Hamburg and Cologne that evening. A police union official said officers "intervened very decisively and also forcefully." According to the WSWS, Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt blamed "violent left-wing groups" and people from migrant backgrounds for the evening clashes.

In France, the Interior Ministry counted about 320,000 protesters on Saturday, including 70,000 in Paris. Organizers counted nearly half a million. It was the third consecutive weekend of mass protest, after 550,000 on October 17 and 410,000 on October 24 by official counts. The student group "Lycéens!" says high school blockades restart today, Monday, as classes resume after the two-week holiday, and will intensify from Tuesday. Macron's concessions include a new university admissions platform, loans for school renovations, and smaller class sizes for 10th grade only. Lycéens! head Pierre Sourzat called them "smoke and mirrors." In a Perspective on the WSWS, Johannes Stern argued that the German protests are part of an international movement "on a collision course with capitalism." He called for rank-and-file committees and a Europe-wide general strike.

Key facts:
- **France, economic stakes:** The Chamber of Commerce puts retailers' losses from three weeks of unrest above €1 billion. Fitch has revised its outlook on French debt to negative, public debt is near 120% of GDP, and the economy shrank in the third quarter.
- **France, repression:** A court upheld a ban on the traditional Nation–Bastille march route. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez warned of firm responses to Halloween-night unrest, and the government is preparing measures to make protesters pay for riot damage.
- **France, the unions:** The union leaderships met Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu on October 21 and pushed national cross-sector action back to November 5.
- **Germany, voices:** Marius, a 23-year-old care worker: "the situation is slowly reaching boiling point… this just isn't working anymore." Milla, 20: it is "really important to show solidarity with all the other protests currently happening across Europe."
- **Politics:** Marine Le Pen leads polls for April's French presidential election and has gained from the unrest. The German organizers explicitly distanced themselves from "extremist groups… no matter from which side."
- **Next dates:** Blockades of French universities and high schools are set for Tuesday and Thursday, cross-sector strikes in France for November 5, and international demonstrations for November 20.

### 4. AI bubble shows cracks as Intel suffers record collapse

Last week's earnings season produced the sharpest test yet of the AI investment boom. Intel beat on third-quarter earnings per share but guided fourth-quarter results far below estimates, citing an explosion at its fab in Israel. The stock fell about 29% on Friday, its worst day on record, wiping out about $140 billion. Alphabet fell 13% on Thursday, erasing about $500 billion in market value, after Google Services revenue missed estimates ($96.3 billion against $98.3 billion expected) and search growth slowed to 12%. Meta reported its first negative free cash flow since 2012. Amazon's AWS revenue rose 39% to $46 billion, but quarterly capex reached $75.9 billion and fourth-quarter sales guidance missed consensus. Nick Beams wrote on the WSWS that the hyperscalers' spending, which continues despite uncertain returns, is a structural compulsion and a sign of the boom's fragility.

Key facts:
- **Capital spending:** The four hyperscalers (Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Alphabet) spent $207 billion on capex in Q3, up from $113 billion a year earlier. They plan $232 billion more in Q4, for roughly $740 billion in 2026.
- **Google's cash position:** AI spending topped $50 billion in Q3 and is projected at about $71 billion this quarter. Free cash flow fell to minus $9.1 billion.
- **Breadth of the selloff:** Nvidia fell 4.2% on Friday and the SOXX semiconductor ETF fell more than 8%. In Asia, SoftBank, a major OpenAI investor, is down 48% from its June peak, and Kioxia fell nearly 16% Monday on a cautious outlook.
- **Software:** Twilio, ServiceNow, Roblox and Reddit all guided Q4 below expectations.
- **Torsten Slok (Apollo):** AI has "delivered zero financial payoff to corporate America outside of tech," and the story "will be decided in the S&P 493, not the Mag 7."

### 5. Bangladesh garment workers strike across 500 factories

The garment strike in Bangladesh reached its third day Monday. Thousands of workers blocked highways and clashed with police in Dhaka's Mirpur industrial area, and the walkouts spread to Savar, Ashulia, Jirani and Gazipur. Industry representatives said workers left at least 529 factories on Sunday, and about 500 were temporarily shut. Workers demand a monthly minimum of 30,000 taka ($242). The current minimum is 12,500 taka ($101), and the Bangladesh Center for Workers Solidarity says typical pay is about 13,000 taka. Police used tear gas and sound grenades. Home Minister Salahuddin Ahmed ordered law enforcement to "crack down on those involved in violence."

The upsurge appears to have broken out largely outside union control. Kamrul Hasan of the Ekota Garments Sramik Federation said some labor leaders had gone into hiding and admitted, "We were not prepared for this unrest either." The government of Prime Minister Tarique Rahman announced a wage board on Sunday and held an emergency cabinet meeting.

Key facts:
- **Employers' threat:** Mahmud Hasan Khan, president of the Bangladesh Garment Manufacturers and Exporters Association (BGMEA), said owners would invoke "no work, no pay" if protests continued through Monday.
- **Industry:** Bangladesh is the world's second-largest garment exporter after China, in a sector worth about $38 billion. Owners blame energy costs linked to the US-Israel war on Iran and slowing export demand.
- **Babul Akhter, Bangladesh Garment and Industrial Workers Federation:** "They cannot build a business on the backs of underpaid workers."
- **Gazipur:** Hundreds blocked the Dhaka–Tangail highway north of the capital.

### 6. Kherson truce ends as Russia launches 433 drones

The six-day localized ceasefire around Russian-occupied Oleshky, the longest truce of the war, expired at 10:59 p.m. Sunday Kyiv time. A Ukrainian official told AFP that Russia rejected Kyiv's proposal to extend it. The truce was mediated by First Lady Melania Trump with involvement from Pope Leo XIV. It allowed a rare joint Kyiv-Moscow evacuation from Oleshky, where about 2,000 civilians remained when it began. Melania Trump said nearly 800 people were evacuated. Kherson Governor Oleksandr Prokudin warned residents to avoid "red zones" near the Dnipro.

Fighting elsewhere never stopped. Ukraine's Air Force said Russia launched 433 drones overnight, of which 371 were downed or suppressed. Zelensky said Sunday's strikes cut power to hundreds of thousands of households in four regions, as Moscow targets the energy grid ahead of winter. On Monday, both sides accused each other of hacking their national air-raid alert apps. Ukraine's app sent out false warnings.

Key facts:
- **Vinnytsia:** A Russian drone strike on the grounds of a vocational school killed three people, including a 13-year-old girl.
- **Ukrainian strikes on Russia:** Over 100 Ukrainian drones were reported downed over Nizhny Novgorod region, and a fire broke out at the Kstovo oil refinery. Kyiv says it struck a missile-component factory in Rostov region.
- **Dnipro:** A Russian strike on the train station on October 29 killed 15 (per CNN).
- **Kremlin:** Spokesman Dmitry Peskov called the Oleshky operation positive. Moscow nonetheless declined to extend the truce.

### UN Western Sahara mission ends after Russian veto

The UN peacekeeping mission in Western Sahara (MINURSO) ended on November 1 after 35 years, when the Security Council failed to renew its mandate. On Friday, Russia vetoed a US draft that would have endorsed Morocco's sovereignty claim and wound the mission down next year. A rival Russian resolution to extend the mission was then voted down. UN spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric said November 1 "marks the start of the liquidation period."

- **Reactions:** Morocco's palace welcomed the end of an "instrument to internationalize the dispute." The Polisario Front called it "an unacceptable decision." Algeria condemned the "excessive bias" of the US draft.
- **Background:** Morocco annexed the former Spanish colony in 1975, and more than 16,000 people died in the war that followed. The self-determination referendum promised in 1991 never took place. The Polisario left the ceasefire in 2020, and the UN reported low-intensity hostilities last month.
- **Alignment:** Washington, Paris and London back Morocco. Algeria, Russia and South Africa back the Polisario. Algeria and Morocco severed relations in 2021, and analysts warn of an arms race.

### This week in history: November 2–8

**25 years ago — firefighters storm Ground Zero (November 2, 2001).** More than 1,000 off-duty New York firefighters marched on the World Trade Center site against Mayor Giuliani's cut in recovery crews from nearly 200 to 64, at a time when fewer than 90 of the 343 FDNY dead had been recovered. When union officials tried to wind up the rally, the crowd chanted "Shut it down!" and broke through police lines. Ironworkers and equipment operators left their machines to join them. A dozen were arrested, and most serious charges were later dropped. Anger focused on Fire Commissioner Thomas Von Essen, a former union president, over his "suck it up" remark. The episode punctured the official "national unity" of the early war on terror.

**50 years ago — Carter defeats Ford (November 2, 1976).** Carter won 297 electoral votes to 240 in the first presidential election after Watergate, on a turnout of 53.5%, the lowest in nearly three decades. George Meany of the AFL-CIO and Leonard Woodcock of the UAW mobilized the unions behind him. In office, Carter deregulated trucking and airlines, invoked Taft-Hartley against striking coal miners in 1978, appointed Volcker in 1979 and escalated the Cold War under Brzezinski. The Workers League, forerunner of the SEP, stood candidates including Helen Halyard and Jean Brust and noted a "growing radicalization of the working class."

**75 years ago — Britain airlifts 6,000 troops into the Suez Canal Zone (November 2, 1951).** This followed the Wafd government's October 8 abrogation of the 1936 treaty and the British killing of five Egyptians in Ismailia. Most of the 30,000–40,000 Egyptian workers in British camps struck, and railway and dock workers refused to move British supplies. The Wafd sought to contain the strike, and the crisis led to the January 1952 Ismailia massacre, Black Saturday in Cairo and the July 1952 Free Officers' coup.

**100 years ago — Piłsudski's press decrees (November 7, 1926).** Six months after his May coup, Piłsudski imposed fines of up to $1,000 and three months' jail on editors, without court proceedings, for reports that might provoke demonstrations or ridicule officials. The PPS, which had backed the coup, and the KPP, which had initially supported it, protested without mobilizing the working class independently.
