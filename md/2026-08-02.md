### 1. Trump calls off planned strike on Iran after "perimeters of a deal" reached

### 7. Washington declares wildfire emergency as fire crosses into Spokane

Governor Bob Ferguson declared a statewide wildfire emergency Saturday as fires burned more than 312 square miles across Washington. The Old Trails Fire, which ignited around noon August 1 in the Airway Heights area, was driven by gusts of 35 to 45 mph into Riverside State Park and then across the Spokane River into the city itself. It has burned roughly 3,000 acres with about 4,000 structures threatened.

Spokane County raised evacuation status for communities northwest of the city to Level 3 — the most urgent designation, meaning immediate departure. Thousands were evacuated from north Spokane overnight. The Washington National Guard was activated for fire response. The fires are burning under a heat dome that pushed Phoenix to 116°F, conditions consistent with the intensifying fire regime documented across the western United States.

### Burnham government signals defence spending increase

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who succeeded Keir Starmer on June 22, announced £1.1 billion in new defence spending at the Farnborough International Airshow, including £800 million for a new surveillance package. Burnham framed the expenditure in the language of industrial policy, pledging to "reindustrialise the nation" alongside defence businesses and declaring: "We are going to honour our commitments on defence and use that investment to transform people's lives."

Burnham has declined to commit to reaching 3% of GDP on defence by 2030, a retreat from Starmer's stated ambition, while retaining the 3.5%-by-2035 pledge. The formulation — rearmament sold as regional regeneration — is the standard mechanism by which social spending is converted into military spending under a Labour government.

### Pakistan-administered Kashmir under sustained state crackdown

Authorities in Azad Jammu and Kashmir have escalated repression against the mass protest movement since early June. The Jammu Kashmir Joint Awami Action Committee — which organized rallies, road blockades, sit-ins and shutter-down strikes in Muzaffarabad, Rawalakot, Mirpur and Kotli against the twelve legislative seats reserved for refugees resident in Pakistan — has been banned under anti-terrorism legislation and several of its leaders arrested.

Dozens have been killed since June, mostly protesters, ahead of the July 27 Legislative Assembly vote; the UN called on July 17 for impartial investigations. At least 38 Kashmiris remain missing, alongside internet shutdowns and mass arbitrary arrests. The movement is driven by electricity and flour price demands and the denial of political representation — a class movement being criminalized as terrorism.

### Bombing at Moscow restaurant kills three

### Ten climbers including Nirmal Purja killed in Broad Peak avalanche

The deaths again raise the largely unexamined question of the commercialization of high-altitude mountaineering and the Nepali and Pakistani workers on whom the industry depends.

### First Americans lose Medicaid under work requirements

Nebraska became the first state to disenroll people under the Medicaid work requirements enacted in Trump's budget law, removing roughly 200 beneficiaries on August 1 for failure to document compliance. State estimates put eventual losses in Nebraska alone at around 30,000. Most adults aged 19–64 without dependents in the 40 expansion states plus DC must now document at least 80 hours per month of work, job training, education or community service.

Projections indicate between 4.9 and 10.1 million people will lose coverage by 2028, and — critically — that between 19% and 37% of those who *already work* will nonetheless lose coverage, largely because of documentation barriers. Montana began enforcement July 1; Arkansas is running a "soft launch" with no disenrollments until the federal January 1, 2027 deadline; California, New York, Illinois and Washington will implement at the deadline.

### US measles total passes 2,371, elimination status "highly likely" to fall

The CDC confirmed 53 new measles cases in its latest weekly update, bringing the 2026 US total to 2,371 confirmed cases across 45 jurisdictions. That already exceeds the 2,289 cases recorded in all of 2025, itself the highest annual total since 1991. Utah leads with 514 cases. Ninety-three percent of patients are unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status.

CIDRAP analysis concludes the United States — measles-free since 2000 — is now "highly likely" to lose its elimination status when the Pan American Health Organization reviews transmission data this fall. Losing elimination status requires twelve months of continuous transmission of a single chain. This is the direct, measurable consequence of the dismantling of public health infrastructure and the elevation of anti-vaccine ideology to federal policy.

### EU AI Act transparency obligations take effect today

As of today, August 2, 2026, Article 50 of the EU AI Act becomes enforceable. Providers and deployers must disclose AI use in direct human interactions, label AI-generated content, and disclose emotion recognition systems and deepfakes. Penalties reach €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover. The European Commission adopted implementation guidelines on July 20. This is the most consequential near-term AI regulatory deadline anywhere in the world, affecting essentially any enterprise using generative systems to produce public-facing material. Notably, it addresses disclosure rather than the underlying questions of ownership, labor displacement, or military application.

## Arts and culture

**Vincent Pastore, 1946–2026.** The actor was found dead Saturday afternoon at his home in the Bronx, aged 80, apparently of natural causes. Pastore played Salvatore "Big Pussy" Bonpensiero on *The Sopranos*, and his character's arc — an aging, financially squeezed soldier who becomes an FBI informant and is executed by his closest friends — was among the series' most sustained explorations of loyalty, betrayal and the disintegration of a social milieu. Pastore came to acting late, after years running a New Rochelle nightclub, and brought to the role a lived familiarity with the world David Chase was dissecting. *The Sopranos* remains the most serious American television treatment of the decay of postwar working- and middle-class life in the New Jersey suburbs, and Pastore's performance was central to it. His subsequent typecasting in a long series of mob roles is its own comment on how the industry processes talent.

**Sam Neill, 1947–2026.** The WSWS published Richard Phillips's obituary of the New Zealand actor, who died aged 78. Phillips situates Neill as a key figure in the Australian and New Zealand new wave — a body of work including *Sleeping Dogs*, *My Brilliant Career* and later *The Piano* that emerged from a period of state film funding and genuine social questioning in both countries. Neill's international career, from *The Hunt for Red October* to *Jurassic Park* to *Peaky Blinders*, made him a familiar face, but the more substantial achievement lies in the earlier films. Tributes came from Toni Collette ("You legend. You sweetheart."), Karl Urban and Alan Cumming.

**Locarno opens Wednesday.** The 79th Locarno Film Festival runs August 5–15 with 233 films from 69 countries and 103 world premieres, including new work by Hong Sangsoo, Denis Côté, Maria Back, Ann Oren and Basil Da Cunha. The Piazza Grande opener is Fanny Liatard and Jérémy Trouilh's *The Green Eyes*. Locarno remains one of the few major festivals still oriented toward formally serious and non-commercial filmmaking rather than awards-season positioning.

**Venice unveils its 83rd edition.** The festival runs September 2–12 under a jury led by Maggie Gyllenhaal, opening with Danny Boyle's *Ink*. The selection includes new films from Lee Chang-dong, Tsai Ming-liang, Lav Diaz, Werner Herzog, Julia Loktev, Luca Guadagnino, Nanni Moretti, Martin McDonagh, Mariano Llinás, and documentarians Laura Poitras and Robert Greene — an unusually strong concentration of directors whose work engages historical and social material seriously. The weekly compilation covering strikes and disputes across the region, including actions by garment, transport and public sector workers. Useful as a reference for identifying struggles not covered elsewhere in the international press.

## Pseudo-left press review

- **Jacobin / DSA** — apologetics for the Cuban Stalinist bureaucracy's cultural policy, and a Marvel film review, alongside advice to "Mamdani-style mayors" on municipal reform
- **Socialist Worker (SWP UK)** — supports Burnham's Labour government while criticizing its rearmament, treating the crisis as one of policy rather than of the party's class character
- **The Communist (RCP/IMT)** — declares Britain "needs a revolution" under Burnham while orienting its practical work to anti-fascist counter-demonstrations and a polemic against the SWP
- **Socialist Alternative** — calls for a "new party" while continuing to describe Corbyn's formation as the vehicle
- **PSL / Liberation News** — defends Maduro as a persecuted anti-imperialist leader rather than a bourgeois nationalist whose government prepared the ground for the coup
- **Left Voice** — advances "defeat of the United States" as its central slogan without a programme for the mobilization of the American working class

### Jacobin / DSA

**"The Cuban Revolution Was a Revolution in Art and Culture"** (Abel Prieto, August 1) — Jacobin gave its platform to Cuba's former culture minister for a celebration of the revolution's literacy campaign and artistic institutions. Prieto's account is the official one: it presents the Cuban state's cultural apparatus as the flowering of revolutionary creativity, with no mention of the Padilla affair, the Quinquenio Gris, the UMAP camps, or the ongoing detention of artists. The publication of an unchallenged apologia for a Stalinist cultural bureaucracy, at a moment when the Cuban state is presiding over blackouts and mass emigration, expresses Jacobin's characteristic method: dissenting aesthetics, official politics.

**"Mamdani-Style Mayors Should Study Peru's Democracy Experiment"** (Jared Abbott and Stephanie McNulty, July 31) — proposes participatory budgeting on the Peruvian model as a template for left-led US municipal administrations. The programmatic content is the administration of austerity with a consultative veneer: participatory budgeting distributes a fixed and shrinking pool of municipal funds while leaving untouched the bond market's control over city finances.

**"The New Spider-Man Is Pure Fun"** (Eileen Jones) and **"Take a Moment to Feel the Pain of the Rich"** (Danny Dorling, July 31) round out the output. Dorling's piece — a critique of press sympathy for billionaires — is unobjectionable as far as it goes, and goes no further than moral indignation.

*Assessment:* Jacobin's orientation remains the reconciliation of a radical readership to municipal Democratic administration, with Stalinist historiography supplying the revolutionary credentials.

### Socialist Worker (SWP UK)

**"Burnham commits to increase defence spending by billions"** (July 31) reports the Farnborough announcement critically, but the SWP's framing throughout is that Labour under Burnham is making the wrong choices — not that the party is an instrument of British imperialism whose rearmament is its central function. **"What's behind Spanish state's migrant crisis in north Africa?"** (July 31) correctly traces the Ceuta catastrophe to colonial legacy, but locates the solution in anti-racist mobilization rather than the unification of Spanish, Moroccan and migrant workers on a socialist programme. **"We battle cancer but have to fight for our pay: ICR strikers speak out"** covers the Institute of Cancer Research strike sympathetically while directing readers toward the very union leaderships isolating the dispute.

*Assessment:* The SWP's line is pressure on a Labour government it will not call on workers to break from.

### The Communist (RCP / IMT)

**"Another government, same system — Britain needs a revolution!"** (Adam Booth, July 30) is the editorial line: Burnham represents no change, and Britain requires revolution. The abstract call is not accompanied by any concrete programme for breaking workers from the Labour Party, which the RCP continues to treat as terrain. **"Stand Up to Opportunism — Why the SWP gets everything wrong on racism"** (Jack Tye Wilson, July 31) polemicizes against the SWP's anti-racist strategy, but from the standpoint of recruitment competition rather than a class analysis of the far right's social base. **"Britain First sent packing in Norwich thanks to communist-led counter-demo"** (Jo Millett, July 31) presents street confrontations with a marginal far-right group as the decisive form of struggle — substituting physical confrontation for the political struggle to win the workers currently being drawn to the far right.

*Assessment:* Maximalist rhetoric combined with an entirely non-revolutionary practice.

### Socialist Alternative / ISA

**"The Democrats Won't Save Us — The Case For A New Party"** and **"The Tragedy Of Your Party & Lessons For Socialists"** develop the ISA's central perspective: a new mass party of the working class, funded without corporate donations, with recallable officials on a worker's wage. The content is a left-reformist party structure, not a revolutionary programme; the ISA has already tied this perspective to the Corbyn-Sultana formation and its 800,000 signups. **"Middle East War: Capitalism Offers No Way Out"** (July 9) accepts a death toll above 10,000 and 50,000 injured, mainly in Lebanon and Iran, but concludes with appeals for a mass anti-war movement rather than the ICFI's perspective of the international unification of the working class against war.

### PSL / Liberation News

**"PSL Statement: Release President Nicolas Maduro and Cilia Flores Now!"** (August 1) correctly denounces the abduction of a head of state, but frames Maduro as an anti-imperialist leader to be defended politically rather than as a bourgeois nationalist whose subordination of the Venezuelan working class to the state and the military created the conditions for the coup. **"Con Edison hikes energy prices"** (July 30) documents a three-year rate increase approved by the Public Service Commission, with the demand directed at state regulators rather than at public ownership under workers' control.

### Left Voice

Left Voice continues to advance "For the Defeat of the United States and Israel" as its central slogan on the Iran war, most recently in a speech from a German socialist summer camp. The formulation substitutes a military outcome for a class programme, and leaves entirely unaddressed how the American working class — the only force capable of stopping the war from within — is to be mobilized. Its July 24 statement defending The People's Forum and BreakThrough News against Trump administration attacks is a correct defense of democratic rights, though it is silent on the political character of those organizations.

*No tendency reviewed made direct reference to the ICFI or WSWS in the period surveyed.*
