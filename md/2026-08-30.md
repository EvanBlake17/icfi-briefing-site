### 1. Himalayan flood catastrophe: death toll passes 750, nearly 3,000 missing

The confirmed death toll from the glacial collapse and flood that struck the Nepal-Tibet border region on Wednesday has passed 750, with roughly 3,000 people still unaccounted for four days later. Nepal's disaster relief agency reports at least 734 dead in Nepal and 16 in China's Tibet Autonomous Region, with 2,498 missing in Nepal and 261 foreign nationals missing on the Tibetan side. Nepal issued fresh flood alerts on Sunday, and a road collapsed in a new landslide as rescue crews worked.

The disaster began when a large mass detached from a glacier-covered slope near Langtang Lirung and plunged into the valley below, generating a seismic signal registered at magnitude 5.2 by instruments in Germany and by the US Geological Survey. The avalanche dammed a river; when the temporary dam gave way, a torrent of water, boulders and mud swept downstream through settlements, roads and tourist areas on both sides of the border.

Key facts:

- Roughly 933 workers remain unaccounted for at hydropower construction sites in Rasuwa and Nuwakot districts. At the Upper Trishuli-1 site, 254 workers were rescued. A site executive told reporters: "The tunnel is filled with mud and it is impossible to enter."
- A new barrier lake formed near the confluence of the Tsogyal and Purupqiangzangbu rivers holds roughly 3 million cubic meters of water, with as much again projected to accumulate. Chinese water authorities initially described the landslide dam as at "high risk of breaching," suspending rescue work before permitting it to resume.
- Nepal has mobilized 19,000 security personnel and 16 helicopters. The Red Cross estimates at least 93,000 people are "in great need."
- Foreign nationals are heavily represented among the missing: at least 33 Britons and at least 41 Australians, including a Himalayan tour group with two boys aged 11 and 14.
- Just over $20 million in international humanitarian aid has been pledged. Australia's Labor government pledged $5 million — $3 million for emergency response, $2 million for food relief.

Writing on the WSWS, Kevin Reed argued the catastrophe "is inextricably linked to accelerating global warming and inadequate public preparation combined with decades of government neglect internationally."

### 2. Six months into the US-Israel war on Iran, Hormuz remains shut

The US-Israeli war on Iran, launched with mass bombardment on February 28, has now run six months with no stated endpoint, a collapsed 60-day ceasefire framework and no public accounting of casualties. The Strait of Hormuz remains under Iran's self-declared "Persian Gulf Strait Authority," and Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said Tehran is "in no hurry" to reopen it.

On Sunday, Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei publicly appealed to Gulf rulers to break with Washington, urging the "rulers of Islamic countries" to "recognise their real enemy, understand its plans and confront it." The appeal came as regional mediators — principally Oman and Qatar — press for renewed talks that Washington has so far treated as an adjunct to escalating economic pressure.

Key facts:

- Very large crude carrier transits through Hormuz have fallen to 2-3 per day, down from roughly 8 per day before the war.
- US Central Command says it has redirected 82 vessels away from Iranian ports. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has opened a new sanctions campaign that administration officials have privately described as an "economic D-day."
- Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei warned that Iran would retaliate "in a seismic manner" against countries that join the US economic pressure campaign.
- Israel said on Sunday that Netanyahu's son was "urgently evacuated" from the United States after an unspecified threat.
- The war's disruption of crude supply is the backdrop to both the Venezuela oil deal and the persistent inflation now driving Federal Reserve policy.

- Ukrainian deep strikes have escalated in parallel: roughly 600 drones over the Moscow region overnight on August 15-16, and attacks that set fire to the Lukoil refinery at Kstovo, 500 miles inside Russia, which produces up to 11 percent of Russian gasoline.

### 4. CIA director's secret Moscow trip and the push for a Trump-Putin-Zelensky summit

CIA Director John Ratcliffe made a covert trip to Moscow this week — the first known visit by a CIA director to Russia since before the outbreak of the Ukraine war — meeting SVR chief Sergei Naryshkin and FSB chief Alexander Bortnikov. Axios and Ukrainska Pravda report Ratcliffe proposed a trilateral Trump-Zelensky-Putin summit and delivered "a bleak assessment" of Russia's war position to Bortnikov, urging Moscow to settle before its situation deteriorates further. CNN reported separately that Ratcliffe delivered a "warning to Russia not to stage an attack in NATO territory."

Zelensky was briefed Friday and supports the trilateral proposal; Putin has previously rejected it. Trump downplayed the trip as "standard business" and "semi-routine." Reporting indicates that even some senior White House officials were not informed in advance.

Key facts:

- The trip came amid an intensifying pattern of NATO-power involvement: on August 24, British Prime Minister Andy Burnham released to Ukraine the secret specifications for the British components of the Storm Shadow missile so that Ukraine and France can establish production there.
- The same day, Burnham and Zelensky signed an agreement making Britain the first foreign state with access to the combat data that trains Ukraine's strike-guidance artificial intelligence.
- Also on August 24, the European Commission approved €6.1 billion for Ukrainian missile and air-defense purchases, an installment of the €90 billion war loan agreed in December.
- Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain and Poland have asked the Commission to restart work on using roughly €200 billion in frozen Russian central bank assets held at Euroclear in Brussels to finance the war. Belgian Prime Minister Bart De Wever has warned the other European powers: "You cannot simply confiscate money. That is an act of war."

Andre Damon, in today's WSWS Perspective, draws the conclusion that the Ratcliffe mission indicates "Washington believes that there is an imminent danger of open warfare between NATO and Russia."

### 7. Fed chair Warsh signals rate increase as inflation runs at 3.7 percent

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole keynote on Friday to signal that the central bank is prepared to raise interest rates. Warsh had come under criticism in financial circles after July's meeting for failing to make his reaction function clear.

"There should be no misunderstanding," Warsh said. "The Fed's price-stability objective of 2 percent, as measured by the personal consumption expenditures price index, is a firm, fixed target… Price stability is not self-executing, nor is inflation necessarily mean-reverting. It is the Fed's job to deliver stable prices."

Key facts:

- Warsh cited PCE inflation at 3.7 percent, with the six-month change running at 4.1 percent, and elevated CPI: "they all tell a similar story… the Fed's predominant focus right now should be on prices."
- He dismissed labor-market softening as "consistent with full employment" — a formulation that treats rising unemployment as acceptable and, as Nick Beams noted on the WSWS, points to the suppression of wage demands as the operative mechanism of disinflation.
- On his own standard: "we must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we have work to do."
- Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee said he "generally agreed" with Warsh's characterization of inflation as the main problem.
- Market-implied odds of a September rate increase jumped to roughly 57 percent; money markets have fully priced in a hike by December.

### Iceland rejects EU accession talks in referendum

Icelandic voters rejected the government's proposal to open EU accession negotiations, with the no camp taking 52.8 percent to 47.2 percent, according to counting reported by public broadcaster RÚV on Sunday. Prime Minister Kristrún Frostadóttir's government had framed the vote as a "now or never" moment, and Brussels had signaled willingness to compromise on fisheries and agriculture to secure a wealthy new member.

Kristrún, who campaigned for yes, acknowledged that rural voters in particular feared for agriculture, fisheries and control of natural resources. Yes campaigners had argued membership would ease Iceland's high interest rates and offer security in a world destabilized by the Ukraine war and Trump's repeated threats to annex Greenland. The result is a significant blow to an EU seeking to consolidate a north Atlantic bloc under conditions of open conflict with Washington over the Arctic.

### Sudan: 200,000 displaced pour into El Obeid as war and floods converge

Conditions in El Obeid, capital of North Kordofan, and in two camps on its outskirts are described by aid workers as dire and deteriorating as civilians flee fighting between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces. The UN says more than 15,000 families have arrived since mid-July, joining more than 200,000 who arrived in the first half of the year, in a city that had roughly half a million residents before the war.

"The situation is very harsh and is deteriorating as El Obeid is receiving IDPs on a daily basis… The city is shockingly overcrowded," said a humanitarian volunteer identified as Maryam. New arrivals came on foot, carrying almost nothing.

- At least 200,000 people have been newly displaced nationally since late 2025 as fighting intensifies alongside seasonal flooding.
- The RSF and allied SPLM-N forces took Geissan in Blue Nile on August 11; the RSF retook Kurmuk on August 13. Mass graves containing 25 civilian bodies were found in Kurmuk after the SAF retook the town.
- Eight million school-age Sudanese children — five of every six — are out of school. Nearly three-quarters of Darfur's schools are damaged or destroyed. The UN warns that "Sudan is losing another generation."

### NASA launches the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off at 7:26 a.m. ET Sunday from Kennedy Space Center aboard a SpaceX Falcon Heavy, beginning a million-mile journey to the second Sun-Earth Lagrange point (L2), where the James Webb telescope also orbits. The 18,000-pound spacecraft, roughly the size of a tour bus, will take more than three months to reach L2, followed by calibration.

Roman has Hubble's optical sharpness with a field of view at least 100 times larger, allowing it to survey enormous swaths of sky at once. Its central missions are the mapping of dark matter through weak gravitational lensing — measuring how gravity bends light across cosmological distances — and the characterization of dark energy through a vastly expanded catalogue of Type Ia supernovae, the "standard candles" used to measure cosmic expansion.

The instrument's origin is itself instructive: it was initially built as a spy telescope for the National Reconnaissance Office and donated to NASA. The observatory carries the name of NASA's first chief astronomer, known as the "Mother of Hubble," who died in 2018. "It's basically like our time to kick the tires," said NASA integration and test scientist Jeremy Perkins.

## Pseudo-left press review

- [Jacobin / DSA](#jacobin--dsa) — frames the UAW election as a referendum on Fain's "reform" project and endorses a consumer boycott at Starbucks in place of industrial action
- [Left Voice](#left-voice) — published a defense of Bogdan Syrotiuk calling for an "international united front," while continuing to orient toward pressuring DSA and its elected officials
- [Liberation News / PSL](#liberation-news--psl) — reports the Tyson layoffs and Dolly Parton's death, but its electoral line remains support for "socialist" Democratic primary candidates
- [Socialist Alternative](#socialist-alternative) — advances "left independent candidates" for the midterms while presenting the Iran war through Financial Times framing
- [Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)](#socialist-worker--swp-uk) — covers NHS and cancer-research strikes militantly but subordinates them to the existing union apparatus
- [Communist / RCP (IMT)](#communist--rcp-imt) — correctly identifies Burnham's Ukraine trip as imperialist, then locates the alternative in party-building abstractions rather than the international unity of the working class

### Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin's most significant piece is **"The United Auto Workers Are Voting for Their Top Leadership"** (Luis Feliz Leon and Jane Slaughter, August 27), which presents the election as "a referendum on President Shawn Fain's attempts to reform the UAW since 2023." The framing is the whole political line: the union apparatus is treated as an institution capable of reform from above by a better leadership, and the members' role is reduced to ratifying or rejecting that leadership. The article does not address the collapse of the 2023 contract's promised gains, the wave of plant closures, or the systematic exclusion of rank-and-file opposition — nor does it mention Will Lehman's campaign or the demand for abolition of the apparatus.

**"Starbucks Workers Are Calling for a Nationwide Boycott"** (Jenny Brown, August 29) reports the union's turn to a consumer boycott without asking why, five years on, no contract exists. A boycott shifts the arena from the workplace to the market, substituting the goodwill of consumers for the collective power of workers — the logic of an apparatus that has ruled out the industrial mobilization that would be required.

Jacobin also ran **"Dolly Parton Used Her Voice to Tell Working-Class Stories."** Its cultural writing remains its strongest material, but the political function is to reduce class to a demographic identity to be represented rather than a social force to be organized.

*Assessment:* Jacobin's orientation remains the reform of the trade union bureaucracy and the Democratic Party from within, with the DSA's electoral operation as the vehicle.

### Left Voice

Left Voice published **"Free Bogdan Syrotiuk: Defend a Socialist Imprisoned for Opposing Imperialist War"** (August 28), stating that "Syrotiuk's case demonstrates the urgent need for an international united front against political repression." This is the second occasion on which the Morenoite PTS's US affiliate has taken up a case initiated and sustained by the ICFI. The defense of Syrotiuk is unconditionally correct, and the growth of support is welcome. But the "united front against political repression" formula detaches the case from what it is actually about — Syrotiuk was prosecuted for building the Fourth International's section in Ukraine and for opposing both Zelensky's regime and Putin's — and converts it into a generic civil-liberties campaign in which the political questions can be evaded.

**"AOC and Mamdani Should Apologize for Their Anti-Black Comments"** (August 27) is the purest expression of the tendency's method: a demand for apologies from two Democratic Party officeholders framed as a "teachable moment" for "Black Puerto Ricans and other Afro/Black Latinx people." The category "working class" does not appear; the political relationship to the Democratic Party is not raised at all.

**"What Does It Mean to Engage in Mass Politics? A Response to Eric Blanc"** (August 27) polemicizes against Blanc's adaptation of DSA to "what elected officials can defend inside the Democratic Party" — an accurate criticism whose limits are set by Left Voice's own orientation toward the DSA milieu as the audience.

*Assessment:* Left Voice combines formal opposition to the Democratic Party with a practical political life conducted entirely within DSA's orbit and within the framework of racial and identity categories.

### Liberation News / PSL

**"Workers in Joslin, IL are fighting back against Tyson's mass layoffs"** (August 28) covers the August 13 announcement of over 2,500 layoffs at the Joslin plant, delivered without notice to workers with more than 15 years' service. The reporting is concrete; the political conclusion is confined to community protest and pressure on the company.

Liberation also published **"In Memoriam: Dolly Parton."** Its recent electoral coverage — celebrating Peggy Flanagan's Minnesota primary win as a rejection of "AIPAC and ICE," and framing Francesca Hong's narrow Wisconsin defeat as evidence that the Democratic establishment "won't stop the rise of socialism" — remains the tendency's decisive weakness: the promotion of Democratic primary candidates as socialist advances, alongside nominal calls for "an independent political movement."

*Assessment:* PSL continues to fuse militant-sounding rhetoric with de facto support for the left wing of the Democratic Party.

### Socialist Alternative

**"Support Left Independent Candidates — Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties"** (August 2) sets the midterm line: not the construction of a revolutionary party, but electoral support for an assortment of independents whose programs are not specified. **"Trump's DHS Goes After Left Groups & Unions: We Won't Be Intimidated"** (August 19) documents real repression, but its perspective is the defense of existing organizations rather than the political mobilization of the working class against the drive to dictatorship.

On the war, **"The 'New' Middle East — Continued War Crisis"** opens by quoting the Financial Times' formulation that "Trump [is] caught between more war or a deal on Iran's terms" — adopting the framing of a leading organ of British finance capital as its own analytical starting point.

*Assessment:* Electoralist and defensive, with the analysis of imperialist war borrowed from the bourgeois press.

### Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

**"NHS strikers say, 'We don't do it for the money, but goodwill doesn't pay the bills'"** and **"Cancer research strikers make their voices heard outside talks with management"** (both August 28) report walkouts by operating theatre staff, clinical support workers, housekeepers and nurses across Greater Manchester, and an indefinite strike at the Institute of Cancer Research that has already forced concessions. The reporting is sympathetic and detailed. The conclusion in every case is to press the union leaderships to call more action — the apparatus is criticized for insufficient militancy, never identified as an instrument of the state.

**"Ukrainian AI system could be used to suppress dissent"** (August 28) notes correctly that Burnham handed over Storm Shadow blueprints and that British police may acquire Ukrainian AI surveillance technology. **"US bosses rake in record profits while workers suffer"** reports US corporate pre-tax earnings at $4.8 trillion in the second quarter alongside a historic low in labour's share.

*Assessment:* Trade-union militancy without a break from the bureaucracy; opposition to war framed as a moral objection to Labour policy rather than a struggle against the British state.

### Communist / RCP (IMT)

**"Buying time with Ukrainian blood: Burnham recommits to the slaughter"** (August 28) is the sharpest piece from any of these tendencies today, identifying Britain and the European powers as "cynically prolonging this bloodshed for their own imperialist interests." **"UK prison overcrowding: Burnham grapples with the crisis of the British state"** treats the prison crisis as "intractable under capitalism." **"Dolly Parton (1946-2026)"** is a substantial appreciation.

The uniform limitation is the conclusion: every article ends by advertising the RCP as the solution, without any concrete perspective for the intervention of the working class — no rank-and-file organization, no independent international strategy. The correct anti-imperialist phrase substitutes for a program.

*Assessment:* Verbal orthodoxy on war and the state combined with propagandistic party-building detached from the actual class struggle.
