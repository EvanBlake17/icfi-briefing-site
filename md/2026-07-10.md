### 4. DR Congo Ebola deaths pass 600 in fastest-growing outbreak on record

### Oral GLP-1 pill orforglipron beats oral semaglutide

In the 52-week ACHIEVE-1 trial of 1,698 adults with type 2 diabetes, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*, orforglipron lowered HbA1c by 1.71–1.91 percentage points from a baseline of 8.3%, versus 1.47 points for oral semaglutide. As the first once-daily, small-molecule (non-peptide) oral GLP-1 drug to outperform oral semaglutide, orforglipron could simplify manufacturing and expand access — though under the profit system such advances remain gated by price.

Adapted from Homer's epic and featuring Matt Damon, Tom Holland, Zendaya, Robert Pattinson and Anne Hathaway, the film represents another large-scale attempt to return ancient epic to mass audiences. Homer's poem — a meditation on war, homecoming, exile and the human cost of a decade-long conflict — carries obvious resonance in an era of open imperialist war; whether Nolan's spectacle engages that content critically or reduces it to visual grandeur will be the test.

In the visual arts, two major exhibitions center women artists of the twentieth century:
- **Alice Neel, "Beautifully Imperfect,"** opens July 16 at the Serralves Foundation in Porto. Neel, a lifelong radical who painted the poor, the working class and the marginalized of New York with unflinching humanism, spent decades ignored by the art establishment for her figurative commitment and her politics. Her rehabilitation is welcome, though institutions often sand away the class content of her work.
- **Ana Mendieta** receives her largest-ever UK exhibition at Tate Modern, featuring pieces never before shown in Britain. Mendieta's "earth-body" works, made after her family's exile from Cuba, grapple with displacement, violence and the body — themes museums tend to fold into identitarian framing at the expense of their broader social meaning.

Cultural output on the left underscores the enduring appeal of the anti-war and working-class tradition. The IMT-aligned communist.red this week published a review praising the 1943 film **The Silent Village** — a re-enactment of the Nazi massacre at Lidice staged by Welsh miners — as "filled with working-class spirit from top to bottom," alongside a review of Elem Klimov's harrowing 1985 anti-war masterpiece **Come and See**. That these tendencies reach for genuinely powerful anti-war art is notable; the question, as always, is whether the political conclusions drawn match the works' unsparing honesty.

The through-line across these developments is the pull of war, exile and class on serious culture — and the countervailing pressure of institutions to defang that content. Genuine artistic achievement, from Neel's portraits to Klimov's cinema, retains its power precisely because it refuses to look away.
