### 1. US strikes intensify as Trump declares Iran ceasefire "over"

The fragile US–Iran ceasefire has collapsed. US forces struck more than 80 targets around Bandar Abbas and Bushehr on Tuesday, then hit roughly 90 additional military objectives on Wednesday across Iranshahr, Konarak, Chabahar and Aq Qala. ### 5. DR Congo Ebola death toll passes 600

Crucially, there is no approved vaccine for this strain, and healthcare workers have threatened to strike over unpaid wages and inadequate protective equipment.

### Shoe factory fire kills 28 in China's Fujian province

At least 28 workers were killed when fire engulfed a five-story footwear factory operated by Huiteng in Jinjiang, Fujian province — China's "shoe capital" — around noon on July 9. Of 239 people present, 213 were evacuated; two died after hospitalization and 26 others reported missing were confirmed dead.

Preliminary findings show the blaze started on the ground floor and spread rapidly through highly flammable shoe-making materials, with large quantities of stock piled in stairwells that hampered escape and firefighting. Company executives and "other relevant personnel" have been detained and the firm's bank accounts frozen. As Peter Symonds notes on the WSWS, the disaster follows a fireworks explosion and a coal-mine catastrophe earlier in 2026 — part of an "ongoing carnage" in which employers treat workplace safety "as a cost that can be cut," especially among small manufacturers squeezed by China's slowing economy.

## Science, technology, and public health

- **H5N1 bird flu** — WHO logs 13 avian/swine flu cases this year including one fatal case; US dairy-cattle infections near 1,000 farms across 17 states
- **Ebola** — frontline health workers in Ituri unpaid since May and short of protective gear, threatening to strike mid-emergency
- **Measles** — cases rising in the US and globally amid falling vaccination rates

The gravest ongoing threat remains **H5N1 avian influenza**. The WHO has tracked 13 avian and swine influenza cases in humans in the first quarter of 2026, including one previously unreported fatal case, and reports the number of affected US dairy-cattle farms has risen to nearly 1,000 across 17 states. A new *Lancet* analysis estimates the true outbreak could be more than double reported figures. The WHO maintains that overall risk to the general population remains low and that no sustained human-to-human transmission has been observed — but the continued circulation in mammals is precisely the condition under which a pandemic-capable variant could emerge. The dismantling of public-health surveillance and the profit-driven refusal to cull or compensate at scale leave the population exposed.

The **DR Congo Ebola** catastrophe (see Top stories) has a distinct scientific and social dimension: there is no approved vaccine for the circulating Bundibugyo strain, and healthcare workers in Ituri say they have gone unpaid since the outbreak was declared in May and lack adequate protective equipment. Austerity and state neglect are directly amplifying a Public Health Emergency of International Concern.

Roundup:
- **Measles:** Cases continue to climb in the US and internationally against a backdrop of falling immunization rates and the political legitimization of anti-vaccine positions.
- **COVID-19:** No major new variant data in the past 24 hours; wastewater and hospitalization surveillance continue to be degraded by funding cuts, obscuring the true burden of ongoing transmission.
- **Workplace safety and technology:** The Jinjiang factory fire (see International) is a reminder that the most lethal "technology" failures remain the elementary ones — blocked exits and flammable stock — where regulation is subordinated to production costs.

## Arts and culture

Two developments this week illustrate the exhaustion of a culture industry driven by risk-averse finance capital: the July 8 Emmy nominations and a slate of nostalgia-driven July releases.

The **Emmy nominations** were led by the hospital drama *The Pitt* with 25 nominations, followed closely by the final season of *Hacks* with 24. That a series set in the chaos of an under-resourced emergency room should top the field is itself a comment on the period — the crisis of the American healthcare system (see the nurse strikes above) has become one of the few subjects through which mainstream television engages social reality with any honesty. The recognition of *The Pitt* reflects genuine popular hunger for drama that depicts working life and institutional breakdown rather than escapism.

Against this, the same week's major releases underscore the domination of the studios by intellectual-property recycling:
- **Disney's live-action *Moana* remake**, with Dwayne Johnson reprising Maui, is the latest in a conveyor belt of live-action reworkings of animated properties — a strategy of extracting rent from existing brands rather than creating new work, and a symptom of a film industry that treats art as a financial asset class.
- **Netflix's *Little House* revival** reboots the same characters and settings as the original series, part of the streaming platforms' relentless mining of intellectual property to hold subscribers in a saturated market.
- An **Apple TV noir–science-fiction series** — an alien working as a Los Angeles private eye — points, at least, to some appetite for genre experimentation within the streaming model.

On the literary side, the writer **Terry Tempest Williams** was profiled July 9 for a body of work attentive to what she calls the "holy ordinary" — the overlooked textures of the natural world and everyday life. Her environmental writing, grounded in observation rather than spectacle, stands as a quiet counterweight to a commercial culture organized around franchise and formula.

Taken together, the week's output confirms a familiar pattern: where genuine artistic achievement appears — as in a drama that takes the collapse of public healthcare seriously — it does so against the grain of an industry whose commanding heights are increasingly given over to the safest possible recycling of the already-successful. The task of a serious cultural criticism is to distinguish the two and to defend the space for art that engages honestly with social life.
