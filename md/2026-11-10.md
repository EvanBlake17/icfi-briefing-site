### 1. Trump declares Iran war "effectively over" as Tehran demands US "surrender" to its terms

After a closed-door White House meeting on Monday with Vice President JD Vance and Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani, Trump said the US-Israeli war on Iran is militarily finished: "Iran militarily is effectively over." He called a resumption of strikes "quite unlikely" and cited "positive momentum." He also made clear that the war's economic machinery stays in place. The US naval blockade of Iranian ports continues, and so does the newly announced **Task Force Iron Gate**, which aims to cut Iran's trade by land and across the Caspian Sea. Trump called the blockade "a continuation of the greatest naval blockade in the history of our country" and said US forces are "locked and loaded, ready to go for any reason that changes." Sheikh Mohammed carried a two-day-old Iranian proposal to end the war and posted about "positive discussions on reaching a deal."

Tehran dismissed Trump's remarks as "fake news." Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, called on Washington to "surrender" to Iran's seven conditions and said the war "has already wrecked the US government." President Masoud Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei wants an end to a situation of "neither war nor peace," and added: "We will not surrender to force and bullying." The talk of de-escalation is about locking in the gains of a war of aggression through economic strangulation. Trump boasted that Iran has been "very much decapitated."

Key facts:
- **Iran's seven conditions** include lifting US sanctions, releasing frozen funds, recognising a new Strait of Hormuz shipping route negotiated with Oman, and ending the war "on all fronts," including Israel's attacks on Lebanon.
- **The war began on February 28, 2026.** Iran moved quickly to close Hormuz and struck US sites in Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and the UAE. Israel occupied southern Lebanon. US strikes have become sporadic, with recent "minor strikes" on Qeshm Island.
- **The US has posted a $20 million reward** for information on Iran's nuclear programme. In June 2025 Trump claimed enrichment had been "obliterated," yet he has made it a central war aim again.
- **Monday's oil drop** came mainly from Trump's remarks (see World economy).

### 2. US-brokered Russia-Ukraine talks open in Abu Dhabi as Europe demands a seat

Russian and Ukrainian delegations meet in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11 under US mediation led by Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. It is the third round in the UAE this year, and the first three-way meeting since Geneva on February 18. Ukraine's delegation is led by Rustem Umerov, now head of the Foreign Intelligence Service. Russia is sending Kremlin aide Vladimir Medinsky, Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov and senior officer Yuri Zhigarlovsky. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the talks will focus on security guarantees and stay three-way. France, Germany and the European Commission are sending officials to Abu Dhabi anyway. Trump told reporters he sees a "very strong inclination" to end the war, and he had told *60 Minutes* that Ukraine will probably "end up with a little bit different shape."

At the G7 foreign ministers' meeting in Paris, Rubio's first with allies since the midterms, European ministers objected to being sidelined. German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul said changing Europe's security architecture is "certainly neither necessary, nor right, nor appropriate," and that "Europe belongs at this table." Ukraine's Andrii Sybiha said: "No one has the right to decide our fate behind our backs." Rubio, for his part, said guarantees for Kyiv "must also be acceptable to Russia" and that "the United States is not a combatant in that war." The dispute is between rival imperialist interests over how Ukraine is carved up. In the WSWS Perspective, Patrick Martin notes that the main criticism of Trump in the US establishment press is that he is not aggressive enough toward Moscow. Thomas Friedman invoked Neville Chamberlain after Trump approved a deal allowing sales of Russian diesel.

Key facts:
- **On territory and guarantees,** a US official says both sides have privately shown "some flexibility" on a territorial exchange. A guarantee modelled on NATO's Article 5 is under discussion, and territory would likely be settled last. Russia occupies about 20 percent of Ukraine. Putin demands all of the Donbas.
- **Zelensky claims Russia is suffering "record losses"** and that long-range strikes on Russian oil facilities are strengthening Kyiv's hand. The Kyiv Independent reports a second record daily Russian loss figure in a month and Ukrainian strikes on a St. Petersburg missile-fuel plant.
- **The G7 meeting (November 9–10)** also includes ministers from Saudi Arabia, Egypt, South Korea and Brazil. Iran is on Tuesday's agenda, and G7 allies have voiced scepticism about the US-Israeli war.
- **Zelensky asked India's Modi** to help open dialogue, citing food security.

### 3. Trump alleges midterm fraud while Democratic leaders preach "moderation"

Trump on Monday reversed his public acceptance of the November 3 results, in which Democrats took both chambers of Congress. "Does anybody really believe that this 'Election' was honest," he wrote on Truth Social, asking "How could the Polls be so wrong???" He offered no evidence and shared graphs questioning turnout data. Only days earlier he had told the *New York Times* he "absolutely" accepted the results. He told *60 Minutes* he had congratulated Democrats and was open to working with them. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer called the turn "inevitable." Megyn Kelly posted "Just stop." Pew Research found that polls had *understated* the Democratic margin by at least two points in 23 of 27 governor and Senate races, the opposite of what Trump implied.

The Democratic leadership's response has been to offer cooperation. In the WSWS Perspective, Patrick Martin dissects a Monday *New York Times* editorial, "This Winning Combination Is the Future of American Politics." It tells Democrats to run as "economic populists and cultural moderates," and it backs "well-funded police departments" and voter ID. Martin notes that Stephen Miller, architect of the mass deportation drive, became White House chief of staff the morning the editorial ran. He also notes that the editorial never mentions Iran, Gaza, Venezuela or Ukraine. As he writes, the vote expressed sentiment that is "far to the left of the Democratic Party."

Key facts:
- **The NYT's models** include Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, who credited Trump with a fall in border crossings, and Kansas Senator-elect Adam Hamilton, whose ad attacked "socialism and open borders." Its third example is Iowa's Josh Turek, who voted with Republicans more than 700 times.
- **House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries** has pledged to seek "common ground." DSA member and New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani spoke of shared purpose with the president (WSWS).
- **Polling cited in the editorial (via WSWS):** three-quarters of Americans are dissatisfied with the country's direction. Only 38 percent view Republicans favourably and 39 percent Democrats. Fifty-three percent say people worth $100 billion or more take more from society than they contribute.
- **Two security breaches near the White House** on Monday triggered a brief lockdown. A vehicle crashed at Lafayette Park and a second person jumped a barrier (Al Jazeera).

### 5. Winter floods kill five in Gaza tent camps as Israel blocks shelter

A rain-soaked sandbank collapsed onto a tent in Deir el-Balah on Sunday, killing four children and a woman. Two people remain missing. Gaza's first winter storm hit on Friday and flooded camps for displaced people. It also weakened buildings already damaged by Israeli air raids. Because Israel restricts specialist rescue equipment, residents dug with their bare hands. Gaza's Government Media Office held Israel, the United States and the "international community" "fully and directly responsible." It also condemned the Trump-chaired "Board of Peace" and the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza. As Jacob Crosse writes on the WSWS, the deaths are a product of policy, not weather: the population has been denied durable shelter.

Key facts:
- **About 70,000 tents were flooded** over the weekend (Al Jazeera). UNICEF says more than 106,000 people were directly affected (WSWS).
- **Save the Children** says more than half a million children live in "critical or catastrophic" shelter conditions. Israel has repeatedly blocked the caravans the ceasefire envisaged as an alternative to tents.
- **Under the "ceasefire"** in force since October 2025, Israel has killed more than 1,600 Palestinians. The toll since October 2023 exceeds 74,600.

### New Zealand election ends in deadlock

Preliminary results from the November 7 election leave no clear winner. Labour (29.36 percent) narrowly beat National (29.21 percent) on the party vote, the lowest combined major-party share on record. National nonetheless holds 42 seats to Labour's 36 because of an electorate "overhang." The National–ACT–NZ First bloc has 67 of 131 seats, a two-seat majority that roughly **399,000 uncounted special votes** (about 14 percent) could erase when the count is completed on November 27. Labour leader Chris Hipkins accuses National of having "manipulated the electoral system" through vote-splitting. He calls democracy not a "plaything for billionaires." Green co-leader Chlöe Swarbrick accuses Prime Minister Christopher Luxon of "voter suppression" over an enrolment change. Luxon says the result is "too close to call."

- **The smaller parties:** the Greens reached a record 13.3 percent, ACT 10.9 percent and NZ First 9.78 percent. The Opportunity Party (5.96 percent, 7 seats) enters parliament for the first time (WSWS figures).
- **Turnout was 74.6 percent of enrolled voters,** the second-lowest on record. More than 1.2 million eligible adults did not vote (WSWS).

### Supreme Court clears border wall through Big Bend National Park

The Supreme Court on Monday granted the administration's emergency request to lift District Judge Kathleen Cardone's injunction against wall construction through a remote section of Big Bend National Park in West Texas. The court said the injunction "impedes the Government from advancing its 'compelling interests' in border security." It found the government "exceedingly likely to prevail" on the argument that the 1996 Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act strips district courts of jurisdiction except over constitutional claims, and it held that "there is no such thing as a 'constitutional' major-questions claim." Only Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson noted dissent, without explanation.

- **The plaintiffs** were the Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church, and a river guide and landowner. They challenged DHS waivers that bypass the Endangered Species Act, NEPA and the National Historic Preservation Act.

### Theoretical and historical polemics

- **Thomas Scripps, "Two syndicalists gut the 1926 General Strike of its political lessons," Part Three.** Scripps reviews Callum Cant and Matthew Lee's *The Future in our Past* (Verso). He shows that the authors, who admit they "have not found a united and experienced vanguard," replace Trotsky's analysis of 1926 with that of J.T. Murphy, who later became a Stalinist. Scripps calls this the "tail-ism" Lenin attacked in *What Is to Be Done?*
- **Kipchumba Ochieng, review of Hakim Adi's *Pan-Africanism and Communism*, Part Seven.** Ochieng shows that Adi defends the Comintern's "Third Period" without examining its result, Hitler's victory in 1933, which the "social fascism" line made possible. He then traces the Popular Front's subordination of colonial struggles to "democratic" imperialists. That runs through France in 1936, Spain, and Italy's 1935 invasion of Ethiopia, and leads to the founding of the Fourth International in 1938.
