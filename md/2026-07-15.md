### 1. US reimposes naval blockade and resumes bombing of Iran as ceasefire collapses

US Central Command reimposed its naval blockade of Iranian ports and coastal waters at 20:00 GMT on July 14 and launched a fourth consecutive night of strikes against Iranian missile, drone, and coastal-defense sites, marking the definitive collapse of the interim "Islamabad memorandum" that had briefly paused the war. Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the blockade "has, in a way, dismantled" the deal.

President Trump, who a day earlier had floated a 20 percent transit fee on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, reversed the proposal and adopted open threats: "They shot first, and that was a big mistake... We have been knocking the hell out of them."

The escalation confirms the analysis advanced repeatedly on the WSWS that the "ceasefire" was a pause for regrouping, not peace, and that US imperialism is driving toward a wider Middle East war to reorder the region.

Key facts:
- Iran's army says at least 7 personnel were killed when 13 missiles struck a guesthouse and accommodation facilities at a military base near Iranshahr.
- At least 2 seafarers were killed in related attacks in the Strait of Hormuz.
- Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck "two non-compliant" supertankers and fired missiles and drones at US military infrastructure in Bahrain and Jordan.

### Germany expands surveillance and police-state powers

Germany's "Security Package 2.0," passed by the federal cabinet in late April 2026, bundles three draft laws authorizing the Federal Criminal Police Office (BKA) and Federal Police to carry out automated data analysis, biometric internet matching, and real-time facial recognition of "wanted persons" at railway stations and airports. Police in three federal states already use Palantir's "Gotham" software, with a fourth set to roll it out and potential nationwide expansion. A petition against the proposals, name-checking Palantir, has surpassed 150,000 signatures, and more than a dozen civil-society groups warn the laws threaten anonymity in public space and may conflict with the EU AI Act. The WSWS argues the measures are an authoritarian response to mounting popular opposition to rearmament and austerity — effectively criminalizing dissent.

### New heat wave endangers workers across the US

A record-shattering heat wave has spread across the United States, with more than 100 million Americans under heat alerts from Los Angeles to Boston. Salt Lake City hit an all-time record 109°F on July 12, and Billings, Montana reached 111°F, with Miles City, Montana touching a preliminary 115°F. The extreme heat is exposing workers to life-threatening conditions in un-air-conditioned plants and outdoor worksites, yet OSHA still lacks a binding federal heat-safety standard. The WSWS reports more than 48 heat-related worker deaths occur annually — including postal workers Wendy Johnson and Jacob Taylor — and calls for independent rank-and-file safety committees, arguing that employers and unions alike subordinate worker safety to production quotas.

## Science, technology, and public health

A record cyclosporiasis outbreak has sickened nearly 7,000 people across 34 states, with Michigan the hardest hit at 3,309 cases — already exceeding the 2019 record with six weeks left in the season. As Evan Blake reports on the WSWS, the surge follows Trump–Kennedy administration cuts that gutted federal foodborne-disease surveillance, eliminating some 3,500 FDA and 2,400 CDC jobs. The outbreak, driven by contaminated fresh produce, is a direct consequence of dismantling public-health infrastructure in the interests of agribusiness profit; the WSWS calls for socialized public health and the expropriation of the agribusiness monopolies.

Disease surveillance:
- **H5N1 avian influenza:** The CDC reports 71 human cases and 2 deaths in the US from 2024 to May 2026, with no confirmed person-to-person spread; globally, 12 additional human cases (3 fatal, in Bangladesh and Cambodia) were reported from August 2025 to June 2026 across Bangladesh, Cambodia, and India. The virus remains widespread in wild birds, US poultry, and dairy cattle.
- **Measles:** The US recorded 164 confirmed cases in 2025 across nine states, with continued clusters driven by declining vaccination rates and the erosion of federal public-health capacity.
- **Ebola (DRC):** US health officials now require citizens who have traveled in the Democratic Republic of Congo to spend 21 days in a third location before entering the US amid a growing outbreak — a punitive measure that does nothing to address the underfunded state of Congo's war-ravaged health system.

Climate and environment:
- The **2026 Europe report of the Lancet Countdown** on health and climate change finds that nearly all monitored European regions (823, or 99.6 percent) saw increased heat-attributable deaths in 2015–24 compared with 1991–2000, warning of a "narrowing window for decisive health action."
- New analyses in **Nature Climate Change** document multidimensional intensification of global heat stress since 1950 and show, across more than 5,100 species, that climate change has caused more frequent local extinctions among temperate than tropical species.

The convergence of these findings — accelerating heat mortality, spreading zoonotic disease, and the deliberate defunding of surveillance — underscores the WSWS argument that the response to biological and climatic threats is being sacrificed to austerity and private profit.

## Arts and culture

The death of **Sam Neill**, the New Zealand actor, dominated cultural news. Neill died in Sydney aged 78, in a passing described as "sudden and unexpected," months after he said in April that he remained cancer-free following treatment for stage-three blood cancer. Neill's five-decade career resists the reduction of screen acting to blockbuster spectacle. Though best known internationally as Dr. Alan Grant in *Jurassic Park*, he built a body of work rooted in the independent and art cinema of Australia and New Zealand — from Gillian Armstrong's *My Brilliant Career* (1979), a landmark of the Australian film revival engaging questions of women's independence and colonial society, to Jane Campion's *The Piano* (1993). His willingness to embody menace and moral ambiguity, in films from *Possession* to *Dead Calm*, reflected a serious craftsman's temperament increasingly rare in an industry dominated by franchise product. His obituaries have rightly stressed the substance of his work over its box-office scale.

In new releases, **Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey*** — an adaptation of Homer starring Matt Damon, Tom Holland, Anne Hathaway, Robert Pattinson, Zendaya, Charlize Theron, and Lupita Nyong'o — anchors the summer film calendar. A large-scale studio treatment of one of the founding works of Western literature carries the potential to bring the epic's themes of war, exile, and homecoming to a mass audience, though it also raises the perennial question of whether the spectacle apparatus of contemporary Hollywood can do justice to the poem's tragic and humanist depths.

Roundup:
- **The Odyssey (film):** Nolan's ensemble adaptation of Homer arrives as the season's most anticipated release, testing whether classical literature can survive translation into franchise-scale cinema.
- **Booker Prize:** The 2026 "Booker Dozen" longlist will be announced July 28 — an occasion to assess the state of literary fiction against the pressures of a concentrated publishing industry.
- **Fraenkel Film Festival:** The expanded 2026 edition opens with artist Christian Marclay's choice, Brian De Palma's *Carrie*, and marks the 40th anniversary re-release of Ross McElwee's *Sherman's March*, a foundational work of personal documentary cinema.
- **Repertory and revival culture** — the festival re-releases and artist-curated screenings — point to a continued appetite for cinema history that runs counter to the streaming platforms' erasure of the film heritage.

These developments should be understood against the broader assault on culture — defunding, censorship of anti-war and pro-Palestinian expression, and the subordination of artistic production to corporate concentration — that the WSWS has documented, and against which genuine artistic achievement, wherever it appears, must be defended.

## WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

**Perspective published:** "A revealing comment from former IMF chief economist" by Nick Beams — read in full on wsws.org

Beams takes as his point of departure a *New York Times* op-ed by Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, who stepped down in June as the IMF's chief economist and, freed from institutional constraints, offered an unusually candid diagnosis of the crisis of world capitalism. Beams' central argument is that Gourinchas's framing of the world economy as caught between two opposing "forces" — the destruction of war and the supposedly countervailing boost of AI investment — is fundamentally misleading: AI under capitalism does not offset the logic of war and fragmentation but intensifies the same contradictions, being deployed to destroy jobs and raise profit rates. The two "forces" are two expressions of a single crisis of the profit system.

Beams treats Gourinchas's own words as significant admissions from within bourgeois orthodoxy. Gourinchas warned of "a dangerous feedback loop" as "countries seek to insulate themselves from perceived risks" and "risk fragmenting the global economy further still," and observed that "the world's superpowers are searching for strategic advantages, identifying choke points, adopting inward-looking policies and increasing military expenditures." Most strikingly, Beams highlights Gourinchas's explicit analogy to the pre-1914 period: "We have seen such dynamics before... What followed was a period of intense deglobalization, coinciding with rising nationalism and militarization — and two world wars." Beams reads this as an unintentional concession that global capitalism is retracing the slide into inter-imperialist war. Gourinchas, he concludes, "could offer no solution" beyond a "vacuous appeal" to IMF cooperation; the only answer is "the perspective of socialist revolution."
