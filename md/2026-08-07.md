### 3. Trump signs two executive orders attacking birthright citizenship

Trump signed two executive orders on Thursday, August 6, aimed at narrowing birthright citizenship — his second frontal assault on the 14th Amendment and the first since the Supreme Court struck down his January 2025 order by a 6-3 vote on June 30, 2026.

The first order expands the categories of people the administration claims are ineligible for birthright citizenship to include "alien enemies of the United States, members of foreign terrorist organizations and large categories of people who lobby and act on behalf of foreign government." The second targets so-called "birth tourism." Both are expected to face immediate legal challenge.

Writing on the WSWS, Jacob Crosse characterized the orders as an attempt to establish, by executive decree, the principle that citizenship is a revocable privilege conferred by the state rather than a constitutional right — the legal architecture for mass denationalization.

Key facts:

- The June 30 ruling explicitly upheld birthright citizenship as constitutional; the new orders attempt to achieve the same end by redefining who falls within the amendment's "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" clause.
- The "alien enemies" category is deliberately elastic and overlaps with the administration's designation of political opponents and pro-Palestinian activists as agents of foreign powers.
- The orders arrive alongside an AP investigation showing ICE has detained more than 50 spouses and parents of active-duty US service members (see United States).

### 5. July jobs report: 83,000 payrolls added, employment level down 833,000 for the year

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released the July employment situation at 8:30 a.m. Eastern this morning. Nonfarm payrolls rose by 83,000 and the unemployment rate held at 4.2 percent.

The headline stability conceals a contracting labor market. The employment level has fallen by 833,000 over the course of 2026, and the jobless rate has been held down primarily by declining labor force participation rather than job creation. June's gain was revised to a mere 57,000, the weakest in four months, following a downward revision of May to 129,000. The broader U-6 measure, which includes involuntary part-time and discouraged workers, has risen to 8.2 percent, and the employment-population ratio sits at a four-year low. Citigroup economists project unemployment breaking above 4.5 percent in coming months.

Key facts:

- Consensus forecasts had been for 80,000-83,000 payrolls with unchanged unemployment — the report landed almost exactly on expectations, giving the Fed no impetus to move before its September 15-16 meeting.
- Job growth has been concentrated in health care and leisure/hospitality; manufacturing and federal employment continue to shed positions.
- The report follows an ADP private payrolls miss on Wednesday that had already signaled softness.

### 8. Amnesty finds "hallmarks of genocide" in Sudan's El Fasher as famine spreads

Amnesty International has described Rapid Support Forces atrocities in El Fasher as "a stain on the conscience of humanity" following the paramilitary group's completion of an 18-month siege and capture of the North Darfur capital. A separate UN investigation concluded the takeover bore "the hallmarks of genocide," with thousands killed.

Famine conditions are now confirmed in El Fasher and Kadugli. Roughly 375,000 people are in the most extreme hunger category, concentrated in North Darfur, South Kordofan and West Kordofan. Amnesty sent its findings to RSF commander General Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo on June 10 and received no response.

- Sudan's war has displaced more people than any other conflict on earth yet receives a fraction of the coverage devoted to Ukraine or Gaza.

### Yen intervention exposes US Treasury market fragility

The dominant financial story of the week remains the extraordinary joint US-Japan currency intervention. The Japanese government and Bank of Japan bought yen for a second consecutive day while the New York Fed sold euros to buy yen on behalf of the US Treasury — the first coordinated US-Japan yen-buying operation since 1998. Tokyo spent roughly ¥5.33 trillion on Friday after a record ¥8.45 trillion the previous day, potentially selling as much as $59 billion. ### FDA approves first mRNA flu vaccine after refusal-to-file reversal

The FDA on August 5 approved Moderna's mFlusiva, the first licensed mRNA-based influenza vaccine. The agency granted standard approval for ages 50-64 on the basis of a 40,000-person trial showing 27 percent fewer confirmed influenza cases than a standard-dose comparator, and accelerated approval for ages 65 and older contingent on a required Phase 4 confirmatory trial.

The significance is manufacturing as much as efficacy: mRNA platforms allow strain composition to be updated in weeks rather than the six months required for egg-based production, which is the principal reason seasonal flu vaccine effectiveness collapses in years when the circulating strain drifts after strain selection. The approval follows a rare FDA refusal-to-file earlier this year that was subsequently reversed — an episode that took place against the backdrop of the administration's broader hostility to mRNA technology, including the cancellation of federal mRNA development contracts.

## Arts and culture

### Bayreuth's AI-generated *Ring* booed at the festival's 150th anniversary

The most significant cultural event of the week is the Bayreuth Festival's new production of Wagner's *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, titled *Ring 10010110*, staged for the 150th anniversary of the cycle's premiere at the Festspielhaus. Curator Marcus Lobbes, working with Nils Corte and dramaturge Andri Hardmeier, fed roughly 1,000 images drawn from the fifteen previous Bayreuth *Ring* productions into a generative model, which then produced a continuous stream of associative imagery as the cycle's visual environment. Singers appeared as floating sculptural figures in costumes by Pia Maria Mackert.

The audience booed. Lobbes and his design team were met with whistles at the curtain call, while conductor Christian Thielemann and the cast received a warm reception. Critical reaction identified the central failure precisely: the software could assemble images but could not construct drama. Reviewers described *Das Rheingold* as "stiff and dramatically inert," with the performers only gradually reclaiming human presence over the course of the four evenings.

The episode deserves attention beyond the opera world. The *Ring* is, among other things, a work about the corrupting power of accumulated wealth and the destruction of the natural and social world by those who seize it — themes that have never been more directly relevant. To reduce its staging to a recombinant collage of prior stagings is to substitute a statistical average of the past for an artistic engagement with the present. Generative models can only interpolate within what has already been made; they cannot pose a question. The boos at Bayreuth were, in this sense, an audience defending art against its liquidation into content.

- **"Victory for the left in Michigan Senate primary shows hunger for change"** (August 5) presents El-Sayed's Democratic primary win as evidence of "progressive momentum." The SWP thus endorses, from Britain, the strategy of subordinating American workers to a party of US imperialism — a position that would have been unthinkable for the International Socialists in their own founding period, and which converges precisely with the framing of the liberal US press.
- **"Trade unionists must lead the fight against the EHRC's transphobic guidance"** (August 5) calls on the union apparatus to lead the defence of trans people against the Equality and Human Rights Commission's guidance. The demand is directed at the same bureaucracies that have imposed real-terms wage cuts across the public sector; the identity question is posed in isolation from any programme for the working class as a whole.
- **"Tensions between US and China point to more pain ahead"** (August 3) analyzes inter-imperialist rivalry in essentially economic terms, without drawing the conclusion that the war danger requires the independent political mobilization of the working class against both blocs.

**Assessment:** The SWP's orientation remains protest-movement building and pressure on the union officialdom, with support for left-Democratic formations abroad. The characterization of a Democratic primary result as a "victory for the left" is the day's clearest expression of the tendency's abandonment of political independence.

### The Communist / RCP (IMT)

- **"Another government, same system – Britain needs a revolution!"** (Adam Booth) responds to the change of government with a general call for revolution. The article contains no transitional programme, no assessment of the Labour Party's role in the war drive, and no accounting for the IMT's own decades inside Labour — the "revolution" is asserted rather than politically prepared.
- **"India's Cockroaches rise: no retreat until Modi falls"** (Ravi Mistry) celebrates youth protests in India. The formulation "until Modi falls" substitutes the removal of an individual for the question of state power and the necessary alliance of the urban working class with the rural poor; the article offers no analysis of the Stalinist parties' role in disarming the Indian working class.
- **"Stand Up to Opportunism – Why the SWP gets everything wrong on racism"** (Jack Tye Wilson) polemicizes against the SWP's anti-racism work. The critique registers real opportunism but is conducted without reference to the historical origins of either tendency's break with Trotskyism — the Cliffite revision of the Soviet class question, the Grantite adaptation to social democracy — which is precisely what would give the polemic content.

**Assessment:** The RCP combines maximalist rhetoric with a practice of recruitment to radicalized moods. Its refusal to conduct any settling of accounts with the Pabloite origins of its own tendency is the political condition for its continued adaptation.

### Left Voice, PSL and Socialist Alternative

All three tendencies published limited new material in the past 24 hours. Left Voice's most recent significant piece covers the cancellation of Temporary Protected Status placing 300,000 additional immigrant workers at risk of deportation — accurate reporting, but framed within a perspective of "united front" work that the WSWS has previously identified as a mechanism for propping up the union bureaucracy and the Democratic Party. The PSL continues to publish anti-ICE and anti-war material while orienting to Democratic-aligned coalitions in practice. Socialist Alternative's line on the El-Sayed and Mamdani campaigns follows its established pattern of critical support for left-Democratic candidates while calling for an eventual break that is perpetually deferred.

**Convergence to note:** Across the SWP, Socialist Alternative and substantial layers of the DSA, the El-Sayed result is being presented in terms functionally identical to the liberal bourgeois press — as evidence that the Democratic Party can be transformed from within. No tendency reviewed here raised the question of the Democratic Party's direct responsibility for the Gaza genocide and the war against Iran.

## What the WSWS should cover today

### 1. Verizon and Kaiser Permanente contracts expire as unions refuse to call strikes

Twenty thousand Verizon workers have been without a contract since August 1 and 24,600 Kaiser Northern California nurses face expirations this month, with no strike deadline set by either the CWA/IBEW or National Nurses United. This is the largest concentration of expired-contract workers in the US at present and a direct test case for the rank-and-file committee perspective; the pattern of allowing contracts to lapse indefinitely while negotiations proceed in secret is the central mechanism by which the apparatus prevents a unified struggle.

### 2. WestJet strike and the fight against unpaid ground work in aviation

Four thousand four hundred CUPE flight attendants shut down Canada's second-largest airline over unpaid boarding and ground time, cancelling 309 flights before a tentative agreement was reached within four days. The rapidity of the settlement, following the 2025 Air Canada strike, raises the question of whether CUPE moved to contain a struggle that had immediate national impact — and the "credit hour" system at issue is industry-wide, affecting flight attendants at every North American carrier.

### 3. Sudan: famine and mass killing in El Fasher and Kordofan

The RSF's capture of El Fasher after an 18-month siege has produced findings of "hallmarks of genocide" from a UN investigation and confirmed famine affecting 375,000 people in the most extreme category. The war is directly fuelled by UAE arms and Gulf capital's interest in Sudanese gold and agricultural land, making it a case study in the neo-colonial recolonization of Africa that receives a small fraction of the coverage devoted to other theatres.

### 4. Tariffs as a $900 tax increase on every US household

The Tax Foundation calculates that the 2026 tariff regime — including the 35 percent levy on Canadian goods effective August 1 — amounts to an average tax increase of roughly $900 per household this year, with no measurable improvement in the trade balance. Public attention to tariffs has collapsed even as the costs are passed through to consumers, making this an under-covered mechanism of downward wealth redistribution and a direct refutation of the "economic nationalism benefits workers" argument advanced by both the Trump administration and the UAW leadership.

### 5. Thai school shooting and the crisis of youth in Southeast Asia

A 15-year-old killed his grandparents and then seven people at his Bangkok-area high school, Thailand's worst massacre since 2022. Youth violence of this kind is emerging across countries with sharply rising inequality, collapsing employment prospects for young people and militarized state cultures — a phenomenon requiring the same social analysis the WSWS has applied to school shootings in the United States rather than the individualized psychological framing offered by the press.

### 6. FIFA's aborted plan to sell equity in the World Cup

Infantino's "FIFA Forward Enterprise" scheme would have sold private stakes in the commercial rights to the World Cup, negotiated without consultation with member associations, players or supporters. The affair is a concentrated example of the drive to convert every remaining collective cultural institution into a financial asset, and of the total exclusion of the players — who are workers — from decisions over the industry they produce.

### 7. Europe's fourth heatwave and the absence of heat protection for workers

Italy issued red alerts for a record 25 cities and Austria broke its national temperature record twice in two days, with no corresponding national heat-stress standard mandating work stoppages for outdoor and agricultural labourers in most of Europe. The absence of enforceable protection — four years into consecutive record summers — is a direct expression of the subordination of workers' lives to production schedules and an area where WSWS coverage of climate can be tied concretely to labour conditions.
