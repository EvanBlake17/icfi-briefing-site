### 1. Russia launches its largest air assault of the year on Kyiv

Russian missiles and drones struck the Ukrainian capital in the early hours of July 6, killing at least 12 people and wounding some 60 more, according to President Volodymyr Zelensky. Emergency crews combed rubble at residential high-rises in two districts that took direct hits. Ukraine's air force said Russia fired 351 drones and 68 missiles overnight, and that all 29 ballistic missiles launched struck their targets — a sign of the widening gap in Ukrainian air defenses after months of attrition and dwindling Western interceptor supplies.

Russia's Defense Ministry described the bombardment as retaliation for Ukrainian long-range strikes that have inflicted severe fuel shortages inside Russia. The Kyiv attack follows a Russian strike days earlier that killed 31 people in the capital — the deadliest single assault on Kyiv this year.

Key facts:
- Ukrainian strikes cut power across occupied Crimea; the Moscow-appointed head of Sevastopol reported blackouts later restored via backup equipment
- Two people were wounded and more than 70 Ukrainian drones downed over Yaroslavl, Russia
- Trump is scheduled to meet Zelensky and Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa on the sidelines of a NATO summit in Turkey this week

### 2. Trump uses Mount Rushmore address to declare communism a "mortal threat"

### 3. Heat dome kills at least 25 in the US as European toll nears 20,400

A multi-day heat dome over the central and eastern United States killed at least 25 people over the July 4 weekend, with New Jersey accounting for 22 of the confirmed deaths. Officials said many victims were found in homes without air conditioning; others were discovered on the street or in parked cars. Atlantic City hit 106°F on July 4 and Newark reached 105°F for the first time since 2001. Roughly 40 million people remained under heat alerts, and July 4 parades were canceled or delayed in Washington, Philadelphia and elsewhere.

The US deaths followed a late-June European heat wave that a preliminary climate-modeling study estimated killed near 20,400 people — one of the continent's deadliest heat events since 2003, led by France, Germany, Spain and Italy. As Kevin Reed writes on the WSWS, class inequality — not the weather alone — determines who lives and dies: roughly 9 million US households have no air conditioning, and grid demand and prices spiked as the most vulnerable were left exposed.

Key facts:
- Spain and Portugal approached 44°C (111°F); France recorded its hottest June day on record at 43.3°C (110°F)
- The World Weather Attribution initiative found fossil-fuel emissions have sharply intensified European heat waves within a few decades
- Cooling demand across parts of Europe hit its highest level in at least 45 years

### 5. PECO utility workers strike for the first time in 145 years

More than 1,600 unionized workers at Philadelphia Electric Company (PECO) walked off the job just after midnight July 4 — the first strike in the utility's 145-year history — after contract negotiations collapsed amid a lethal heat wave. IBEW Local 614 members across automation, engineering, customer service, facilities and line work struck over wages, benefit tiers and pensions. The union is demanding that all roughly 1,500 members be covered by pensions; some 600 currently are not.

The union also objected that PECO's offer gives call-center workers — 98 percent of whom are women — a smaller raise than other members. "We will not accept a contract that undercuts the women of our union," said the union's Anastasi. PECO says its offer includes a wage increase of nearly 20 percent over five years plus enhanced retirement and medical benefits. Philadelphia's estimated living wage is $23.34/hr. IBEW locals across Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia and Georgia have directed members not to take PECO emergency or side work. The WSWS warns union leadership may isolate rather than expand the strike and urges independent rank-and-file organization.

### New Caledonia election produces no outright winner

New Caledonia's Congress election delivered a near-deadlock. The main loyalist (pro-France) coalition Les Loyalistes–Le Rassemblement, led by Sonia Backes, won 24 of the 54 seats — four short of an absolute majority — on the strength of its dominance in the populous South Province. Pro-independence parties, led by the Kanak and Socialist National Liberation Front (FLNKS), took 26 seats collectively but ran on three separate lists, weakening their hand. The centrist Eveil Océanien won the remaining four seats, again positioning itself as kingmaker. Turnout was 63.71 percent. The Congress holds its inaugural sitting July 10 to elect its president.

The vote follows Macron's repeated postponements after the 2024 uprising that killed 14. As John Braddock writes on the WSWS, the deadlock reflects unresolved poverty and colonial alienation that neither the new assembly nor negotiations with Paris will address.

## World economy

- [June jobs report collapses to 57,000](#world-economy) — payroll growth far below the 115,000 consensus; unemployment falls to 4.2% only because labor force participation dropped to a post-2021 low
- Gold, silver and Bitcoin rallied on the weak data as markets priced in Fed easing; oil slid below $68 for the first time in months
- The Dow closed at a record 52,900 ahead of the holiday even as leisure and hospitality shed 61,000 jobs

The dominant economic story is a sharply weakening US labor market. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payrolls rose just **57,000** in June — well below the 115,000 Dow Jones consensus and slower than a downwardly revised 129,000 in May. The unemployment rate fell to **4.2 percent**, but largely because the labor force participation rate dropped 0.3 point to 61.5 percent, the lowest since March 2021. April and May payrolls were revised down by a combined 74,000. Leisure and hospitality shed 61,000 jobs. The soft report pushed back expectations of a Fed rate hike and fueled bets on easing — a reminder that "good news" for equities (cheaper money) increasingly coincides with deteriorating conditions for workers.

Data roundup:
- **US equities:** The Dow closed July 2 at a record 52,900.07 (+1.14%); the S&P 500 was little changed; the Nasdaq-100 fell 1.61% on tech volatility
- **Gold:** Rose from roughly $3,900 to around $4,200/oz entering July (about +8%), still well off January's $5,600 record
- **Oil:** WTI crude slipped below $68 for the first time in about 125 days after a surge in Saudi exports, later steadying near $68.60
- **Crypto:** Bitcoin rebounded from a low near $58,000 to around $64,000 (about +10%), outperforming gold, driven by the weak jobs data and Fed-easing expectations
- **Trade:** USMCA entered annual review after the US failed to reach a continuation deal (see International); the EU enacted its side of a US trade agreement
- **Executive pay vs. labor:** As the WSWS documents in auto parts, Magna's CEO received a 15% raise and Adient's a 40% raise amid plant closures, while Magna Highland Park hires start at $17.50/hr

The combination of record equity highs, surging executive compensation and a stalling labor market underscores the class polarization driving the day's data.

## Science, technology, and public health

- [US measles cases near 2,170](#science-technology-and-public-health) — 2026 is on pace to match the entire 2025 total with six months left; 92% of cases in unvaccinated or unknown-status people
- New "Cicada" (BA.3.2) COVID variant detected in wastewater across 25 US states and 22 countries; regulators back an updated fall vaccine
- *Nature* researchers identify single neurons enabling human language generation using AI-assisted recordings

**Measles surges toward a record.** As of July 2, the CDC confirmed **2,170 measles cases across 41 jurisdictions** in 2026 — nearly matching the 2,289 cases recorded for all of 2025, with half the year remaining. There have been 31 outbreaks, and 93 percent of cases are outbreak-associated. Ninety-two percent of cases are in unvaccinated people or those of unknown status; only 4 percent had received two MMR doses. Children aged 5–19 make up 51 percent of cases and under-5s another 20 percent. The surge is driven by declining MMR coverage, which fell to 92.5 percent in 2024–25, below the 95 percent herd-immunity threshold.

**A new COVID variant with immune-escape potential.** BA.3.2 ("Cicada") has been detected in wastewater across 25 US states and confirmed in 22 other countries, according to CIDRAP. It carries more than 70 mutations from the ancestral SARS-CoV-2 strain, raising immune-escape concerns, and is circulating alongside dominant lineages XFG ("Stratus") and NB.1.8.1 ("Nimbus"). Regulatory advisers have unanimously backed updating the fall vaccine formulation to target newer lineages.

Roundup:
- **H5N1 bird flu:** The CDC's global summary reports 12 human infections and 3 deaths (1 in Bangladesh, 2 in Cambodia) between August 2025 and June 2026, with no confirmed human-to-human transmission; US risk assessed as "low"
- **Neuroscience:** A July 1 *Nature* paper (Vol. 655) combined single-neuron recordings with AI to identify brain cells that enable human language generation; a companion paper showed targeted neuronal modulation restored motor function in a Huntington's mouse model
- **AI regulation:** The FTC opened a public comment period (through July 31) on a proposed policy statement addressing whether AI companies manipulate chatbot behavior in ways that violate consumer expectations of accuracy — running parallel to the EU's June 29 AI Act simplification package, which defers high-risk compliance deadlines to December 2027
- **Climate attribution:** The World Weather Attribution initiative concluded fossil-fuel emissions have rapidly worsened European heat waves over just a few decades, tying the ~20,400-death June event directly to warming

## Arts and culture

Christopher Nolan's **The Odyssey** premieres in London today, July 6, ahead of a July 17 wide release by Universal in IMAX and premium formats. Adapted from Homer's epic, the film follows Odysseus's perilous return from the Trojan War through encounters with the Cyclops, the Sirens and Calypso. It carries an unusually large ensemble — Matt Damon as Odysseus, Anne Hathaway as Penelope, Tom Holland as Telemachus, Zendaya as Athena, Robert Pattinson as Antinous and Charlize Theron as Circe — and was shot on location across Italy, Iceland, Greece, Morocco and Scotland. That a major studio is mounting a mass-audience adaptation of one of the foundational works of world literature — a narrative of war, exile, endurance and homecoming — is itself notable in an industry dominated by franchise product; the reception will test whether serious engagement with the classical inheritance can still command a broad public.

The premiere arrives against the backdrop of the US "semiquincentennial," a commemoration the Trump administration has sought to bend toward nationalist and militarist spectacle — the Mount Rushmore address and fireworks being the clearest example. The tension between the democratic and revolutionary content of 1776 and its reactionary appropriation is a live cultural battleground, one the pseudo-left is also contesting (see below).

Roundup:
