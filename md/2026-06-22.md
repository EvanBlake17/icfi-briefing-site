### 1. US-Iran negotiations resume in Switzerland amid fragile ceasefire

### 4. France gripped by record June heatwave; 3 dead, 49 departments on red alert

France is in the grip of a record-breaking June heatwave, with 42.2°C recorded in Pissos (Landes) and Châteaumeillant (Cher). Paris's overnight low of 24.2°C broke its June record set in 2017. Forty-nine departments were placed under maximum red alert and 40 more under orange alert — putting nearly 35 million people, over 90% of France's population, under heat warnings. Météo-France called the episode of "exceptional intensity," comparing it to the August 2003 heatwave that killed roughly 15,000 in France.

Key facts:
- Three confirmed heat-related deaths in Gironde (residents aged 80–95); 13 drowning deaths over the weekend; more fatalities expected.
- More than 180 monthly temperature records broken or equalled on Sunday alone.
- 845 schools closed or minimally staffed; 71 SNCF Intercités routes cancelled; roads melted in Meurthe-et-Moselle; 600 households lost power in Indre-et-Loire.
- The dead so far are all elderly — a marker of the class character of climate vulnerability, as the isolated elderly poor bore the brunt in 2003.

The full-year 2025 total was 2,288 cases — meaning the US is on pace to surpass last year's historically high count with six months still remaining, driven by low vaccination rates in certain communities and continued global importations.

### NEJM details rare H5N5 human case with severe outcome

The *New England Journal of Medicine* published the first well-documented H5N5 human case report: a 75-year-old immunocompromised woman with Waldenström macroglobulinemia developed ARDS after bird exposure. Critically, her initial upper-respiratory nasal swab tested negative for influenza A — the virus was only detected in lower-respiratory specimens. The finding warns that standard upper-airway testing may miss avian flu in pneumonia patients, with implications for clinical surveillance.

## Arts and culture

The art world is absorbing a heavy stretch of losses. The death of British painter **David Hockney** at 88, on June 11, removes one of the most vital figures to emerge from mid-20th-century pop art. Hockney's California swimming-pool paintings, double portraits, photo-collages and opera designs spanned six decades; his restless experimentation — from Polaroid joiners to iPad drawing — reflected an artist who refused to treat any medium as beneath serious art. Whatever the commercial mythology built around him, Hockney's best work testifies to a sustained effort to render perception, light and intimacy with fresh eyes, against the grain of art-market cynicism.

The losses extend across disciplines. Photographer **Duane Michals** died June 9 at 94; avant-garde guitarist **James Blood Ulmer**, who fused jazz, funk and blues in collaborations with Ornette Coleman, died June 3 at 86. Also among the month's dead: documentary photographer David Plowden, chronicler of American industrial decline, and Russian dissident artist **Robert Kuzovkov**, shot dead in Poland — a killing that, if politically motivated, would underscore the dangers facing Russian oppositionists in exile and merits scrutiny rather than the silence it has received.

- **Oliver Tree**, the 32-year-old singer-songwriter, died in a helicopter crash near Rio de Janeiro during his world tour. His viral alt-pop persona was built heavily on internet irony, but his death cuts short an artist still searching for a voice beneath the performance.
