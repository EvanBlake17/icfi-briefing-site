### 1. US pauses Iran bombing campaign as Netanyahu flies to Washington

The United States halted its bombing of Iran over the weekend after 13 consecutive nights of strikes, and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu departed for Washington on Monday for a Tuesday White House meeting with Trump — the first face-to-face session between the two since the war began in late February. The pause immediately moved markets, with crude falling sharply and equity futures rising.

Trump has not committed to a new ceasefire. Asked about reports that US precision munitions stocks were being depleted by the campaign, he insisted the military has "far more than we need." The pause follows the collapse earlier this month of the ceasefire established under the June 17 US-Iran memorandum of understanding, an agreement Netanyahu opposed. Netanyahu will also attend the Washington funeral of Senator Lindsey Graham.

**Key facts:**

- US strikes over the past two weeks targeted Iranian military command centers, drone storage facilities, communications networks, coastal surveillance sites and maritime capabilities, according to the Pentagon.
- The Defense Casualty Analysis System lists 14 US service members killed. Nearly 100 have been injured, mostly minor, since July 7. Three of the most recent dead were killed by Iranian strikes in Jordan, one by a drone detonation in Iraq.
- Iranian deaths stand at a minimum of 3,468, including 376 children, according to figures cited by the WSWS.
- UN envoy Mike Waltz said last week that Trump was giving talks "some space." Iran condemned Ukrainian strikes on a vessel in the Caspian Sea that Kyiv said was carrying military goods to Russia, which killed one crew member.
- Andre Damon's Perspective on the WSWS today argues that the entire establishment debate treats the war "as a policy that has failed" rather than as a crime, and that the Nuremberg standard — aggression as "the supreme international crime" — applies directly.

### 2. Trump reimposes global tariffs under Section 301 after Supreme Court defeat

Five months after the Supreme Court struck down his IEEPA "reciprocal tariffs" in a 6-3 ruling, Trump has restored country-specific duties through a different statute. The administration has announced tariffs of 10 to 12.5 percent on roughly 60 countries under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, to take effect August 19. The stated justification is inadequate enforcement of foreign labor laws — a rationale Nick Beams of the WSWS calls "an obvious sham," given that the administration is simultaneously dismantling labor protections at home.

Section 301 requires the US Trade Representative to conduct investigations before imposing duties, and trade lawyers have already flagged the compressed timeline as legally vulnerable. USTR Jamieson Greer has said new investigations are being opened into several countries.

**Key facts:**

- The February 20 ruling held that the executive exceeded its constitutional authority by taxing imports without Congress. The administration replaced the IEEPA tariffs with a 10 percent baseline under Section 122, later raised to 15 percent.
- The 2025 US goods trade deficit was essentially unchanged at $901 billion despite three years of tariff escalation, and more than 100,000 US manufacturing jobs were lost over the same period.
- The August 19 effective date is now the dominant overhang for markets heading into this week's Federal Reserve decision and Big Tech earnings.
- Beams situates the tariff regime within the imperialist drive toward "a new world war," noting the parallel between the present trade conflict and the economic warfare of the 1930s.

- Nearly 100,000 hectares have burned in France alone this season.

### 4. German state seizes on Berlin Pride attack to demand new surveillance powers

- Police ended the Pride party early and launched a large-scale manhunt overnight Saturday.
- Prosecutors confirmed Ballout's prior ISIS-related conviction and "high number" of previous offenses.
- Dobrindt characterized the attack as suspected Islamist terrorism within hours.

### 5. US measles cases hit a 35-year high

The CDC has confirmed 2,318 measles cases in the United States in 2026 as of July 23, across 44 jurisdictions — the worst year since 1991 and already well past the 2025 total, with five months still to run. Thirty-five distinct outbreaks have been recorded this year, and 2,153 cases, or 93 percent, are outbreak-associated.

The proximate cause is the collapse of vaccination coverage. MMR coverage among kindergarteners fell from 95.2 percent in the 2019-20 school year to 92.5 percent in 2024-25, below the roughly 95 percent threshold required for herd immunity. Benjamin Mateus of the WSWS calculates that this leaves approximately 286,000 children in each entering cohort unprotected, and attributes the resurgence not to any scientific failure but to state-sponsored anti-vaccine propaganda combined with the systematic defunding of public health infrastructure.

**Key facts:**

- Ninety-three percent of confirmed cases were in people unvaccinated for MMR.
- No US deaths have been recorded in 2026; there were three in 2025.
- The country's measles elimination status — held since 2000 — will be formally reviewed in November. Sustained transmission for more than 12 months would end it.
- Summer travel is expected to push the total higher through August.

### 6. Democrats decline to mobilize opposition to Trump's election plans

- In *Watson*, the Supreme Court ruled for states on counting timely-postmarked ballots, a partial check on the federal effort.
- Election administration is constitutionally a matter for Congress and the states; the order is an assertion of executive supremacy over both.

### 7. Two-thirds of Gaza projected to face acute hunger by year's end

The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification released a new assessment last week finding that famine conditions in Gaza have formally ended following the October 2025 ceasefire and improved aid deliveries, but that the gains are fragile and reversing. The IPC projects that 1.9 million people — roughly 90 percent of Gaza's population — will face high levels of acute food insecurity between July and December 2026, with more than two-thirds in "crisis" phase or worse, as humanitarian agencies cut aid flows because of funding shortfalls.

**Key facts:**

- Most of Gaza's 2.1 million residents remain displaced in overcrowded tent camps or heavily damaged neighborhoods with limited access to clean water and healthcare.
- Eighty-three percent of households report no source of income, leaving them entirely dependent on external assistance.
- Only one in five children in Gaza currently has access to an adequate diet.
- Acute malnutrition among children fell sharply after the ceasefire, demonstrating that the earlier famine was a product of the blockade, not of scarcity.

### Burnham government takes office amid Trump's public attacks on Britain

Andy Burnham became Britain's 59th prime minister on July 20 after meeting King Charles III, having won the Labour leadership the week before. He is the country's seventh prime minister in a decade. Keir Starmer resigned in June after eighteen months of policy reversals, heavy May local election defeats and the controversy over appointing Peter Mandelson as ambassador to Washington.

Burnham, until recently mayor of Greater Manchester, has pledged to address the cost of living, end homelessness, "re-industrialize" the country and put "life's essentials back under public control" — a formulation that stops well short of nationalization commitments and has already been read by the financial press as rhetorical. Trump used the occasion to denounce Britain publicly as a "Poverty Stricken Disaster." The essential continuity is fiscal: Burnham inherits Starmer's spending rules, NATO rearmament commitments and the austerity budget framework, and no element of the Labour program contemplates breaking with them.

### Australian Labor conference passes everything unanimously with no floor debate

### Lancet Countdown Europe report finds heat mortality up 52 deaths per million

The 2026 Europe report of the Lancet Countdown on health and climate change, published in *The Lancet Public Health*, finds that heat-related mortality in Europe rose by 52 deaths per million inhabitants between the 1991-2000 and 2015-24 periods. In 2024, Europeans experienced 182 hours of at least moderate heat stress risk during light physical activity — 113 hours, or 166 percent, more than the 1991-2000 annual average. The report's subtitle, "narrowing window for decisive health action," is not rhetorical: it documents that despite a growing volume of scientific literature on the climate-health nexus, individual, political, corporate and media engagement with it *declined* in 2023 relative to prior years.

This finding bears directly on the current European catastrophe. The mortality data are not projections but retrospective measurements — the deaths have already occurred, and the institutional response has moved backward.

### Cambodia confirms fifth human H5N1 case of 2026

Cambodia's Ministry of Health confirmed H5N1 avian influenza in a nine-month-old girl on July 9, the country's fifth human case this year. She was isolated and given intensive care. Between August 4, 2025 and June 10, 2026, twelve human H5N1 infections were reported by three countries outside the United States, with three deaths — one in Bangladesh, two in Cambodia. Cumulative global human cases since 1997 stand at 1,022. In the US, 71 human cases with two associated deaths were identified between 2024 and May 2026. No person-to-person transmission has been identified in any of these cases, and the CDC continues to assess public risk as low — an assessment that rests entirely on the absence of a mutation that virologists regard as a matter of time rather than possibility.

### AI infrastructure spending reshapes the physical economy

Thursday's US Q2 GDP release is expected to show 2.3 percent annualized growth, up from 2.1 percent, with AI capital expenditure identified alongside consumer spending as the principal driver. This is a striking macroeconomic fact: a substantial fraction of measured US growth now consists of a handful of corporations building server farms for one another. Alphabet alone will spend up to $205 billion this year. The electricity demands of this buildout are colliding directly with grid stress already documented during the July heat emergency, when the Department of Energy intervened to prevent blackouts on PJM.

## Arts and culture

Christopher Nolan's *The Odyssey* has taken $639.6 million worldwide in two weekends — $284.4 million domestic, $353.2 million international — including $140 million in IMAX, the format's largest sophomore weekend on record. The second-weekend decline of 27 percent is among the smallest ever recorded for a wide release of this scale, and the film is projected to pass $1 billion in the first week of August. That an R-rated adaptation of Homer, shot substantially on large-format film and running well over two and a half hours, should perform this way deserves more attention than the trade press has given it.

The commercial mechanics are less interesting than what the result indicates about audiences. The prevailing industry assumption for fifteen years has been that mass audiences require franchise familiarity, and studios have accordingly withdrawn from serious adult filmmaking. The reception of *The Odyssey* suggests instead a substantial appetite for work that treats its viewers as capable of sustained attention and engages material — exile, return, the destruction wrought by a decade of war on those who fought it and those who waited — that carries obvious weight in the third year of a general crisis of war. Homer's poem is, among other things, about what soldiers bring home. Whether Nolan's film reckons seriously with that or aestheticizes it is a question worth the WSWS reviewing at length.

**Frank Gehry (1929-2025).** The WSWS has published Paul Bartizan's obituary of the architect, who died in Santa Monica on December 5 at 96 after a brief respiratory illness. Bartizan traces the arc from the 1978 Santa Monica house — assembled from chain link, corrugated metal and plywood, and initially regarded as an affront — through the Guggenheim Bilbao (1997) and Walt Disney Concert Hall (2003). Gehry's contribution was to make computer-aided design serve sculptural, non-orthogonal form, producing what Bartizan characterizes as a contemporary cubism in built space. The obituary is honest about the contradiction: the "Bilbao effect" became a template for using signature architecture as an instrument of real estate speculation and urban gentrification, precisely the opposite of the democratic ambition Gehry professed. Bartizan reads Gehry's stated interest in finding a "sliver of space" for human feeling within commercial constraints against Trotsky's conception of an architecture that would eventually serve the masses rather than their landlords.

### The contemporary relevance of Felix Morrow's *Revolution and Counter-Revolution in Spain* — Our reporters

A report on a Berlin symposium marking the 90th anniversary of the Spanish Civil War. Peter Schwarz argues that Morrow's 1937 analysis, written in the immediate aftermath of the defeat, remains the indispensable account of why the Spanish revolution was destroyed — not by fascist military superiority but by the Popular Front policy, executed by the Stalinists and supported by the anarchist and centrist leaderships, which subordinated the working class to the Republican bourgeoisie and physically suppressed the revolutionary movement in Barcelona in May 1937.

Schwarz's argument for contemporary relevance rests on the origins of fascism in capitalist crisis and on the role of the "left" in disarming the working class before it. The parallel drawn is to the AfD's growth in Germany and the character of the response from the SPD, Left Party and the trade union apparatus — a defense of the existing state and its institutions rather than an independent mobilization of the working class. The symposium is part of the sustained effort by the ICFI's German section to root political work in the theoretical settling of accounts with Stalinism and Popular Frontism.

### This week in history: July 27 – August 2

**Korey Stringer, 2001.** The Minnesota Vikings offensive tackle died of heatstroke on August 1, 2001, after collapsing during training camp in temperatures with a heat index of 110°F. His core body temperature reached 108.8°F. The NFL had no mandatory heat protocols; Stringer had been publicly criticized in the press the previous day for leaving practice early. His death forced changes to league heat policy but only after litigation, and the underlying calculus — that the profitability of the spectacle takes precedence over the bodies producing it — remains.

**West Virginia miners' wildcat, 1976.** In late July 1976 a wildcat strike over hospital and medical benefits spread from Cabin Creek across the West Virginia coalfields, ultimately involving over 100,000 miners across several states in defiance of a federal court injunction and of the United Mine Workers leadership under Arnold Miller. The strike was a decisive moment in the postwar confrontation between the miners and the union apparatus, and Miller's role in breaking it prefigured the wholesale integration of the UMWA into the operators' structure the following decade.

**UN Refugee Convention, 1951.** Signed July 28, 1951, in the aftermath of a war that displaced tens of millions across Europe, the Convention established non-refoulement — the prohibition on returning refugees to territories where their lives or freedom are threatened — as a principle of international law. Every major imperialist power that signed it is now in systematic violation, from Mediterranean pushbacks to the US deportation regime.

**Philippine independence plebiscite vote, 1926.** The Philippine House voted in July 1926 for a plebiscite on independence from the United States. Governor-General Leonard Wood vetoed it. The episode illustrates the reality behind American anti-colonial rhetoric: the United States, which had acquired the islands by purchase and suppressed the Philippine republic in a war that killed hundreds of thousands, simply refused to permit the population to be asked.

Left Voice has published a defense of The People's Forum and BreakThrough News following the July 22 subpoenas issued by the House Ways and Means Committee demanding internal communications and financial records, including Emma Lee and M. Carlstad's *Stand against the state's attack on The People's Forum and BreakThrough News*. The opposition to this McCarthyite campaign is correct and necessary. What is absent is any accounting of the political character of the organizations being defended, or any explanation of how a defense of democratic rights is to be conducted other than through the same coalition politics that has repeatedly collapsed.

Nancy Cázares' piece on Hamas agreeing to transfer administration of Gaza to a pro-imperialist committee under threat of renewed war describes the outcome as "a victory for Israel and U.S. imperialism." The characterization is accurate as far as it goes, but Left Voice offered no independent perspective to the Palestinian and Israeli working class at any point in the preceding period; the analysis is retrospective commentary on a defeat.

André Barbieri's coverage of Trump's prime-time address on the 2020 election and the declassification announcement treats the conspiracy campaign as primarily a propaganda exercise rather than as the ideological groundwork for the concrete measures now being prepared against the midterms.

*Assessment:* Correct positions on specific questions of democratic rights, unmoored from any strategy for the working class independent of the milieu Left Voice orbits.

### Liberation News / PSL

The PSL's statements on the Iran war maintain a formally anti-imperialist position, describing the destruction of Iranian sites as opening "the gates of hell in the Middle East, perhaps igniting a regional and global war." The PSL has also published statements against the immigration raids and a party statement on Russia's intervention in Ukraine.

The critical political question is what the PSL proposes. The July 7 statement *New York is ready for socialism* supplies the answer: an electoral orientation to municipal office in the framework of the Democratic Party's periphery. The PSL's characterization of Russia's intervention, meanwhile, avoids any independent working-class position, substituting a campist framework in which opposition to NATO becomes de facto political support for the Kremlin — a position that cuts directly against uniting Russian and Ukrainian workers.

*Assessment:* Militant phraseology on imperialism combined with an electoralist and campist practice.

### Socialist Worker / SWP (UK)

*Governments look the other way as thousands face nightmare fires* attacks right-wing politicians for cutting fire service funding. The framing is significant for what it excludes: the fires are treated as a resourcing failure attributable to a particular political current, not as a product of the profit system, and the incoming Labour government — which has committed to the fiscal rules that make such cuts inevitable — escapes examination.

*Get behind the cancer research strikers* calls for solidarity with Institute of Cancer Research workers striking through Unite. The appeal is to support the union's action, with no discussion of Unite's record of isolating and selling out disputes.

*The spectre of socialism is haunting America* interviews American socialists on the growth of socialist sentiment. The tendencies celebrated are precisely those integrating into the Democratic Party.

*Assessment:* A left-reformist orientation to the union apparatus and to a Labour government whose program the SWP declines to subject to criticism.

### Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

Adam Booth's *The many faces of Andy Burnham — how long until the mask slips?* argues that the Burnham government will enter crisis immediately and criticizes the "false optimism" of trade union leaders and progressive figures. Callum Parkinson and Dane Yates' *Burnham's "Number 10 North" and the myth behind "Manchesterism"* argues that regional devolution cannot address British capitalism's systemic inequality. Both contain accurate observations. Both nonetheless treat the Labour Party as the arena in which the crisis will be resolved, with the RCP's role being to await and interpret the disillusionment rather than to build an independent revolutionary party.

*Anarchism in practice*, examining the Spanish Civil War and Rojava, is a useful corrective to anarchist mythology — though the IMT's own record on the Popular Front, and its long history of entrism in social-democratic parties, sits uneasily with the argument.

*Assessment:* Theoretical orthodoxy in historical questions, adaptation to Labourism in practice.
