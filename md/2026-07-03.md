### 1. Russia launches one of the war's largest strikes on Kyiv, killing at least 22

Russia carried out its heaviest bombardment of Ukraine in over two weeks overnight July 1–2, firing 74 missiles and 496 Shahed-type drones in a coordinated attack that struck 33 locations, mostly in the capital. At least 22 people were killed and nearly 100 wounded in Kyiv, where a multistory residential building collapsed and a central hotel was hit. Authorities declared July 3 a day of mourning.

The barrage included some of Russia's most advanced weapons — four Zircon hypersonic missiles, 24 Iskander ballistic missiles, 34 Kh-101 cruise missiles, eight Kalibr and four Kh-59/69 — alongside the mass drone swarm. Ukrainian air defenses intercepted 48 missiles and 476 drones, but 25 ballistic missiles and 12 drones penetrated, underscoring the growing strain on Ukraine's Western-supplied interceptor stocks. The strike came as the Institute for the Study of War assessed continued incremental Russian gains on the ground.

Key facts:
- 25 ballistic missiles and 12 drones evaded interception, striking 33 locations
- In Kharkiv, three more people were wounded July 2, including a 7-year-old girl
- ISW assesses Russia gained a net 11 square miles of Ukrainian territory in June and holds a presence in 37 percent of Kostyantynivka in the Donbas
- The attack followed weeks of escalating Russian long-range strikes as US and European military aid negotiations remain unresolved

### 2. US Supreme Court upholds birthright citizenship, strikes down Trump order 6-3

The US Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that children born on US soil to parents "unlawfully or temporarily present" are citizens at birth under the 14th Amendment, striking down President Trump's executive order seeking to end birthright citizenship. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the majority, joined by Sotomayor, Kagan, Barrett and Jackson; Thomas, Alito and Gorsuch dissented, while Kavanaugh concurred in part.

The ruling reaffirms the 1898 precedent of *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, whose descendants publicly celebrated the decision. But the political establishment immediately signaled it would seek to overturn birthright citizenship by other means. House Speaker Mike Johnson said he was "very disappointed," warning the ruling "subjects the country to serious challenges going forward, and we'll have to deal with it as Congress." Trump and congressional Republicans said they will now pursue legislation to end birthright citizenship by statute.

Key facts:
- The decision leaves the 14th Amendment's citizenship guarantee intact but Republicans are already drafting statutory workarounds
- The ruling arrives amid the largest immigration enforcement operation in US history (see United States section)
- Kavanaugh's partial concurrence leaves open procedural avenues the administration may exploit

### 4. US adds just 57,000 jobs in June as labor force shrinks

US nonfarm payrolls rose just 57,000 in June, roughly half the 115,000 consensus forecast and down from a downwardly revised 129,000 in May. The Bureau of Labor Statistics report — released July 2, moved up from its usual first-Friday schedule for the holiday — was the weakest in four months and revised April and May down by a combined 74,000.

The headline unemployment rate ticked down to 4.2 percent from 4.3 percent, but only because the labor force shrank by 720,000, dropping the participation rate to 61.5 percent — its lowest since March 2021. Average hourly earnings rose 3.5 percent year-over-year to $37.64, trailing inflation for a third straight month. Leisure and hospitality employment fell by 61,000, partly attributed to World Cup-related disruption.

Key facts:
- Real wages have now fallen behind inflation for three consecutive months
- BMO's Ian Lyngen said the data "makes it difficult to envision a path toward a July Fed hike," with rate-cut odds rising
- Gains were concentrated in professional/business services (+36,000), social assistance (+25,000) and healthcare (+22,000)

### 5. Venezuela earthquake death toll passes 2,500 as rescue phase winds down

The confirmed death toll from the twin June 24 earthquakes in Venezuela has passed 2,595, with 12,400 injured, according to acting President Delcy Rodríguez, as authorities begin winding down the search-and-rescue phase. The two quakes — magnitude 7.2 and 7.5, striking seconds apart — leveled roughly 1,000 buildings, including hospitals, and more than 400 schools.

Nearly 50,000 people remain unaccounted for, and the USGS PAGER system warns the ultimate toll could run far higher than confirmed figures. Roughly 1.8 million people need humanitarian assistance. UN agencies described skyrocketing needs, with UNICEF's Roberto Benes reporting families "are sleeping outside, afraid of more aftershocks." Some 30,000 Venezuelan and 2,700 foreign rescue workers have pulled more than 6,400 people from the rubble. As Andrea Lobo notes on the WSWS, the catastrophe has been compounded by years of economic collapse under US sanctions and by a "botched" official relief effort that has left the working class to fend for itself.

### Keiko Fujimori declares herself Peru's president-elect

Keiko Fujimori of the right-wing Fuerza Popular declared herself Peru's president-elect after official counts showed her defeating leftist Roberto Sánchez by roughly 50,000 votes — 50.135 to 49.865 percent, a margin of under 0.20 percent out of some 18 million cast. ### Germany adopts sweeping war laws with NATO chief present

The Merz-Klingbeil government adopted a comprehensive military package July 1, with NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte present at a cabinet session held at the Defence Ministry rather than the Chancellery — the first such attendance by a NATO chief. Three central laws are being pushed to the Bundestag: a Reserve Strengthening Act eliminating "double voluntariness" and making reserve call-ups mandatory for former soldiers even in peacetime (3–12 weeks annually); a Bundeswehr Infrastructure Acceleration Act exempting military construction from environmental and planning law by declaring it of "overriding public interest"; and amendments subordinating civilian infrastructure, healthcare and the economy to military planning under "comprehensive defence."

The measures implement Germany's new Military Strategy, which designates Russia the central threat and aims to expand forces to 260,000 active soldiers and 200,000 reserves. As Johannes Stern writes on the WSWS, the laws serve both external war preparation and internal suppression of antiwar opposition, reviving German militarism eight decades after 1945.

### Iran and Oman push Hormuz transit-fee plan over US objections

### Heat dome endangers warehouse and auto workers

A record heat dome blanketed more than half the US ahead of July 4, with 162 million people across 35 states under heat alerts. As Tom Hall reports on the WSWS, workers at a UPS facility in Knoxville, Tennessee described "physically lying on the floor" from heat exposure while supervisors walked past; at Ford's Dearborn Truck Plant, managers reportedly ordered the removal of fans workers had rigged at stations exceeding 100°F. ### Lancet publishes landmark neurology trials

*The Lancet* published a special neurology issue featuring three closely watched studies. The **PADOVA trial** tested prasinezumab, an anti-alpha-synuclein antibody, in early-stage Parkinson's disease; the **ORATORIO-HAND study** extended ocrelizumab treatment to wheelchair-using patients with progressive multiple sclerosis, a group historically excluded from trials; and the **HEAD study** compared tau PET tracers used in Alzheimer's diagnosis. Together the studies reflect a shift toward disease-modifying rather than purely symptomatic treatment of neurodegenerative disease — advances that, under the profit system, will be rationed by cost and access rather than distributed on the basis of need.

### US measles cases surpass 2,100 as outbreaks multiply

As of late June, the CDC reported 2,134 confirmed US measles cases for 2026 across 30 outbreaks, with 93 percent of cases outbreak-associated — the continuing consequence of falling childhood vaccination rates. Pennsylvania alone recorded 75 cases across six counties since late April. Internationally, Bangladesh reported 11,011 laboratory-confirmed measles infections and 680 confirmed or suspected deaths since March 15, a stark illustration of collapsing global immunization coverage.

## Arts and culture

The most politically significant cultural development of the day is the detention of Turkish comedian **Deniz Göktaş** at Istanbul airport over political satire. As Ulaş Sevinç reports on the WSWS, the arrest is part of a widening crackdown by the Erdoğan government on artists, comedians and journalists whose work criticizes the state. The targeting of satire — historically among the sharpest weapons of the oppressed against arbitrary power — reflects the regime's fear of any independent voice as economic crisis and political instability deepen in Turkey. The case belongs to a broader international pattern in which the ruling class responds to social discontent by criminalizing dissent.

On the WSWS arts pages, **Clara Weiss** reviews the late historian Alexander Rabinowitch's fourth volume, *The Bolsheviks Survive: Petrograd 1919*. The book documents the Bolsheviks' rule during the civil war's most difficult year — fuel shortages, British intelligence infiltration by agents such as Paul Dukes, and Finnish counterrevolutionary conspiracies. It emphasizes Leon Trotsky's decisive intervention against General Yudenich's October 1919 assault on Petrograd, when Trotsky arrived by armored train and mobilized both the Red Army and the city's working class to prevent occupation. Rabinowitch also examines the March 1919 Eighth Party Congress, where the Stalin-backed Military Opposition challenged Trotsky's disciplined, centralized Red Army model — a conflict Lenin resolved in Trotsky's favor. Weiss praises the archival scholarship while noting the volume's neglect of the international context, above all the founding of the Communist International in 1919.

Roundup:
- **Victor Willis**, the original lead singer of the Village People and co-writer of "Y.M.C.A.," "In the Navy" and "Macho Man," died June 30 at 74. Willis's voice defined a strand of 1970s disco whose exuberant, working-class and often coded gay sensibility has since been crudely appropriated as a backdrop to reactionary political spectacle — a reminder of how popular art is stripped of its original social meaning.
- The **Munich Film Festival** (June 26–July 5) continues, with an international program drawing attention amid European cinema's ongoing funding squeeze.
- The **Margaret Mead Film Festival** at New York's American Museum of Natural History features documentary work including *Time and Water*, a eulogy for a vanishing Icelandic glacier — part of a growing body of cinema confronting the climate crisis as a social and not merely natural catastrophe.

## Pseudo-left press review

- [Jacobin / DSA](#jacobin--dsa) — defends the DSA against liberal attacks while urging the "left" to work through the Democratic Party
- [Left Voice](#left-voice) — frames DSA electoral wins as a break to the left while offering no independent revolutionary alternative

- [SWP (UK) and the RCP/IMT](#swp-uk-and-the-rcpimt) — both adapt to the collapse of "Your Party," promoting Corbyn and Green leader Zack Polanski

### Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin's front page is dominated by the debate over the DSA's electoral rise. In **"Jonathan Chait Doesn't Understand the Socialists He's Attacking,"** editor Bhaskar Sunkara defends the DSA against a liberal *Atlantic* essay, insisting the organization stands in continuity with founder Michael Harrington. The political line is explicit: the DSA's project is the "realignment" of the Democratic Party rather than a break from it — a program of pressuring capitalist politicians from within, which subordinates the radicalizing base the WSWS Perspective describes to the very party of Wall Street and war. **"Socialism Was Central to W. E. B. Du Bois's Thought"** by Jeff Goodwin reclaims Du Bois's Marxism against liberal sanitization — a genuine contribution historically, though deployed to lend socialist authority to Jacobin's reformist orientation. **"An Unchangeable Constitution?"** features historian Jill Lepore on the decline of constitutional amendment in favor of judicial rule, treating the crisis of American democracy as a procedural rather than a class question. Overall orientation: channel the youth radicalization into the Democratic primary machine.

### Left Voice

Left Voice, publication of the Trotskyist Fraction (PTS), interprets the DSA primary victories as evidence of a leftward shift among workers and youth, correctly criticizing the DSA's dependence on the Democratic Party. But its own perspective substitutes militant rhetoric and calls for "self-organization" for the concrete construction of a revolutionary party rooted in the ICFI's programmatic struggle against Pabloism, leaving its readers oriented toward the same milieu it criticizes. Its coverage of Gaza and Ukraine combines correct denunciations of imperialism with a tendency to tail spontaneous protest movements rather than fight for their political independence.

### Liberation News / PSL

Liberation News centers its coverage on US imperialist aggression against Venezuela following the earthquake and on the Iran war's aftermath. Its anti-imperialism, however, is bound up with uncritical defense of the Maduro government and other bourgeois-nationalist regimes, obscuring the class contradictions — the seventeen family clans of Peru, the Venezuelan state's abandonment of earthquake victims — that drive the very crises it describes. The PSL frames the defense of these governments as anti-imperialist solidarity, when a Marxist orientation requires the political independence of the working class from all factions of the national bourgeoisie.

### Socialist Alternative

Socialist Alternative continues to promote the call for a "new party for working people," pointing to the DSA wins and to European developments as evidence of an opening. Its perspective remains one of building electoral and protest pressure to win reforms, adapting to left-moving Democrats and trade union officials rather than exposing the union bureaucracy's role — most sharply illustrated by the impending Goodyear and Bridgestone contract fights, where such officials are preparing new concessions.

### SWP (UK) and the RCP/IMT

Both British tendencies are responding to the collapse of "Your Party," the Corbyn-linked project whose leadership has just voted to ban dual membership for socialist organizations. As the WSWS documented in June, the SWP had published positive write-ups of the project and continues to promote Corbyn as an ally — including inviting him to its Marxism 2026 festival — while drawing no lessons from the debacle. The RCP (former Socialist Appeal/IMT) has responded by championing Green Party leader Zack Polanski as a left successor, again directing workers toward a bourgeois party. Both orientations exemplify the pseudo-left's chronic search for a left-reformist vehicle to divert the class struggle away from the fight for a revolutionary party.
