### 1. Trump says Iran talks are "going fine" as Tehran offers a two-day Hormuz reopening plan

Speaking to reporters at his Florida residence on Saturday, Trump said negotiations with Iran were "going fine but you know they're not very reliable." He said he is "not in that much of a hurry," because the US has already "knocked out their nuclear." He added: "We still have to take care of, I would say one or two sites, but we can do that in 10 minutes. You probably could look at Pickaxe Mountain." That is a suspected underground complex, and Tehran denies any nuclear work takes place there. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Trump had not decided on new strikes and that Washington remained open to a diplomatic solution.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told Al Jazeera that Iran and Qatari mediators had reached a new proposal that would cut the time needed to fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz to two days. Last month's version offered one week, conditional on the US lifting its blockade of Iranian ports, releasing frozen assets and waiving oil sanctions, and Washington rejected it. Araghchi called recent US moves toward "broader dialogue" a "positive step." He said Iran is not planning to meet US officials, and that whether talks are direct or indirect will be decided "once Washington agrees to Iran's conditions." Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has meanwhile ordered the armed forces to answer any new attack without "geographic limits" and to update their target lists.

Key facts:
- **Strike timing:** Washington has reportedly told Gulf allies to prepare for strikes as early as November 4. The White House says it does "not comment on sensitive military plans that may or may not exist" (Times of Israel).
- **Mediation:** Qatari mediators held more than 12 hours of talks in Tehran before Iranian negotiators travelled to Doha. A US official denied a report of a "preliminary breakthrough."
- **Tehran's line:** A senior Iranian official said there will be no nuclear talks and no reopening of Hormuz until the US accepts Iran's demands.
- **Blockade:** CENTCOM says the US blockade has now redirected 144 vessels, up from 138 two weeks ago. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent posted that the US has "taken out" 1.5 billion barrels of Iranian oil, without giving a timeframe, and that Gulf "transit levels are back to pre-conflict levels minus Iranian barrels."
- **Qeshm incident:** Iranian state media said a drone struck fishing boats near Qeshm Island, wounding two. Two fishermen reported missing were later found. Iran blamed the US, and CENTCOM did not respond.
- **Regional alarm:** Egyptian Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty said he is "extremely worried" about escalation.

As Kevin Reed noted on the WSWS, the war began on February 28, the day after Trump said publicly that he had made no final decision on using force. Trump's talk of no "hurry" carries the same weight.

### 2. Third weekend of revolt: 320,000 march in France as German youth protests end in riots

On Saturday, youth-led protests over living costs, education and militarism took place in France, Germany, Italy and Portugal. The French government counted about 320,000 marchers at roughly 150 demonstrations, down from 410,000 on October 24 and more than 500,000 on October 17. About 70,000 police and gendarmes were deployed. In Paris, the main march of about 70,000 was moved out of the centre onto the Boulevards des Maréchaux after a last-minute court fight. Students led it hand in hand, with CGT stewards at the front. Clashes broke out at Porte de Charenton at dusk, and police answered bottles and fireworks with stun grenades and tear gas. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez called an attack in which a riot officer was hit by a Molotov cocktail an "attempted murder." The BBC reported more than 500 arrests after clashes in Toulouse, Lyon and Marseille.

In Germany, the youth campaign *Gen Z regelt* ("Gen Z's got this") rallied in more than 30 cities against school underfunding, living costs, spending cuts and the return of military conscription. Evening clashes followed. In Frankfurt, rioters smashed shop windows, including at a gun shop, looted stores and threw Molotov cocktails at emergency services. Hesse Interior Minister Roman Poseck blamed "around 100 clearly identified left-wing extremists." He added: "We all have those images from France in our minds… France must not be held up as a model in this regard." In Portugal, police with dogs stormed the rebuilt housing camp in Lisbon's Rossio Square at 4:30 a.m. on Sunday, a day after thousands marched against the government's rent bill.

Key facts:
- **Germany:** Authorities counted up to 18,000 protesters in Frankfurt, Munich and Berlin, and German media reported tens of thousands nationwide. Berlin police reported 27 officers injured and up to 56 people detained. Hamburg police arrested 120 people after barricades were set alight, according to the broadcaster NDR.
- **The movement's demands:** "We are against compulsory military service and we are in favour of a fairer distribution of the burden," said *Gen Z regelt* spokesperson Lina Friedrich. She described the aim as fighting "for peace and freedom, rather than for war and coercion."
- **France's controls:** Place de la République and Les Halles are closed from 11 p.m. to 6 a.m., and unaccompanied minors face a 9 p.m. curfew around the Champs-Élysées. A Paris court suspended an earlier curfew on minors on Wednesday as a "serious and illegal infringement" of freedom of movement.
- **A student's conditions:** Corentin Cabreux, 21, told AP that his state-run student residence has "damp stains all along the bottom of my walls… cockroaches everywhere, rats."
- **Portugal:** Protesters had pitched more than 50 tents. Police also cleared a camp in Porto. The housing group Casa para Viver said 60 people were injured in last week's clearances. The Bank of Portugal estimates the national housing shortage at 300,000 homes, about the size of Lisbon's entire housing stock.
- **Italy:** There were clashes between police and demonstrators in Rome and Turin.

Every government involved is giving the movement the same law-and-order treatment, presenting the riots as the work of "extremists" while extending curfews and police powers. The common target of the demands is the redirection of social spending into rearmament.

### 3. Judge bars Justice Department threats against election workers as DOJ moves to segregate Alaska ballots

Late Friday, US District Judge Trina Thompson in the Northern District of California issued a temporary restraining order. It bars the Justice Department from threatening or coercing election workers to stop processing ballots, and from demanding that local officials segregate or withhold ballots the federal government suspects were cast by noncitizens. The suit was brought by labor unions and civil rights groups. It came the day after the DOJ sent letters to election officials in all 50 states threatening to prosecute and jail anyone who failed to preserve ballot records. Thompson wrote that the department's "intimidation, threats, and attempts at coercion force election workers daily to choose between acceding to defendants' unlawful demands or adhering to their duties."

In Alaska, the DOJ asked a judge on Friday to order state officials to "preserve and segregate all ballots ranking Daniel J. Sullivan," the retired teacher who shares a name with the Republican incumbent, to investigate possible voter "confusion." US Attorney Michael Heyman confirmed a criminal investigation and said reporting on the Peltola campaign had brought a "significant surge of law enforcement assets to Alaska." An FBI agent visited a boat dealer in Petersburg who had donated to Dan J. Sullivan to ask whether he had been misled. The dealer said he had not: "Everybody knows who the incumbent is." The DOJ also filed proposed questions it wants to compel the Peltola campaign and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee to answer, as part of its civil suit seeking to block certification if Sullivan loses. Trump says he told the department to file the case.

Key facts:
- **Flagged California ballots:** Two UCLA data scientists found that more than 40% of the ballots flagged by the administration carry names that do not appear on the state's voter rolls at all (AP).
- **Peltola:** "I am not interested in tricking voters," she said Thursday. The DSCC denies any involvement.
- **What is at stake:** The ballot-segregation demand would let the administration hold specific ballots in reserve for a post-election challenge in a Senate race expected to help decide control of the chamber.

Patrick Martin noted on the WSWS that the *Times* report driving the Alaska campaign rests largely on Peltola's former campaign manager, who has since retracted her comments. He also pointed out that cross-party "spoiler" manoeuvres are routine and not illegal.

### 4. Starving Oleshky residents evacuated under the first local truce of the Ukraine war

Evacuees from the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky in Kherson region crossed into Ukrainian-held territory on Saturday, at the Belarusian border in Chernihiv region. They travelled under a six-day local ceasefire mediated by US First Lady Melania Trump with help from Pope Leo XIV. It is the longest pause in fighting anywhere on the front since February 2022. Residents described months of famine after fighting and drones cut supply routes. Some said food deliveries stopped in May. "We chopped grass and squash… just to hold on," Svitlana Yermakova told Reuters. An elderly man in a wheelchair said he had lost 20kg in a year. Zelenskyy thanked the first lady and said "further steps toward de-escalation… are also needed."

Elsewhere the air war intensified. The Institute for the Study of War counted 134 Russian drones overnight on October 30–31 and 168 drones and loitering munitions during the day on October 31. Kyiv had electricity shortages on Saturday after strikes on energy infrastructure, according to the power company DTEK. Ukraine struck a defence facility in Rostov region and two refineries in Ufa. Trilateral US–Russia–Ukraine talks are planned for Abu Dhabi. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said on October 30 that preparing for them "does not mean the parties will cease fighting."

Key facts:
- **The route out:** Rather than crossing the Dnipro, evacuees had to take a long detour through occupied territory and Belarus to reach Ukrainian-held land.
- **Not everyone left:** Some Oleshky residents chose to stay behind (Al Jazeera).
- **The truce is local:** It covers only the evacuation zone, while Russian strikes continue elsewhere on the front.

### 5. India's opposition rallies in Delhi against the purge of 130 million voters

The removal of 130 million names, more than the entire electorate of most countries, is the largest disenfranchisement exercise under way anywhere. The Congress party, which ran the Indian state for most of its history, is leading the protest in order to keep it within parliamentary channels.

### 7. FBI blames North Korea for the record Binance crypto heist

On Saturday the FBI attributed the theft of "more than $6 billion USD in virtual assets" from Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, on or about October 29, to TraderTraitor. The group is believed to be part of North Korea's Lazarus Group, the outfit accused of the 2014 Sony Pictures hack. It is the largest theft in history. Binance says it has replaced 90% of the stolen tokens, and co-CEO Richard Teng says users "will not bear the financial impact." Withdrawals began resuming in phases on Sunday.

Key facts:
- **How it was done:** Teng described a "complex attack" using a compromised laptop and malware designed to delete evidence of its activity.
- **Laundering:** The FBI says the hackers are converting stolen assets into Ethereum and will launder them further through decentralised finance and instant-swap services. It urged the private sector to block transactions linked to the addresses involved.
- **Cover:** Binance set aside about $6.34 billion. The money came partly from its $1 billion Secure Asset Fund for Users, partly from its futures insurance fund and partly from its corporate treasury.
- **Market reaction:** When the hack was announced, Bitcoin fell nearly 5%, Ethereum nearly 6% and Binance's own token BNB nearly 10% (Guardian).
- **Context:** Analysts say crypto theft is a central revenue source for North Korea's sanctioned weapons programmes. The attack also adds to concern that advancing AI models could undermine cryptographic and IT security.

A single breach removed more than $6 billion from a largely unregulated exchange that sits at the centre of a market with trillions of dollars in nominal value.

### Patrick Stewart, 1940–2026

Patrick Stewart died on Wednesday at his home in Los Angeles, aged 86, after what his spokesperson called "a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia." Tributes continued through the weekend. Stewart grew up in working-class Mirfield, West Yorkshire, and made his name at the Royal Shakespeare Company before he was cast at 47 as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in *Star Trek: The Next Generation* (1987–94). He went on to four feature films, *Star Trek: Picard* (2020–23) and Professor Charles Xavier in the *X-Men* films. Ian McKellen called him "the most respected and beloved actor of his generation on either side of the Atlantic." He wrote that Stewart was "defined by being born and bred in Yorkshire… Yorkshiremen have no truck with half-truths or avoidance of big issues." Ben Kingsley recalled watching him rehearse Grumio on his first day at the RSC.

Stewart's significance went beyond his voice and presence. Picard was a humane, reasoning figure in a series that imagined a future without money or want, in which diplomacy came before force. That vision is far from Hollywood's current output of franchise cynicism and militarised superheroes. The Guardian's appreciation argues that Stewart "could make anyone feel hopeful for humanity." He also spoke publicly about the domestic violence he witnessed as a child and campaigned against it for many years.

### Jacobin / DSA

- **["Preparing for MAGA's Worst on Election Day"](https://jacobin.com/2026/10/trump-gop-midterms-election-defense)** (Stephanie Luce, Oct 31). Luce imagines Trump declaring fraud, citing Iranian "cyber interference" and invoking the Insurrection Act after a Democratic win. Her answer is that "leftists should be prepared to operate in a broad front with liberals and others." This is a popular front by name. It places the defence of democratic rights under the leadership of the Democratic Party, the party that runs 67 military-intelligence candidates and is accommodating the administration's attacks on elections. Luce also predicts "a rout of the Republicans" that even Trump's lies "won't be able to hold," which plays down the danger in the same piece that warns of it.
- **["The Iran War Might Soon Get Catastrophically Worse"](https://jacobin.com/2026/10/trump-netanyahu-iran-post-midterms-escalation)** (Branko Marcetic, Oct 31). Marcetic draws on Chris Murphy and the Quincy Institute's Trita Parsi to argue that a post-election bombing campaign will "backfire terribly" because Iran is "more dangerous and less likely to capitulate than ever." The objection is that the policy will fail. He relies on Murphy as his authority even though Murphy endorses the war's aims of stopping Iran's nuclear programme and its "support for terrorist organizations," and he never calls for working-class action against the war.
- **["Only Israel's Palestinians Vote Against Genocide"](https://jacobin.com/2026/10/israel-elections-joint-list-palestinian-arabs)** (Matan Kaminer, Oct 31). Kaminer reports that of more than 800,000 Palestinian votes, 37% went to Ra'am and 51% to the Joint List. He presents the Knesset vote as the main expression of opposition to genocide. The article stays inside the framework of the Zionist state's parliament and does not address the Jewish working class or the region.

**Assessment:** Jacobin's output is about managing the Democratic Party's victory. It warns of dangers that it then proposes to answer through the Democratic Party itself.
