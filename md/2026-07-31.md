### 1. US launches heavy wave of strikes across Iran as Tehran hits Kuwait and Jordan

US Central Command resumed large-scale air operations against Iran on Thursday after a five-day pause, striking Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps command centers, missile and drone production facilities, and coastal defense installations across Qeshm Island, Kish Island, Fars, Bushehr, Khuzestan and Bandar Abbas. The war, now in its fifth month, has drawn in a widening circle of states, with Iranian retaliation striking targets in three Gulf and Arab countries.

At least three people were killed in the strike on Qeshm Island — a married couple and their two-year-old child — with two to three more reported trapped under rubble. Iran's retaliation killed a worker in Kuwait when a Chinese-owned building was hit, and Jordanian air defenses intercepted five missiles fired at the kingdom.

Key facts:

- CENTCOM described the operation as a "heavy wave" of strikes; targets included IRGC naval assets guarding the northern approaches to the Strait of Hormuz.
- The IRGC said via the Fars news agency: "With the help of God Almighty, the aggressor will be punished today."
- Kuwait's Defense Ministry confirmed one death; Iranian missiles also struck bases in Bahrain, according to WSWS reporting.
- Brent crude swung wildly through the week, closing near $89–90 a barrel after Saudi Arabia floated a naval coalition to protect Red Sea and Hormuz shipping.
- A Quinnipiac poll released July 29 found 60 percent of US voters oppose the war and 66 percent disapprove of Trump's handling of it — the highest opposition recorded since the fighting began on February 28.

Writing on the WSWS, Jordan Shilton linked the Iran strikes to the simultaneous Ukrainian attack on an Iranian vessel in the Caspian Sea and a Russian missile crossing into Polish territory, arguing that the Middle Eastern and European wars are converging into "a single global conflict."

### 2. Trump announces Hamas disarmament agreement as Israel withholds confirmation

Trump announced Thursday that he had secured an agreement for "the COMPLETE DISARMAMENT of Hamas and all other armed groups in Gaza," presenting it as the next phase of his 20-point plan. A senior Hamas official told the BBC the organization had agreed in principle. Israel issued no public confirmation, and Israeli officials briefed reporters that they remain "deeply skeptical" Hamas will surrender weapons ahead of an Israeli withdrawal.

The plan envisions a phased Israeli pullback keyed to disarmament milestones, an International Stabilization Force, and a newly constituted Palestinian police force overseen by Trump's proposed "Board of Peace." - Gaza's Government Media Office counts at least 3,795 Israeli violations of the truce, with 1,207 Palestinians killed and 3,914 injured since October 10.
- The "International Stabilization Force" has no agreed troop contributors; Arab states have conditioned participation on a path to Palestinian statehood, which the Israeli government rejects.

### 3. Spain deploys army to Ceuta as tens of thousands cross from Morocco

Spain's government ordered the Armed Forces to reinforce the Civil Guard in the North African enclave of Ceuta after what the Interior Ministry estimated at roughly 49,000 migrants crossed from Morocco within 24 hours. At least 18 people died attempting the crossing, revised upward from an initial count of nine. Ceuta's regional government head called on Madrid to declare a national emergency, saying reception facilities have been overwhelmed.

The crisis follows a diplomatic rupture between Rabat and Madrid. Morocco is accused of deliberately relaxing border enforcement after Spain permitted Polisario Front leader Brahim Ghali to receive hospital treatment on Spanish territory — using migrants, in effect, as an instrument of state pressure.

Key facts:

- Other reports put the figure at 2,000–3,000 crossings on Thursday alone, indicating substantial uncertainty in official counts.
- Ceuta, a city of about 84,000, is one of two Spanish enclaves bordering Morocco and the EU's only land frontier with Africa apart from Melilla.
- Spain's response has been framed almost entirely as a security operation, with no announced expansion of asylum processing or humanitarian capacity.
- The deployment marks the second time in five years Spain has sent the military to Ceuta over migration.

### 4. US GDP growth slows sharply as inflation surges and a divided Fed holds rates

The Bureau of Economic Analysis advance estimate put real GDP growth at an annualized 1.5 percent in the second quarter, down from 2.1 percent in the first and well below consensus. The more striking number was the GDP price index, which jumped to 5.7 percent from 3.6 percent — the clearest stagflationary signal of the current cycle and the reason three regional Federal Reserve presidents broke ranks two days earlier.

The Federal Open Market Committee voted 9-3 on July 29 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50–3.75 percent for a fifth consecutive meeting. Beth Hammack (Cleveland), Neel Kashkari (Minneapolis) and Lorie Logan (Dallas) dissented in favor of a hike, with inflation now running above the Fed's 2 percent target for more than five years. Chair Kevin Warsh cited "elevated uncertainty" tied in part to the Middle East war.

Key facts:

- Consumer spending rose 3.2 percent annualized, up sharply from 0.5 percent in Q1 — largely reflecting price increases rather than volume.
- Imports surged 11.5 percent, driven by AI-related computer chip shipments, subtracting roughly 1.5 points from headline growth.
- Core PCE came in at 3.4 percent quarter-on-quarter, easing from 4.4 percent but still far above target; core PCE year-on-year held at 3.3 percent.
- A three-way dissent at the FOMC is rare — the last comparable split was in 2019.
- Markets have repriced to expect one to two rate *hikes* by year-end rather than cuts.
- The same Quinnipiac survey found 49 percent of voters say they are worse off financially than a year ago.

### 5. Trump tariff wave hits 60 trading partners as pharmaceutical duties reach 100 percent

A 100 percent tariff on patented pharmaceutical imports took effect Friday, July 31, with a reduced 15 percent rate for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, Liechtenstein and the UK. It follows the Section 301 tariffs of 10–12.5 percent imposed on roughly 60 trading partners that took effect July 24, and precedes an additional 50 percent duty on $20 billion of Canadian goods scheduled for August 19.

The average US tariff rate now stands at roughly 12.8 percent, the highest since World War II. Economists estimate the added cost to the average US household at about $900 in 2026, with importers passing costs to consumers rather than relocating production.

Key facts:

- The new tariffs on more than 80 countries were officially justified under forced-labor trade rules.
- Mexico's threatened tariff increase to 30 percent was delayed 90 days.
- The Canadian package covers wine, hockey sticks and a range of consumer goods, and lands weeks after Ottawa cancelled a planned joint ribbon-cutting for the Gordie Howe International Bridge over trade tensions.
- Pharmaceutical tariffs at 100 percent apply to patented drugs — a category where US consumers already pay the highest prices in the world.

### 8. Ukraine war grinds on as Russian refining capacity falls to a 21-year low

Kyiv said Russian strikes killed six people, including a child, at a supermarket; Moscow said Ukrainian strikes killed four. A Russian overnight attack on the Kyiv region injured five, including a child, and partially destroyed a home in the Brovary district. Ukrainian long-range strikes have cut Russian oil refining capacity to roughly 3.8 million barrels a day from a pre-war 5.2 million, its lowest level in 21 years. Ukraine's General Staff puts cumulative Russian personnel losses since February 2022 at approximately 1,444,810, including 1,360 in the past 24 hours — figures that cannot be independently verified but which indicate the scale of the meat grinder on both sides.

Key facts:

- Ukrainian forces also struck an Iranian-flagged vessel in the Caspian Sea, extending the war's reach into a theater directly linked to the Iran conflict.
- A Russian missile crossed into Polish territory this week, according to WSWS reporting — the kind of incident that carries direct NATO Article 5 implications.
- The degradation of Russian refining is feeding back into global fuel markets already disrupted by the closure risk in the Strait of Hormuz.

### Israel holds 70 percent of Gaza nine months into the ceasefire

Behind the "peace deal" framing dominating coverage of Trump's disarmament announcement, the material situation in Gaza has continued to deteriorate. Israeli forces now control roughly 70 percent of the territory, with the so-called "yellow line" repeatedly advanced. Aid agencies report that shifting lines of control are cutting off food and medical access for thousands of families, with entire neighbourhoods rendered inaccessible to humanitarian convoys without Israeli coordination that is frequently denied.

The casualty figures cited by Gaza's Government Media Office — 1,207 killed since October 10 — receive almost no mention in Western coverage of the negotiations, which treats the ceasefire as an accomplished fact.

### Sudan's drone war kills hundreds of civilians as coverage collapses

Sudan's civil war has escalated into a systematic air and drone campaign against civilian infrastructure. More than 277 civilians were killed in a single two-week stretch, roughly three-quarters of them by drone strikes. A strike on El Daein Teaching Hospital reportedly killed 64 people, including women and children.

The conflict — which has produced the largest displacement crisis in the world — has effectively vanished from Western front pages, receiving a fraction of the coverage devoted to Iran and Gaza. Both the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces are armed and financed by rival Gulf patrons, a dimension almost never foregrounded in mainstream accounts.

### Keiko Fujimori inaugurated in Peru with an emergency-powers program

Keiko Fujimori was sworn in as Peru's president on July 28 after winning June's runoff by fewer than 50,000 votes, becoming the country's tenth head of state in a decade. Her inaugural address pledged emergency executive powers, an "iron fist" against crime modelled on Nayib Bukele's methods in El Salvador, and construction of a megaprison on the Cecot model.

The geopolitical backdrop has shifted decisively: China now accounts for 36 percent of Peru's trade and over $30 billion in investment, against 14 percent and $6.7 billion for the United States — a displacement that makes Peru a live front in the US-China conflict in Latin America.

### UN confirms 144 dead or missing on the West African Atlantic route

The UN refugee agency recorded 144 refugees and migrants dead or missing off Nouadhibou, Mauritania, between July 14 and 18. The deadliest vessel had departed from Bufuloto, Gambia, and drifted for nearly 25 days before rescue; 38 survivors were found and 143 of those aboard are dead or missing. An EU-Mauritania agreement cut Canary Islands arrivals from 47,000 to 18,000 — an outcome the EU counts as a policy success and which, as Katerina Selin argues on the WSWS, has simply pushed crossings onto longer and more lethal routes.

### Medicaid work requirements roll out toward a January cutoff

State Medicaid agencies are required to complete member outreach between June 30 and August 31 ahead of the January implementation of federal work requirements under the "One Big Beautiful Bill Act." From January, adults aged 19 to 64 must periodically document 80 hours a month of work, schooling or volunteering to retain coverage.

Key facts:

- CBO estimates the work requirement alone will strip coverage from 5.2 million people, with 4.8 million becoming uninsured, and cut federal Medicaid spending by $326 billion over ten years.
- The Urban Institute projects 4.9 to 10.1 million losing coverage by 2028; CMS's June 2026 rule narrowing the "medical frailty" exemption is expected to push losses above original CBO estimates.
- Medicaid currently covers 74.9 million people; the Act contains $911 billion in total cuts.
- Arkansas's 2018–19 pilot terminated 18,000 people, of whom only about 1,900 ever regained coverage — the empirical baseline for what national implementation will produce.

### DRC Ebola outbreak becomes fastest-growing on record

The Ebola epidemic in the Democratic Republic of Congo, declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern by the WHO on May 16, has become the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak ever recorded. As of July 28 the DRC reported 3,360 confirmed cases and 1,487 deaths — an increase of 98 cases and 50 deaths in a single day. Ituri province accounts for 2,988 cases and 1,238 deaths across 28 of 36 health zones; North Kivu reported 11 new cases.

The outbreak passed 1,000 confirmed cases within 40 days of response activation, compared with roughly 235 days for the 2018 North Kivu outbreak to reach the same threshold. The critical complicating factor is the strain: this is *Bundibugyo* ebolavirus, for which no approved vaccine or specific treatment exists — the licensed vaccines and monoclonal antibody therapies were developed against *Zaire* ebolavirus. Health workers are left with isolation, contact tracing and supportive care. Uganda declared its own outbreak over on July 28 after no new cases since June; imported cases were previously confirmed in the United States and France.

The absence of a Bundibugyo countermeasure after decades of filovirus research is a direct product of pharmaceutical priorities: development proceeded on the strain that produced the 2014 West African epidemic and stopped there.

### US measles cases hit a 35-year high

The CDC confirmed 2,318 measles cases nationwide as of late July, already surpassing the 2,289 recorded for all of 2025 and the highest annual total since 1991 — with five months of the year still to run. Seven states account for over 84 percent of cases, led by South Carolina (670) and Utah (522). Ninety-three percent of those infected were unvaccinated.

### COVID "Nimbus" variant drives growth in 37 states

The NB.1.8.1 "Nimbus" variant, a descendant of JN.1, made up roughly 43 percent of sequenced US cases in late June. The CDC now estimates infections are growing or likely growing in 37 states with none declining, and national test positivity reached 4.8 percent as of July 12, with rising emergency department visits. The summer wave confirms that COVID has not settled into the seasonal pattern of pre-pandemic respiratory viruses.

## Arts and culture

**Glen Hansard, 1970–2026.** The Irish songwriter and actor died in a single-vehicle motorcycle crash at Strawberry Beds, Lucan, outside Dublin in the early hours of Wednesday, July 29. He was 56.

Hansard's biography is inseparable from the social conditions of 1980s Dublin. He left school at 13 and began busking on Grafton Street during a decade of mass emigration and unemployment, forming The Frames in 1990. He appeared as the guitarist Outspan Foster in Alan Parker's *The Commitments* (1991) — a film that, whatever its commercial packaging, took working-class north Dublin seriously as a subject and treated its characters' aspirations as neither quaint nor pathological.

His enduring work is *Once* (2007), John Carney's low-budget film in which Hansard and the Czech-born musician and pianist Markéta Irglová played two unnamed musicians — a Dublin street busker and a Czech immigrant selling flowers — who make a record together over a single week. The film was shot for roughly €112,000, much of it on handheld cameras with natural light, and refused nearly every convention of the romance genre: no resolution, no rescue, no upward mobility. Its power came from the precision with which it observed people who make art in the interstices of wage labour, and from the fact that the songs were performed rather than mimed. "Falling Slowly" won the 2008 Academy Award for Best Original Song, and Irglová, cut off mid-speech by the orchestra, was brought back on stage by host Jon Stewart to finish — one of the few genuinely unscripted moments in the ceremony's recent history. Hansard continued to record and tour, appearing regularly at benefit performances for Dublin housing campaigns and for refugees.

**Venice unveils its 83rd edition.** The Venice Film Festival announced its 2026 lineup for the September 2–12 festival. Danny Boyle's *Ink*, on the rise of Rupert Murdoch, opens the festival, having already been acquired by Netflix before its premiere — an arrangement that says a good deal about the current relationship between festival prestige and streaming capital. The competition is unusually weighted toward directors of long standing: Lee Chang-dong, Tsai Ming-liang, Werner Herzog, Lav Diaz, Paul Schrader, Wim Wenders, Hirokazu Kore-eda, Shinya Tsukamoto, Nanni Moretti, Julia Loktev, Mariano Llinás, Luca Guadagnino, Martin McDonagh, and documentarians Laura Poitras and Robert Greene.
