### 1. Trump escalates Iran war as US death toll climbs

The US war on Iran, which resumed on July 11 after Trump declared the June ceasefire "over," entered its ninth consecutive night of strikes over the weekend. US Central Command said the bombing is "designed to further degrade Iran's ability to threaten commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz and swiftly punish Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps forces." Targets have widened from military installations to civilian infrastructure — bridges, railways, ports and the power grid — enforced by a naval blockade.

American casualties are mounting. Two US soldiers were killed and one initially reported missing in an Iranian missile strike on a base inside Jordan on July 17-18; a third was killed in Iraq during a controlled detonation of ordnance from a downed Iranian drone. The *Washington Post* put the cumulative toll at "17, possibly 18, dead and more than 430 wounded" since the war began February 28. As the WSWS Perspective by Andre Damon noted, "no Pentagon spokesman has taken questions on the war since May 5," underscoring the secrecy around the true toll.

The *Wall Street Journal* reported July 15 that Trump "is leaning toward expanding U.S. military operations," including sending ground forces to seize Iranian islands near the Strait of Hormuz. A White House Situation Room meeting reportedly discussed seizing Kharg Island — which handles up to 90 percent of Iran's oil exports and sits 20 miles off the mainland.

Key facts:
- Iran retains substantial retaliatory capacity: it struck a Kuwaiti power-and-water plant on consecutive days, hit HIMARS launchers in Kuwait, and struck air bases in Bahrain and Jordan.
- A US official told the *Post* the Pentagon is running low on air-defense and long-range strike missiles: "We do not have enough to safely sustain operations, and I don't think the White House is aware of that."
- The *WSJ* reported Iran's precision targeting has raised US suspicions of Chinese or Russian assistance.
- Damon cites Seymour Hersh's June 10 report that Trump asked whether using low-yield nuclear weapons against Iran's underground missile plants "was doable."

### 2. Russia hits Kyiv with one of the war's largest ballistic barrages

Russia battered Kyiv overnight July 18-19 with one of the largest ballistic missile barrages of the war, killing at least one person and wounding 16, according to President Volodymyr Zelensky. Russia launched 41 missiles — including 25 ballistic missiles — and 125 attack drones across Ukraine. Ukrainian forces intercepted 108 drones and 18 missiles, but the volume of ballistic missiles exposed persistent holes in Kyiv's air defenses.

Residential buildings, warehouses, a supermarket and a dormitory were damaged across several districts, with three people in serious condition. Recent attacks on Kyiv and its surrounding metropolitan area have killed at least 60 people since the start of July.

Key facts:
- The strike came amid NATO-funded Ukrainian deep strikes on Russian cities, part of what Damon's Perspective calls a single "conflagration" alongside the Iran war.
- Ukrainian air defense interceptor stocks remain a chronic vulnerability, dependent on US and European resupply now competing with Middle East demand.

### 3. Germany joins French nuclear exercise, breaking with post-war order

Chancellor Friedrich Merz announced after defense talks with President Emmanuel Macron at an air base near Cologne that German conventional forces will participate in a French nuclear exercise before year's end — the first time Germany has ever taken part in such a drill. The two governments agreed to establish a permanent nuclear steering group and deeper integration into France's nuclear doctrine. As the WSWS's Ulrich Rippert wrote, this marks "a historic rupture with Germany's post-war order."

Merz said "the world we live in today requires new answers," while insisting the cooperation is "not a move to replace NATO's nuclear umbrella." Macron said Germany would play a "vanguard role" in European deterrence.

Key facts:
- Previous German leaders had refused French offers of nuclear cooperation; the reversal reflects growing European "strategic autonomy" amid doubts over US commitments.
- The move follows last month's collapse of the joint Franco-German FCAS fighter-jet project.
- Rippert links the shift to a 32.7% German defense budget increase, funded through welfare cuts.

### Chile's economy contracts for fifth straight month

Chile's economy has now contracted for five consecutive months, with GDP down 0.9% year-over-year in May. Unemployment stands at 9.4%, with informal jobs rising sharply as formal employment falls. ### First BHP iron ore strike in Pilbara in decades

Around 200 workers — roughly 44% of BHP's 450-strong Port Hedland terminal workforce — walked off the job on July 16 in the first protected industrial action at the site since 2000, following seven months of failed enterprise bargaining. The central demand is pay parity: workers seek a $25,000 annual increase to close remuneration gaps of up to A$40,000 versus other BHP Pilbara sites, where a 16% raise was already secured. The eight-hour stoppage cost an estimated A$40-50 million in disrupted iron ore revenue against daily throughput of roughly A$120 million, with a Fair Work Commission session set for July 21. But the Mining and Energy Union organized only a symbolic "family-friendly BBQ" rather than blocking operations, allowing all seven scheduled ships to load. The WSWS's Martin Scott called for independent rank-and-file committees to wrest control from a union bureaucracy it describes as "an industrial police force."

### Philippines reclassified as upper-middle-income economy

The World Bank reclassified the Philippines as an upper-middle-income economy, up from lower-middle-income status. While Manila trumpeted the upgrade as validation of its growth model, the reclassification masks deep social inequality, mass labor export and remittance dependence, and precarious informal employment for tens of millions. The threshold change is driven largely by nominal GNI per-capita figures rather than any real improvement in workers' living standards.

### US measles cases near record, elimination status at risk

As of July 16, the CDC reports 2,260 confirmed US measles cases in 2026, with 93% (2,108) outbreak-associated across 44 jurisdictions. South Carolina (670), Utah (490) and Texas (182) lead case counts, and 93% of cases occurred in unvaccinated individuals or those of unknown vaccination status. The US is on pace to lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000 when PAHO conducts its formal review in November 2026. The resurgence is a direct consequence of declining vaccination rates, amplified by the anti-science measures and public-health defunding advanced under the Trump administration and its HHS leadership.

### H5N1 reaches Australia for the first time

As of June 30, Australia confirmed its first-ever H5N1 detections — five wild-bird cases in Western and South Australia — meaning clade 2.3.4.4b has now reached every continent. Globally, more than 180 million poultry have been culled and over 1,000 US dairy farms have reported cattle infections; 1,022 cumulative human cases have been recorded worldwide since 1997. ### Brenda Fricker, first Irish woman to win an Academy Award, dies at 81

Irish actress Brenda Fricker died July 16 in Dublin after a period of ill health, aged 81. She became the first Irish woman to win an Academy Award, taking Best Supporting Actress for her portrayal of Bridget Fahy Brown, mother of the disabled artist Christy Brown, in Jim Sheridan's 1989 film *My Left Foot*. Fricker's performance was rooted in an unsentimental realism — she conveyed the exhaustion, tenderness and dignity of a working-class Dublin mother raising a son written off by society, without a trace of condescension. Over a career spanning stage, television (*Casualty*) and film, she represented a tradition of humane, socially grounded acting increasingly marginalized in an industry dominated by franchise spectacle. Her passing, following those of Sam Neill and Bonnie Tyler earlier in the month, marks the loss of performers who came up through repertory and popular traditions that valued craft over celebrity branding.
