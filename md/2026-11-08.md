### 1. US launches Task Force Iron Gate to choke Iran's land and Caspian routes

US Central Command announced "Task Force Iron Gate" on Saturday. The Tampa-based unit will try to stop Iran moving weapons and materials across its land borders and the Caspian Sea. This takes the months-long naval blockade of Iranian ports onto land. The Caspian is Iran's direct link to Russia and Kazakhstan, and its land borders run with Iraq, Turkey, the South Caucasus, Turkmenistan, Afghanistan and Pakistan. Enforcing the task force's mandate would therefore bring US forces into friction with trade involving Russia and China, not only Iran.

The announcement came on the eve of November 9. US officials told Fox News that night's new moon made it favourable for operations, and the White House has been holding war-planning meetings. Qatari-mediated talks are reportedly continuing.

Key facts:
- **Cooper:** CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper said the task force reinforces "the success of our ironclad blockade against Iran at sea."
- **Interdictions:** Since July, US forces have redirected 147 commercial vessels and disabled five that refused to comply. On Saturday CENTCOM said it fired on the stern of the St. Kitts-flagged cargo vessel M/V Huda in the Gulf of Oman to disable it as it tried to break the blockade.
- **Explosions in Iran:** IRNA reported blasts on Qeshm Island and in Jask, Sirik and Bandar Abbas, all on the Strait of Hormuz coast. It did not say what caused them, and no casualties were reported.
- **Mines:** The IRGC Navy said two oil tankers struck sea mines near Hormuz. Maritime authorities had not independently confirmed this.
- **Rescue:** The UAE said it rescued 22 people from a cargo ship that caught fire after the mine reports.

### 2. Far-right gunman kills six in Krakow's main square

At about 3 pm on Saturday a man drove onto Krakow's Main Market Square and fired at random people from his car with an AR-15-style semi-automatic rifle. He killed six people, four women and two men, and wounded 15, including police officers and foreign nationals. A British national was among the dead. Police shot the suspect and detained him. He is a Polish citizen and is believed to have acted alone. Three bodies, two women and a man, were later found at his home. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said they appeared to be his immediate family.

Interior Minister Marcin Kierwinski said the suspect appeared "fascinated by far-right ideologies" and held ultra-nationalist views. Tusk called the attack terrorism and declared two days of national mourning. Poland's government has spent years promoting nationalism and one of the largest rearmament programmes in Europe, and the official response so far has passed over the political climate that this produces.

Key facts:
- **The attack:** The suspect wore military-style clothing, and several more weapons were found in his car. He returned fire during his arrest and wounded two officers, one of them critically.
- **Precedent:** This is Poland's deadliest shooting since the fall of Stalinism in 1989. It follows a wave of stabbings in October, including three school attacks in less than a week, which Tusk called a "cult of violence."
- **Firearms:** Poland issued more than 50,000 firearms licences in 2025, bringing the total to 411,000.
- **Reaction:** Condolences came from President Karol Nawrocki, UK Prime Minister Andy Burnham, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas and Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Polish officials have repeatedly blamed Russia for sabotage on Polish soil, but have made no such claim about this attack.

### 3. Herzog picks Eisenkot to form Israel's next government

President Isaac Herzog gave Gadi Eisenkot, the former chief of staff, the mandate to form a government after the October 27 election. He passed over Benjamin Netanyahu, whose right-wing and religious bloc remains the largest at 53 seats. Eisenkot has 28 days to assemble a coalition, with a possible 14-day extension.

Eisenkot's endorsement by 67 Knesset members depends on Arab-led parties, and he has ruled out governing with them. Joint List leader Ahmad Tibi said he expects a minority government that rests on their "external support." That would be a government kept in office by Palestinian citizens' votes but closed to their participation. Netanyahu has gone on the attack: "Such a government, dependent on Islamists, will dismantle the state of Israel from within."

Key facts:
- **Mandate:** Eisenkot was nominated with 67 endorsements to Netanyahu's 46.
- **Netanyahu's leverage:** If no government is formed, the Knesset dissolves and new elections follow, with Netanyahu staying on as caretaker prime minister.
- **Continuity:** Eisenkot led the army during the Gaza war. The reporting gives no sign that his coalition talks involve ending the occupation or the war.

### 4. France's cost-of-living protests enter a fourth weekend as unions claim 250,000

For the fourth weekend running, protests against rising living costs spread across France on Saturday. The Interior Ministry counted about 146,000 marchers nationwide, 46,000 of them in Paris. Unions claimed 250,000, with 75,000 in the capital. Days earlier, an estimated 575,000 joined the union day of action on November 5. The movement began in mid-September over conditions in under-resourced high schools and has since taken in wages, fuel prices and public services. Students still lead it, and workers from several sectors have joined.

The government responded with force. It deployed about 70,000 security personnel, and Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said authorities were "ready and waiting" for rioters. Police used tear gas and batons in Paris, saying about 200 people attacked officers who were arresting a man for setting fire to a bin. In Lyon they used tear gas on several hundred people blocking a road.

Key facts:
- **Mélenchon's launch:** Jean-Luc Mélenchon presented his programme to about 17,000 supporters on Saturday. It includes retirement at 60, a €1,700 monthly minimum wage, leaving NATO, partial renationalisation, a 2 percent minimum tax on billionaires' wealth and an end to repaying France's debt to the ECB.
- **Polls:** Mélenchon polls at 17.5–18 percent and leads the left. Marine Le Pen leads overall, and an October Toluna Harris Interactive poll had her beating Mélenchon 70 percent to 30 in a run-off.
- **The government's line:** Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu on the debt proposal: "Borrowing money and deciding not to give it back – that's called theft."
- **The unions:** Al Jazeera's correspondent reports that union leaderships, "weakened in recent years," feel "renewed momentum." The unions are pressing the government for higher pay and better facilities.

The movement's demands are fuelling an election campaign whose two front-runners are a far-right nationalist and a left-nationalist reformist.

### 6. Saudi-led strikes kill 33 near Sanaa as Houthis fire missiles at Riyadh

Houthi Health Ministry spokesperson Anees al-Asbahi said coalition air attacks on al-Shariyah village, near Sanaa, killed 33 people, including eight women and 13 children, and wounded at least 39. On Sunday the Saudi-led coalition said its air defences destroyed three Houthi ballistic missiles, two aimed at Riyadh and one at the oil-producing Eastern Province.

The fighting is part of the government's "Dawn of Yemen" offensive to retake Houthi-held territory, including Sanaa. In September the Houthis seized much of the Red Sea coast, and the government campaign began in October.

Key facts:
- **Houthi claims:** Military spokesperson Yahya Saree said the coalition carried out 105 air and missile strikes across Sanaa and seven other governorates in 24 hours, and 4,369 since the escalation began. - **UN figures:** More than 1,600 people have been killed and nearly 300,000 displaced since the 2022 ceasefire collapsed in July, when the Houthis declared a blockade of Saudi ports.

A drone attack on a military base in Santa Rosa del Sur, in the foothills of the San Lucas range in northern Colombia, killed three soldiers and wounded at least 16. ### El Hierro declares an emergency after 3,300 migrant arrivals in a week

El Hierro, the smallest of the Canary Islands with about 11,400 residents, declared an emergency after more than 3,300 people arrived in about 30 boats since early November. Maritime rescue services rescued 3,261 people off the Canaries in the first six days of the month. Regional leader Fernando Clavijo called the situation "absolutely unsustainable" and said the pace is nearing that of the 2024 record, when more than 46,000 people arrived.

- **Deaths:** Two people were found dead on a boat this week, and survivors told AFP that five more probably died during the voyage. At least nine people have died off the islands in recent weeks.
- **Conditions:** Alpidio Armas, head of the island council, said conditions at the hospital have become "undesirable," and officials are looking for temporary housing. Buses have been diverted to migration operations and medical staff brought in from other islands.
- **EU border policy:** Until this surge, arrivals from January to October were down 5.5 percent year on year at 13,327. Al Jazeera attributes the earlier drop to tighter surveillance and deals with origin and transit states. Patrols off Senegal, Mauritania and Morocco have pushed departures south to The Gambia, Guinea-Bissau and Guinea, making the Atlantic route longer and more lethal.

### Russian diesel tankers turn away from the US

The diesel arrangement Trump announced after his October 9 call with Putin is coming apart before any fuel has arrived. Trump had promised more than 300,000 tonnes immediately, 500,000 tonnes in November and then 1 million tonnes, under a temporary OFAC exemption that runs until April 7. Russia has sent four loaded tankers. According to Kpler and MarineTraffic, the *Talisman* and *Leruo* left Primorsk declaring the US East Coast as their destination and are now heading for Brazil and the Mediterranean. A third changed its destination from New York to "for orders." The *Financial Times* reports that the sanctioned tanker *Beast* is signalling "Atlantic."

Congress's statutory ban on Russian energy imports remains in force. Lawmakers have warned banks, ports and distributors of possible civil or criminal liability, and New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill ordered port and terminal operators to turn the ships away. "It's clear there's hesitancy here in bringing these barrels onshore," Kpler's Matt Smith told AP. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov insisted "all the diesel will find its buyers," and Putin envoy Kirill Dmitriev called the reports "fake." Workers bear the cost of the war-driven fuel shortage: US retail diesel topped $6 a gallon in September, nearly double pre-war levels.

- **Scale:** Aarathi Krishnan of Raksha Intelligence estimates the first shipment equals about two hours of US distillate consumption.
- **Terms:** Neither government has disclosed whether the deal is in writing, or its price, payment terms or the companies involved.

### Human brain tissue grows to half the brain volume of engineered mice

A Stanford team led by psychiatrist Sergiu Pasca reports in *Nature* that it engineered mice to develop without most of the cerebral cortex and much of the hippocampus. At two days old, the mice were injected with human brain organoids, clusters of neural cells grown in the lab from stem cells. Transplants took in 25 of 29 mice. Within three months the human tissue had grown about fivefold, connected to the mouse blood supply, filled more than 90 percent of the empty cavities and made up roughly half the brain's volume.

- **Function:** The mice walked and vocalised mostly normally, and they did better in a maze than mice without the grafts, which suggests the human tissue contributed to cognition. Some human cells formed von Economo neurons, which are rare and normally absent in mice.
- **Disease modelling:** Mice exposed to low oxygen showed brain damage and impaired gait, which could give researchers a model for cerebral palsy. The authors also point to schizophrenia and dementia.
- **Ethics:** The team ended the experiments when the human cells reached six months, before they formed wider connections with the mouse brain. UC San Diego sociologist John Evans said the study is not alarming in itself, but the field has no clear "stopping points" between mice and non-human primates.

The work is a major technical achievement in studying human brain development. Whether research like it continues to be done openly and for public benefit depends on scientific institutions that are now being defunded and placed under political control.

### *Lincoln in the Bardo* at the Metropolitan Opera

Missy Mazzoli's opera of George Saunders's 2017 Booker Prize-winning novel premiered at the Met on October 19 and is still drawing strong reviews. It runs alongside Cherubini's *Medea* and Puccini's *Tosca*. The novel is set in a Georgetown cemetery in February 1862, on the night after the burial of Lincoln's 11-year-old son Willie. Lincoln's private grief becomes the personal side of a national bloodletting over slavery.

New American work rarely enters the Met's mainstream repertory. This premiere comes as the federal government guts arts funding, and as the history of the Civil War and Reconstruction is being rewritten in museums and classrooms. Its success with audiences shows a hunger for serious art about historical catastrophe and collective loss.
