### 1. Iran-US ceasefire teeters as IRGC strikes vessel in Strait of Hormuz

A fragile ceasefire between the United States and Iran came under severe strain on June 25 when Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) struck a Singapore-flagged cargo vessel with a drone near the Oman coast. The ship sustained bridge damage but reported no injuries and continued its voyage. The attack came eight days after Washington and Tehran signed a June 17 memorandum of understanding (MOU) establishing a 60-day window to negotiate a comprehensive deal — including the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and a freeze on Iran's nuclear program — following the war earlier this year that has been dubbed the "Twelve-Day War."

The strike underscored that the reopening of the world's most important oil chokepoint remains contested. Maritime traffic had surged 105% on June 24, with 70 ships transiting, but three tankers reversed course after Iran warned against unauthorized routes, leaving shipping firms in what one report called a "limbo period." Secretary of State Marco Rubio warned that Iranian transit tolls would spread "like a contagion" to other waterways. Oman, a key mediator, stated future arrangements "will not involve imposing any transit fees," but Tehran remained deliberately ambiguous and lashed out at a joint US–Gulf Cooperation Council statement as containing "interventionist, irresponsible and provocative positions."

In Washington, the White House submitted a supplemental funding request of $87.6 billion for the war — down from the $200 billion initially sought in March — of which $67.15 billion goes to the Defense Department. As Kevin Reed noted on the WSWS, the Senate's nonbinding Iran War Powers Resolution was dismissed by the administration as "unconstitutional and therefore unenforceable," while Democratic objections focused narrowly on "unclear strategy or legal basis" rather than opposing the war itself — exposing a bipartisan war consensus.

Key facts:
- Brent crude fell 3.8% to $73.87/barrel; US crude dropped 3.9% to $70.34, approaching pre-war levels
- Under the MOU, Iran agreed to reopen Hormuz, readmit IAEA inspectors, and negotiate downblending its highly enriched uranium stockpile
- IAEA chief Rafael Grossi said "a very strong verification system" is essential; inspectors expect access soon
- VP JD Vance called IAEA access "a major milestone... and the first step in permanently denuclearizing Iran"
- The funding package was padded with farm aid ($11.1 billion), Ebola response, and infrastructure money to build cross-party support
- The IMF projects global growth slowing to 2.5% in 2026, citing the Middle East conflict's energy-price and supply effects

### 2. Venezuela earthquake "doublet" kills hundreds; USGS warns of thousands dead

A rare seismic "doublet" devastated northern Venezuela on the evening of June 24. A Mw 7.2 foreshock struck near San Felipe, 284 km west of Caracas, at 6:04 pm local time, followed just 39 seconds later by a Mw 7.5 mainshock near Yumare — the strongest earthquake to hit the country in 125 years. At least 188 to 235 people are confirmed dead, with between 1,520 and more than 4,300 injured and at least 200 reported trapped or missing. The US Geological Survey issued its highest-level casualty alert, warning of a "substantial probability" that the death toll could exceed 10,000.

The scale of destruction reflects deep structural vulnerability. Roughly 80% of Venezuela's population lives in earthquake-prone zones, much of it in informal housing built without any seismic standards. Simón Bolívar International Airport in Caracas was severely damaged and closed, and dozens of buildings collapsed in the capital's Altamira neighborhood. Interim President Delcy Rodríguez said "dozens of buildings have collapsed, and we are currently carrying out very intense rescue efforts." Search-and-rescue teams from the US, Mexico, China, India and Brazil are en route.

As Andrea Lobo wrote on the WSWS, the catastrophe is "not merely a product of natural forces" but of decades of structural vulnerability — US sanctions that blocked importation of construction materials and medical equipment, Chavista failure to modernize infrastructure despite oil revenues, and the US invasion of Venezuela in January 2026. The Trump administration's offer of earthquake aid ("The U.S.A. stands ready, willing, and able to help!") is read as a potential pretext for occupation, with Haiti's 2010 earthquake cited as the historical parallel.

Key facts:
- WSWS reports over 37,600 missing-person reports filed
- The mainshock-foreshock pairing within 39 seconds makes this a textbook "doublet" event
- Many of Venezuela's collapsed structures lacked any seismic reinforcement

Russian forces, meanwhile, attacked Ukraine overnight June 24–25 with 90 long-range drones and one Iskander-M ballistic missile; Ukraine claimed a 93.26% interception rate, downing 83 of the 90 drones.

Russian soldier desertion requests have risen sharply, correlated with the stalling advance. The class dimension — largely absent from mainstream coverage — is that Russian conscripts drawn from poor rural and ethnic-minority regions are dying for oligarchic interests, while Ukrainian workers bear the burden of a war the Western powers are prolonging to weaken Russia strategically.

### 5. Supreme Court rules 6–3 that asylum seekers can be turned away at the border

In a 6–3 ruling on June 25 in *Mullin v. Al Otro Lado*, the US Supreme Court held that migrants who present themselves to US officials while still standing on Mexican soil cannot claim asylum, because they have not technically "arrived in" the United States. Writing for the conservative majority, Justice Samuel Alito reasoned that because asylum seekers are not physically in the US when turned away, the legal protections under federal law have not yet activated.

Justice Sonia Sotomayor delivered an oral dissent from the bench, stating that the ruling "regrettably and tragically extinguishes the light of the torch of the Statue of Liberty." The decision overturned lower-court orders that had blocked the administration's cap on daily asylum appointments — a metering practice that originated under the Obama administration and was expanded under Trump.

The court issued a second immigration ruling the same day, blocking recipients of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) from seeking judicial relief — removing judicial oversight of executive detention and deportation decisions. The two rulings together effectively nullify asylum rights for tens of thousands fleeing conditions created or worsened by decades of US intervention, while weaponizing the legal fiction of non-arrival against the most vulnerable layers of the working class.

### 6. US factory job cuts hit worst level since 2008 financial crisis

S&P Global's June 2026 manufacturing PMI report, released June 23, found that US factory employment cuts reached their highest rate since the 2008–09 financial crisis, excluding the COVID-19 lockdowns of early 2020. It is the third month in the past four with crisis-level factory job cuts. S&P attributed the trend to "concerns over the sustainability of recent demand alongside worries over escalating raw material costs."

The data lands amid a fresh wave of layoffs. Lucid Motors announced its second mass layoff of 2026 on Monday, cutting roughly 1,500 workers — 18% of its workforce — as the US EV market cools. Yet June's headline flash manufacturing PMI rose to 55.7, a 49-month high. The paradox is resolved by the fact that the gain was driven almost entirely by corporate stockpiling — manufacturers front-loading inventory to hedge against Middle East supply-chain disruption and tariff exposure — rather than genuine consumer demand.

Key facts:
- BLS data shows manufacturing employment up 23,000 in 2026 overall, masking severe churn and displacement at the factory-floor level
- The simultaneous rise in the PMI and collapse in factory employment illustrates how capitalist headline metrics obscure mass working-class displacement
- Workers are bearing the direct costs of a trade war and a Middle East conflict fought, in part, to control energy supply chains

### Germany launches frontal assault on pensions

The Merz-Klingbeil government's pension commission presented 33 reform recommendations on June 23, endorsed by Chancellor Friedrich Merz, which the WSWS describes as "the most massive attack on pensions ever undertaken by a German government." The package links the statutory retirement age to life expectancy from 2031, raising it from 67 to 67.5 between 2031 and 2041 and leaving the door open to 70 by 2092; abolishes penalty-free early retirement at 63 (raising the minimum to 64); and ends the block-model partial-retirement scheme.

The most far-reaching element is a Swedish-style mandatory capital-funded pension: workers and employers would each contribute an additional 2% of gross wages — on top of the current 18.6% rate — into a new state-run investment fund channeling some 30 billion euros annually into financial markets. As Marianne Arens and Peter Schwarz note on the WSWS, this feeds "a bubble that must inevitably burst," and the reform's logic is explicit redistribution from workers to fund rearmament — civil servants and ministers retain separate systems providing 70% of final salary, versus 48% for regular workers. Labor unions and opposition parties have criticized the plan; the commission aims to approve it before parliament's summer recess.

## Arts and culture

The intersection of art and the politics of war and historical memory dominates the cultural calendar this week. At the Australian War Memorial's reopening — analyzed in the WSWS coverage section below — the state's embrace of an alleged war criminal as a guest of honor underscored how official culture is being mobilized to sanitize imperial violence. A counterpoint emerges in documentary cinema: Academy Award winner Alex Gibney's *Knife: The Attempted Murder of Salman Rushdie*, inspired by the novelist's memoir of the 2022 stabbing that cost him an eye, confronts the long shadow of religious-reactionary violence against artistic freedom and the fatwa that has hung over Rushdie since 1989. The film's premiere this year keeps in view a question the cultural establishment prefers to bury: the defense of free expression against both theocratic and state repression.

Festival season is at its height across the United States. The 23rd Rochester International Jazz Festival (June 19–27) is presenting more than 300 shows with over 1,750 artists, including Gladys Knight on June 26 and the Count Basie Orchestra on June 27 — a living link to the Black American musical tradition that arose out of the Great Migration and the social struggles of the 20th century. The 25th-anniversary Tribeca Festival has showcased work from both established and first-time filmmakers, while regional festivals from Utah to upstate New York continue to provide some of the few remaining venues for independent and working artists outside the streaming conglomerates.

Roundup:
- **Documentary:** Gibney's *Knife* situates the Rushdie attack within decades of clerical incitement and the failure of liberal institutions to defend writers under threat — a theme of renewed urgency amid rising censorship.
- **Music:** The persistence of large free and low-cost jazz festivals reflects genuine popular demand for live communal art even as recorded-music revenue is captured by a handful of platforms; performers like Gladys Knight carry a repertoire forged in the civil-rights era.
- **Visual arts and memory:** The reopening of national war memorials across the Anglosphere is increasingly an exercise in militarist myth-making, recasting colonial and imperial wars as occasions for solemn national pride.
- **Cultural economy:** The contrast between SpaceX's $75 billion IPO and the defunding of public cultural and postal infrastructure exemplifies the diversion of social wealth from the commons to private capital — a pressure felt acutely by museums, libraries and performing-arts institutions facing budget cuts.

The throughline is the deepening conflict between art as a vehicle for truth-telling about war, repression and social life, and the drive of the ruling class to subordinate culture to nationalism, spectacle and the market. Genuine artistic achievement — whether in Rushdie's literary defiance or in the jazz tradition's improvisational humanism — retains its force precisely because it resists that subordination.

## WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

**Perspective published:** "The American Revolution and Its Place in History: From the War Against Monarchy to 'No Kings'" by David North — read in full on wsws.org (published June 26, 2026).

These are North's introductory remarks to a WSWS webinar on the American Revolution, delivered amid the mass "No Kings" protest movement against Trumpian authoritarianism. North argues the current democratic crisis cannot be understood without recovering a materialist analysis of the bourgeois democratic revolution of 1776, whose "world-historical significance" he anchors in Tom Paine's declaration that "the cause of America" was "the cause of all mankind." He polemicizes against two convergent tendencies: patriotic mythologizing, and the dominant left mode of dismissing the founding documents as mere propaganda masking the exploitation of the enslaved, Indigenous peoples, women and propertyless laborers. North accepts those exclusions as fact but insists the proper response is class analysis, not moral denunciation — offering the memorable analogy that to call the Declaration mere cover for ruling-class interests is like "claiming that Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel to cover up a crack." He warns against the 1619 Project's substitution of race for class as "history's central axis," which (1) reduces the agency of the working class, (2) severs socialism from the democratic gains workers have inherited, and (3) disarms workers politically. His four-part analytical framework — what was revolutionary, what was limited, what was betrayed, and what remains unresolved — is offered as the Trotskyist method for approaching all bourgeois revolutions. The political conclusion: without historical depth and socialist leadership, the "No Kings" movement will remain captive to the Democratic Party.

### "Round up all the new hires and tell them to vote yes": UAW holds shotgun vote at Nexteer — Jerry White

After three previous contract rejections at Nexteer Automotive in Saginaw, Michigan, the UAW moved the ratification vote from the union hall — where opposition had organized — onto the factory floor, with officials instructing supervisors to "round up all the new hires and tell them to vote yes." Officials simultaneously threatened that a strike would be illegal under the extended contract, defying an 86% worker mandate for a strike vote. The proposed deal offers starting pay of $19.50/hour rising to only $27 by 2030, with a cost-of-living allowance that activates only if year-over-year inflation exceeds 7.5% — a threshold met just once in 25 years. White names UAW President Shawn Fain, Region 1D Director Steve Dawes and International Servicing Rep Jason Tuck as coordinating with management over three months — including threats of plant closure — to wear down resistance. The article is a concrete illustration of the gulf between Fain's militant public image and the apparatus's role in suppressing the rank and file, and underscores the WSWS call for independent rank-and-file committees.

### Detroit Federation of Teachers pushes through contract as closures continue — Phyllis Steele

The Detroit Federation of Teachers ratified a contract touting a $100,100 top salary by 2027–28 and special-education bonuses — but Superintendent Nikolai Vitti revealed the district accelerated school closures specifically "to generate more revenue to address the need to increase salaries," meaning pay gains come at the direct cost of educational infrastructure. Much of the new compensation is structured as bonuses that do not count toward pensions, and special-education staffing shortfalls are met not by hiring but by paying existing teachers extra to absorb additional students with disabilities. Steele situates this within a national pattern: over half of America's 50 largest districts face budget cuts, overwhelmingly under Democratic administrations with union complicity. The article calls for independent rank-and-file educator committees demanding a halt to school closures and full compensation restoration.

### Australian ruling elite fawns over alleged war criminal Ben Roberts-Smith — Oscar Grenfell

After 51 days of workers' protests, President Rodrigo Paz declared a "state of exception" suspending demonstration rights and authorizing military deployment — hours after the Central Obrera Boliviana (COB) signed a "pacification" agreement with Paz, ending the blockades just as military operations began. ### Call for inquiry into dropping of charges over New Zealand's Pike River mine disaster — Tom Peters

Former Pike River Coal CEO Peter Whittall had criminal charges dropped in December 2013 after WorkSafe accepted a $3.41 million payment from an unnamed insurer to victims' families — an arrangement a 2017 Supreme Court ruling found unlawful — yet charges were never reinstated. ### Alan Greenspan (1926–2026) and the crisis of capitalism — Nick Beams

Beams marks Greenspan's death at 100 by arguing his 19-year Federal Reserve chairmanship (1987–2006) was defined by the ideological promotion of financial deregulation — championing repeal of Glass-Steagall and blocking derivatives regulation — and the "Greenspan put," which guaranteed Fed rescue of markets after crashes, creating systematic moral hazard and incentivizing escalating speculation. The 2008 crash that followed saw roughly 10 million Americans lose their homes while banks received hundreds of billions in bailouts with no executive prosecutions; Greenspan himself later admitted to Congress that his intellectual framework had "collapsed." Beams concludes that Greenspan did not create capitalism's contradictions but accelerated them, facilitating a financial oligarchy that now faces an existential reckoning.
