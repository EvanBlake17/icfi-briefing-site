### 2. Magnitude 7.7 earthquake strikes Indonesia's Flores island

A shallow magnitude 7.7 earthquake struck off Flores island in East Nusa Tenggara province at 5:58am local time Saturday (21:58 GMT Friday), collapsing buildings and triggering a tsunami warning across four provinces. The USGS put the depth at 10 kilometres, with the epicentre about 68 kilometres north-northwest of Ende.

Indonesia's Meteorology, Climatology and Geophysics Agency (BMKG) issued tsunami warnings for East Nusa Tenggara, West Nusa Tenggara, South Sulawesi and Southeast Sulawesi. Hundreds of residents of Maumere fled to higher ground before the alert was lifted. Early figures from the Maumere Search and Rescue Agency put the toll at two dead and two injured, but communications with outlying districts remain incomplete and the count is expected to rise.

Key facts:

- **Three aftershocks within 30 minutes**, measuring M5.9, M5.6 and M6.1.
- The region has a catastrophic history: the 1992 Flores earthquake and tsunami killed some 2,500 people; the 2021 Flores Sea quake caused widespread damage across the same island chain.
- The quake is the second major seismic event in the same 24-hour window: a **magnitude 4.8** earthquake damaged buildings in Granada province, Spain.

### 8. Indiana declares statewide emergency after 1-in-1,000-year rainfall

Governor Mike Braun declared a statewide disaster emergency and mobilized the Indiana National Guard on Thursday, roughly 36 hours after eastern Indiana absorbed a rainfall event the National Weather Service classified in the **1-in-1,000-year range**. Parts of the state received 6 to nearly 12 inches of rain in a week onto ground already saturated.

By Friday morning the **White River had surged past 24 feet**, breaking the 23-foot crest recorded during Indiana's historic 1913 flood. Flash flooding continued across Indiana and Illinois for a sixth consecutive day, with roughly **36 million people** under flood alerts across the Midwest. The flooding followed a deadly derecho — an unusually long-lived windstorm — that tore through the region a day earlier.

Key facts:

- National Guard units were directed to Gary, Munster and Hobart in the state's industrial northwest, among the poorest urban areas in the Midwest.
- The event is the latest in a pattern of "once-in-a-millennium" precipitation events now occurring with routine frequency, against a backdrop of federal disaster-response funding cuts.

### France's Constitutional Council strikes down under-15 social media ban

France's Conseil Constitutionnel on Friday struck down Article 1 of the law banning social media access for children under 15, ruling that it "constitutes an infringement that is neither appropriate, necessary, nor proportionate" to freedom of expression and communication. France had become the first EU member state to legislate a blanket ban only last month.

The council's central objection was that enforcement would require "every person, even an adult, to prove their age before accessing" social media, without adequate legal safeguards — a mass identification regime. President Macron has tasked Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu with redrafting the legislation.

### Simultaneous blackouts hit four Central Asian states

Power failed simultaneously across parts of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan on Friday afternoon after a "sudden change in the power flow" in the interconnected Central Asian grid, according to Kazakhstan's Energy Ministry. Almaty, Bishkek, Dushanbe and Khujand all lost power; Uzbekistan reported outages in southern regions near the Tajik border.

Alatau Zharyq Company said the cascade began at **2:38pm** when three 500 kV overhead transmission lines operated by national grid operator KEGOC shut down unexpectedly. Supply has since been fully restored. The incident is the second major failure of the shared Soviet-era grid in recent years, exposing decades of deferred infrastructure investment across four post-Soviet republics whose economies are dominated by extractive industries and remittance flows.

### Australian whistleblower David McBride released on parole after 27 months

### "The Brink of War": Reykjavik 1986 as historical costume drama

Among Friday's US theatrical releases is **The Brink of War**, a dramatization of the October 1986 Reykjavik summit between Ronald Reagan and Mikhail Gorbachev, at which discussions on human rights, the Strategic Defense Initiative and the elimination of nuclear arsenals collapsed over SDI. The subject matter is genuinely significant: Reykjavik was one of the closest approaches to nuclear disarmament in history, and its failure prefigured the Stalinist bureaucracy's capitulation and the dissolution of the USSR.

Early critical response has been unfavourable — one reviewer called it "a stage play adaptation gone horribly wrong" — and the film reportedly reduces the summit to a chamber drama of personal negotiation. The historical problem is that Gorbachev arrived at Reykjavik not as a peace envoy but as the representative of a privileged bureaucratic caste preparing the restoration of capitalism. A film that treats the summit as a contest of two statesmen's characters cannot address why the promise of disarmament evaporated — a question with obvious contemporary weight as the nuclear powers rearm and Washington wages open war on Iran.

### Anglicare report shows deepening rental crisis for Australian workers and welfare recipients — Jessicah Green

The annual Anglicare Rental Affordability Snapshot finds virtually no rental properties nationwide affordable for low-income workers and welfare recipients — a result driven, the article argues, by successive Labor and Coalition governments' protection of property investors through negative gearing and capital gains concessions, combined with the near-total abandonment of public housing construction. The article situates the housing crisis as a class question rather than a supply-and-demand problem, noting that the same Labor government presiding over this collapse is funding AUKUS submarines and expanding military spending. It draws the conclusion that housing is a social right that cannot be secured through appeals to the parliamentary parties, and links the crisis to the broader assault on working-class living standards being carried out under conditions of war preparation.

### Australia: Labor government tightens war-related powers over universities — Mike Head

Mike Head reports on new federal Labor measures expanding wartime-style powers over Australian universities, with punitive consequences for researchers and institutions found non-compliant. The measures extend the Defence Trade Controls regime and national security screening into academic research and international collaboration, particularly with China. Head situates this within the broader integration of Australian universities into US war planning through AUKUS, arguing that the "national security" framework functions to police academic staff and suppress political dissent on campus — the same institutions where opposition to the Gaza genocide and the Iran war has been strongest. The article connects the university measures to the McBride precedent: the criminalization of disclosure and the insulation of the military-intelligence apparatus from public scrutiny are two aspects of a single process. No comparable analysis exists in the Australian mainstream press, which has reported the changes narrowly as technical national-security compliance rules.

### Sri Lankan government rejects returning wartime military-occupied land — M. Thevarajah

More than fifteen years after the end of the civil war, the Sri Lankan government continues to refuse the return of thousands of acres of civilian land in the north and east still held under military occupation. Thevarajah documents the concrete consequences for displaced Tamil families barred from their homes, farmland and fishing grounds, and identifies the continuity of policy across every government — including the current National People's Power administration, which came to power promising reconciliation. The article ties the refusal to the permanent militarization of the Tamil areas and to the Sinhala-Buddhist chauvinism that remains the ideological foundation of the Sri Lankan state, arguing that the land question cannot be resolved within the framework of the capitalist nation-state established in 1948, and advancing the perspective of a united struggle of Sinhala, Tamil and Muslim workers for a Socialist Republic of Sri Lanka and Eelam as part of a Union of Socialist Republics of South Asia. This receives essentially no attention outside Tamil-focused and specialist human-rights media.

### Johannes Stern on RT: Bogdan Syrotiuk convicted "for socialist thoughts" — Andre Damon

Andre Damon reports on WSWS editor Johannes Stern's RT interview concerning **Bogdan Syrotiuk**, the 27-year-old Ukrainian Trotskyist and member of the Young Guard of Bolshevik-Leninists facing 15 years' imprisonment for antiwar writings published on the WSWS. Stern emphasized that Syrotiuk was prosecuted not for any act but for his political convictions — convicted, in effect, "for socialist thoughts." The article stresses the ICFI's opposition both to the Zelensky government's suppression of socialist and antiwar opposition and to the Putin regime's reactionary invasion, rejecting the framework in which defence of Syrotiuk implies support for Moscow. The case has received essentially no coverage in the Western press, which has been silent on the banning of parties, the jailing of oppositionists and the suppression of independent media in Ukraine while promoting the war as a defence of democracy.

### Australian and New Zealand students and youth demand freedom for Ukrainian socialist Bogdan Syrotiuk

The International Youth and Students for Social Equality in Australia and New Zealand issued statements demanding Syrotiuk's release, part of the ICFI's international defence campaign. The statements were accompanied by appeals to students and young workers to take up the case as a democratic-rights question inseparable from opposition to the war itself, and to draw the connection to the persecution of antiwar voices in their own countries — including the criminalization of whistleblowing in Australia and the surveillance of student organizations. The campaign has secured endorsements from academics, artists and trade unionists internationally.

### Workers Struggles: Asia and Australia
