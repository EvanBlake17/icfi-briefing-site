### 1. US weighs ground troops in Iran as war enters a sixth night

The US-Iran war entered its sixth consecutive night of strikes, with the White House now openly discussing a ground campaign. According to the *Wall Street Journal*, administration officials have met to weigh sending ground forces into Iran and seizing Iranian islands near the Strait of Hormuz. Overnight US strikes killed 8 people and wounded 20 in southern and western Iran, hitting six bridges in Hormozgan province, airport infrastructure, and Chabahar port.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards claimed they "destroyed several US refueling aircraft and fighter jets" in retaliatory strikes on US assets in Jordan and at Bahrain's Sakhir air base. Qatar intercepted a missile Friday morning — a child was injured by falling shrapnel — while Bahrain said it destroyed "hostile Iranian aerial attacks" it branded "cowardly attacks targeting civilians." Ceasefire talks have collapsed, and a regional expert warned the two sides risk a "forever war."

As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, the pivot from air strikes to a potential invasion reflects the failure of the bombing campaign, quoting Trump's own framing: "Sometimes you need a ground campaign, but we have other people that will do the ground campaign for us." A US official described the strikes as "shaping operations" to "set the stage, if needed."

The war has now killed and wounded civilians across multiple Gulf states, drawing Qatar, Bahrain and Jordan directly into the line of fire and raising the danger of a general Middle East conflagration. The collapse of ceasefire diplomacy — with neither Washington nor Tehran signaling any willingness to negotiate — has removed the last off-ramp. The ICFI has warned that the Iran war is not an isolated adventure but one front of an emerging Third World War that also encompasses Ukraine, the Asia-Pacific, and the drive against China.

Key facts:
- Only 3 commodity vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz yesterday, the lowest since May, after Iran vowed to halt oil and gas exports through the strait while attacks continue
- Washington revoked its sanctions waiver on Iranian oil effective 12:01 a.m. EDT July 17
- US strikes have killed at least 35 people this month; Iran's Foreign Ministry called a hospital strike "a cowardly war crime against the most innocent"
- Current US regional forces number roughly 50,000, though few are ground troops

### 2. More than 500 Rohingya feared dead off Myanmar's coast

More than 500 Rohingya refugees are feared dead after two boats capsized off Myanmar's coast, in what would be one of the deadliest single episodes on record along one of the world's most lethal maritime routes. Two boats carrying roughly 250 and 280 people departed Rakhine State in late June; the second is believed to have sunk off the Ayeyarwady coast on July 8.

UNHCR and IOM said they are "gravely concerned by the potentially devastating loss of life" and called for "stronger regional and international efforts... to prevent further loss of life." The catastrophe follows the deadliest year on record in 2025, when over 6,500 Rohingya fled by sea and nearly 900 were reported dead or missing.

Key facts:
- Roughly 1.2 million stateless Rohingya remain in overcrowded Bangladesh refugee camps, where aid cuts have forced reduced rations
- The disaster reflects the persecution of the Rohingya by Myanmar's military junta combined with the collapse of international refugee funding
- Departures spike during monsoon season despite the extreme danger, as camp conditions deteriorate
- The route across the Andaman Sea and Bay of Bengal has become, in the UN's words, "one of the world's deadliest," a direct product of the closure of legal avenues of escape

### 4. Catastrophic flash flooding returns to Texas Hill Country

Catastrophic flash flooding struck Texas Hill Country again, killing at least two people and forcing more than 200 rescues almost exactly one year after the July 2025 floods that killed more than 130, including dozens of girls and counselors at Camp Mystic. Parts of the region received a year's worth of rain in three days.

Governor Greg Abbott confirmed two deaths: a man near Comfort and a woman near Uvalde. One victim, John Mark Steward, 65, was swept from his mobile home near Kerrville after calling a neighbor at 3:06 a.m. to report the rising water. Rescuers used boats and helicopters to save stranded drivers and residents trapped in their homes.

Key facts:
- The disaster arrives amid ongoing scrutiny of warning-system and infrastructure failures exposed by the 2025 catastrophe
- The recurrence in the same region within a year underscores the intersection of climate-driven extreme rainfall and inadequate emergency preparedness
- Federal cuts to weather and emergency-response agencies loom over the response

### 6. Hong Kong raids two bookstores in a third crackdown this year

Hong Kong police raided two independent bookstores — "Have A Nice Stay," founded by former journalists, and the longstanding Greenfield Book Store — arresting five people on July 15. Authorities allege the material "stirred up hatred" against the Hong Kong government, judiciary, and law enforcement. Customs officials referred the case after finding allegedly seditious books in an overseas shipment.

It is the third round of arrests targeting independent bookstores in four months. In March, police arrested staff of the Book Punch store over the sale of a biography of jailed media proprietor Jimmy Lai. The pattern signals an intensifying campaign against independent media and publishing under Hong Kong's national security law.

Key facts:
- The specific book titles deemed "seditious" have not been publicly disclosed
- The targets — a former-journalists' shop and a dissident biography — point to a broad assault on independent publishing infrastructure

### Wildfire smoke makes Detroit, Chicago and Minneapolis the world's most polluted cities

Thick smoke from wildfires in Canada and northeastern Minnesota swept across the Great Lakes and Northeast, and by late Thursday morning Detroit, Minneapolis, and Chicago were the three most polluted major cities in the world, according to IQAir. All three registered "code purple" — "very unhealthy" — air on the EPA index, with all of Michigan and much of Minnesota under hazardous air-quality alerts. Officials urged residents to remain indoors.

The fine particulate matter in wildfire smoke lodges deep in the lungs and enters the bloodstream, raising the risk of heart attacks, strokes, and chronic respiratory disease. The International Workers Alliance of Rank-and-File Committees issued a call to "shut down plants and workplaces amidst spreading toxic air," noting that workers — especially those in outdoor and industrial jobs — are compelled to keep working through hazardous conditions while corporate operations continue uninterrupted.

### US measles nears a 34-year record as outbreaks spread

The US measles outbreak added 61 new cases, bringing 2026 to 2,231 confirmed cases — just 58 short of the entire 2025 total, itself a 34-year high. The relentless spread reflects collapsing vaccination coverage amid the anti-science campaign driven by the Trump administration's public-health apparatus. Public-health experts warn that once the 2025 total is surpassed — likely within days — the United States will record its worst measles year since elimination was declared in 2000, a reversal of a signal achievement of twentieth-century public health.

The WSWS reported on two simultaneous disease outbreaks in New York City — Legionnaires' disease and cyclosporiasis — which Philip Guelpa analyzed as products of the systematic prioritization of profit over public health, from deferred maintenance of cooling systems to inadequate food-safety inspection.

Roundup of other developments:
- **H5N1 bird flu:** The USDA confirmed another infected dairy herd in Utah — the state's 23rd — bringing the national total to 1,161 herds across 19 states. Internationally, 12 human H5N1 infections were reported between August 2025 and June 2026 (Bangladesh, Cambodia, India), three of them fatal
- **Long COVID:** A clinical trial found that two commonly prescribed drugs may offer short-term improvements in long-COVID fatigue, potentially addressing a care gap for the 1–5% of adults worldwide with the condition
- **Chronic kidney disease:** An international study reported that finerenone slows kidney-function decline and reduces serious kidney and cardiovascular complications in people with CKD who do not have diabetes — a potentially significant expansion of the drug's indicated population
- **Climate science:** Researchers identified a hidden geochemical feedback loop that may explain how Earth has regulated its climate over tens of millions of years
- **Materials science:** A study of an ancient sea worm found jaws combining proteins and metal ions in a way that yields metal-like strength, pointing to a new category of bio-inspired materials

## Arts and culture

The most significant cultural news of the period remains the loss of two towering figures of postwar visual art in recent weeks — British painter **David Hockney**, who died June 11 at 88, and American photographer **Duane Michals**, who died June 9 at 94. Hockney, one of the most popular artists of his generation, moved fluidly between painting, photography, stage design, and iPad drawing; his California pool paintings and Yorkshire landscapes united technical brilliance with an unfashionable insistence on observation and joy in the visible world. Michals broke with the documentary tradition of American photography by staging sequenced, text-annotated images that probed mortality, memory, and desire — an intensely literary body of work that expanded the expressive range of the medium. Their deaths, alongside those of photographers William Coupon and Hiromitsu Morimoto and performance artist Charles Dennis, mark the passing of a generation shaped by the social and artistic ferment of the mid-twentieth century.

Roundup:
- **Exhibitions:** The Metropolitan Museum of Art opened "Raphael: Sublime Poetry," a major survey of one of the central figures of the Italian High Renaissance. Such retrospectives, drawing large audiences, testify to an enduring popular hunger for serious art against the commodified churn of the culture industry.
- **Film and television:** Netflix's adaptation of Laura Ingalls Wilder's *Little House* books revisits the mythology of American frontier settlement — material that demands a critical reckoning with the westward expansion, dispossession, and class struggle it romanticizes, rather than nostalgic reproduction.
- **Technology and childhood:** A wave of AI-driven toys, dolls, and robots "built to befriend children" is drawing warnings from child-development experts that such products risk crowding out genuine human relationships — a commercial encroachment of surveillance-capitalist technology into the most intimate spheres of childhood.
- **Music and popular culture:** Jacobin's essay on 1970s British punk (see pseudo-left review) is a reminder of how a genuine cultural revolt against the "no future" of postwar decay was expressed through DIY practice — and later commodified.

These developments unfold against a backdrop of war, censorship (see the Hong Kong bookstore raids above), and austerity pressures on public arts funding, which continue to narrow the space for serious artistic work independent of the market and the state.

### Jacobin / DSA

- **"Don't Just Nationalize AI. Democratize It."** (Michael A. McCarthy) argues for "democratic control" of AI by "working-class institutions." The formulation evades the question of state power, presenting a reorganization of ownership as achievable through pressure within the existing capitalist framework rather than the conquest of political power by the working class.
- **"Richard Wolff on the Left's New Openings in 2026"** frames American hegemonic decline and regional wars as opening opportunities for "socialist alternatives" — but Wolff's perennial conclusion points toward cooperatives and progressive electoral pressure, not the revolutionary overthrow of the capitalist state.
- **"These Veteran Candidates Are Attacking Vets' Health Care"** critiques veteran politicians who back VA privatization — a reformist framing that measures candidates against a better administration of the imperialist military rather than opposing militarism as such.

The WSWS directly answered Jacobin this period. In "After NYC elections, Jacobin offers services of DSA to Democratic establishment, union bureaucracy," Erik Schreiber and Barry Grey document Jacobin explicitly urging Democratic leaders to co-opt the DSA to lend the party left credibility — an open avowal of the DSA's function as a prop for the Democratic Party and a mechanism for containing the class struggle. Overall orientation: the frank integration of the pseudo-left into bourgeois politics.

### Left Voice

- **"Build a Mass International Movement Against the Imperialist War in Iran! For the Defeat of the United States and Israel!"** offers a militant-sounding defeatist slogan on Iran. The rhetoric is correct in form, but Left Voice's practical orientation — as the WSWS documented in "Left Voice's 'united front' aims to prop up the union bureaucracy and Democratic Party" (June 2) — subordinates such calls to alliances with the very apparatuses that police the working class.
- Ongoing theoretical pieces on "New and Old Debates on Anti-imperialism and Socialism" restage debates with figures like Vivek Chibber while obscuring the ICFI's analysis of the world war and the necessity of an independent revolutionary party.

Overall orientation: revolutionary phraseology combined with a united-front practice that ties workers to the labor bureaucracy and, indirectly, the Democrats.

### PSL / Liberation News

- **"PSL Statement: Stop Trump's War on Iran!"** calls for street mobilizations against the war — a positive impulse, but the PSL's framing routinely tails bourgeois-nationalist and "anti-imperialist" bourgeois regimes rather than advancing the independent mobilization of the international working class against both imperialism and the national bourgeoisies.
- The PSL's broader Palestine and anti-war agitation continues to channel opposition into single-issue protest and pressure politics rather than a struggle for workers' power.

Overall orientation: genuine anti-war activism harnessed to a Stalinist-derived program that substitutes campism for revolutionary internationalism.

### Socialist Worker (SWP UK)

- **"Anti-racist majority fights far right"** celebrates counter-demonstrations that outnumbered far-right gatherings across UK cities — a mobilization the SWP folds into cross-class "unity" that ultimately looks to Labour's left and the TUC rather than an independent working-class movement.
- **"Oppose Labour's plans to turn army barracks into camps for refugees"** rightly attacks the Starmer government's refugee policy but frames the solution in terms of pressuring Labour rather than breaking with it.

Overall orientation: activist anti-racism that leaves the political subordination of workers to the Labour Party untouched.

### Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

- **"The many faces of Andy Burnham – how long until the mask slips?"** correctly warns that a Burnham-led government would be "a crisis government from day one," but the IMT's abstract "communist" branding coexists with an orientation toward left-Labour figures and left populism.
- **"Farage's sham resignation: bin all the liars, crooks, and racists!"** adopts a tone of blanket denunciation of Westminster while offering no concrete revolutionary program to connect popular disillusionment to the building of a party.

Overall orientation: maximalist rhetoric married to a propagandistic passivity that substitutes recruitment slogans for the fight to build a section of a genuine world party.

The WSWS also published Tony Robson's "Pseudo-left ditch Gaza and anti-war posturing to back Sharon Graham as Unite General Secretary," documenting how British pseudo-left groups abandoned Palestine solidarity to endorse an establishment union candidate — a concrete illustration of the tendencies reviewed above.

Across all six tendencies, the common thread this period is the subordination of the working class to one or another wing of the bourgeois political establishment — the Democratic Party in the United States, the Labour Party in Britain, or bourgeois-nationalist regimes internationally. None advances the central lesson of the war crisis: that the fight against imperialist war requires a break with the pseudo-left's national-reformist framework and the construction of a unified international movement of the working class on the program of the Fourth International.
