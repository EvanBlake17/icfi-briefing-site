### 3. Ukraine launches mass drone strikes deep into Russia

Overnight June 6, Ukraine launched drone strikes reaching up to 1,000 km into Russia, targeting Russian naval arsenals at the historic port of Kronstadt near St. Petersburg and a major oil transshipment terminal. Key facts:

### 5. US Senate approves $70 billion immigration enforcement bill

### 6. North Korea unveils new uranium plant as Xi prepares first Pyongyang visit in seven years

### 7. Iranian drones strike Kuwait airport, killing Indian migrant worker

Kuwait's defence ministry detected 30 ballistic missiles and drones launched by Iran on June 3, severely damaging the passenger terminal of Kuwait International Airport. One person was killed — an Indian national — and at least 63 were injured, including airport workers and passengers. Kuwait's foreign ministry said the attack also damaged unnamed diplomatic missions.

Iran's Revolutionary Guard denied firing on the airport, claiming via state broadcaster IRIB that the damage came from a failed US Patriot interceptor. US Central Command called the claim "false" and described the attack as "deliberate, calculated and unjustified." Kuwait Airways resumed flights after a brief suspension.

The single fatality — almost certainly a low-wage migrant worker among the estimated 3.5 million Indian nationals employed in Kuwait under kafala-style arrangements — was treated by the global news cycle as a data point, with no reporting on who he was.

### Germany fails to win UN Security Council seat

Germany received only 104 votes in the UN General Assembly's election for a non-permanent Security Council seat, far short of the 127 needed. Portugal (134) and Austria (131) won the two available European seats — the first time Germany has failed to win such a seat since reunification in 1990, despite Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul's intensive worldwide lobbying.

On the WSWS, Peter Schwarz argued the defeat reflects global mistrust of Germany's aggressive militarism — its backing of Israeli operations in Gaza framed as a "national interest," support for Trump's Iran conflict, co-leadership of Ukraine war logistics, and development of long-range weapons with Kyiv. Notably, the Greens, Left Party and AfD criticized the government for damaging Germany's standing rather than questioning the militarization itself.

### Bolivian general strike enters sixth week

Workers maintain a single demand — Paz's resignation — and rejected negotiations; ten peasants who occupied an oil field were sentenced to three years imprisonment.

### May jobs report masks low-wage growth and falling real wages

Beyond its role in triggering the market sell-off, the May jobs report offers a window into the actual condition of US workers. The 172,000 gain was concentrated in leisure and hospitality (+70,000, of which food services accounted for +48,000), local government and healthcare — among the lowest-paid sectors. ## Arts and culture

The most significant cultural event marked in the past day is the death of tenor saxophonist **Sonny Rollins**, one of the last and greatest figures of the bebop generation, reviewed at length on the WSWS by Jesse Thomas and John Andrews. Born September 7, 1930 in Harlem and dead at 95 on May 25, 2026 in Woodstock, New York from progressive pulmonary fibrosis, Rollins possessed what the authors describe as "an earthy, broad sound" that synthesized Coleman Hawkins, Lester Young and Charlie Parker into something wholly his own.

His landmark recordings — *Saxophone Colossus* (1956), *Tenor Madness* (1956) and *Freedom Suite* (1958) — and his improvisations stretching past ten minutes helped define post-war jazz. The WSWS obituary is distinguished from most by its attention to Rollins's social formation. As a child he attended Camp Unity, described as "an interracial, antichauvinist, anticapitalist summer camp" run by Communist Party branches, and his aunt Mirium was a left-wing activist. *Freedom Suite*, recorded against the backdrop of the civil-rights struggle, stands as one of the era's most explicit fusions of musical innovation and social protest.

Rollins's life also illustrates the toll of capitalist society on artists: he overcame heroin addiction in the early 1950s and took two extended sabbaticals (1959–1962 and 1968–1971) to study yoga and meditation, famously practicing alone on the Williamsburg Bridge. In a final, bitter irony, the September 11, 2001 attacks near his Manhattan apartment caused the pulmonary damage that ended his performing career; his last public performance came in 2012. His death severs another link to a generation of Black American musicians who forged a major art form under conditions of segregation and exploitation.

Rollins's passing invites reflection on the present cultural moment, in which militarism and nationalism increasingly saturate official culture, and arts funding remains under sustained political pressure. Against that backdrop, the artistic seriousness and internationalist sensibility of figures like Rollins — formed in the milieu of the 1930s–40s left — stand as a reproach to the commodified, identity-segmented cultural product of today.

Cultural roundup:
- **Jazz legacy:** Tributes from across the music world underscore Rollins's influence on generations of saxophonists; his catalogue on Prestige, Blue Note and Riverside remains foundational to the post-war canon.
- **Historical memory:** The WSWS framing — situating his art in the Communist-influenced cultural institutions of mid-century America — recovers a history of working-class and anti-racist cultural organizing that bourgeois obituaries routinely omit.
