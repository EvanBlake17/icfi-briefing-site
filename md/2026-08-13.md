### Colombia earthquake death toll passes 265 as rescue window closes

The 7.4-magnitude earthquake that struck western Colombia — the strongest to hit the country in 47 years — has killed at least 265 people, with rescue crews entering what officials describe as the final hours in which survivors can realistically be pulled from collapsed buildings. More than 3,700 people have been wounded and over 25,800 families displaced or otherwise affected across Cali, Pereira, Manizales and Quibdó.

The gap between official and civilian casualty accounting is itself a political fact. The government lists nearly 500 people as missing; crowd-sourced databases maintained by families and volunteers put the number above 4,100. President Abelardo de la Espriella, who took office pledging law-and-order austerity, has moved to declare an economic emergency, a mechanism that will govern how reconstruction is financed and who bears the cost.

Key facts:

- Death toll at least 265, with more than 3,700 wounded and 25,800+ families affected.
- Official missing count near 500; civilian-run databases list more than 4,100.
- De la Espriella: "Our efforts are focused on the disappeared."
- Cali's mayor: "The next 20 hours are the most critical to find and rescue people alive. Every minute is vital."
- Foreign aid: US pledged over $15 million plus disaster teams; Mexico sent 19.5 tons of supplies and 255 specialists; El Salvador dispatched two planes with 100 tons of aid.

The scale of building collapse in dense urban working-class districts, and the reliance on volunteer digging in the critical first 72 hours, points to the familiar pattern in which seismic risk is socially distributed — concentrated in substandard, unregulated housing.

### US measles cases hit a 35-year high

As of August 6, the CDC has confirmed 2,465 measles cases across 47 jurisdictions in 2026 — surpassing the full-year 2025 total of 2,289 and marking the highest annual count since 1991. Seven states account for over 84 percent of cases, led by South Carolina with 670 and Utah with 522. Ninety-three percent of those infected were unvaccinated or of unknown vaccination status.

The United States now stands to lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000, which requires that no chain of continuous transmission exceed twelve months. The proximate cause is not scientific uncertainty but the deliberate degradation of public vaccination programs and the promotion of anti-vaccine positions from within the federal health apparatus itself.

### Lancet trial finds statin discontinuation at 75 non-inferior

A French randomized trial by the SAGA/SITE Study Group, published August 11 in *The Lancet Healthy Longevity*, found that in adults aged 75 and over taking statins for primary prevention — with no prior cardiovascular disease — discontinuation was non-inferior to continuation for all-cause mortality over three years. The finding supports individualized deprescribing and reduction of pill burden in older patients, though it will predictably be seized on by insurers and health systems as a cost-cutting rationale rather than a clinical one.

It is the largest AI-infrastructure supply-chain breach identified so far this year, and a demonstration that the entire commercial AI buildout rests on volunteer-maintained open-source components with no security funding.

### Kennedy Center ordered to pay musician who refused to perform under Trump's name

A District of Columbia Superior Court has ordered the Kennedy Center to pay $252,479.70 in attorney's fees to jazz musician Chuck Redd, who cancelled his annual Christmas Eve performance after the institution's board voted to add Trump's name to the building. The court dismissed the Center's suit against Redd in June, finding it had failed to prove he had signed any performance contract. The decision is significant beyond its dollar amount: a federally chartered cultural institution attempted to use civil litigation to punish an individual artist for a political refusal, and lost.

Redd's cancellation was one of the earlier acts in an exodus that has now hollowed out the institution. The Washington National Opera — resident at the Kennedy Center since 1971 — announced in January that it would move its performances elsewhere, a departure Kennedy Center president Richard Grenell reframed as a strategic decision by "the Trump Kennedy Center" to end an exclusive partnership. Composer Philip Glass withdrew the world premiere of his Symphony No. 15, "Lincoln," stating that the leadership's values were incompatible with the work's message. House Republicans have proposed naming the Opera House after Melania Trump.

What is being enacted is not simply vandalism of a building's signage but the conversion of a national cultural institution into an instrument of personal cult and state ideology. The response — individual withdrawal by artists — has been principled but atomized, and has left the institution's programming to be filled by those willing to accept the terms.

### Australian union calls off strike by Victorian doctors — Martin Scott, Eric Ludlow

The Australian Salaried Medical Officers' Federation cancelled a strike by Victorian public hospital doctors scheduled for August 14 — the first such stoppage in twenty years, backed by a 97 percent strike vote — after the state Labor government challenged the technical validity of the strike notice. The article's significance is in the mechanism: Labor did not have to defeat the strike politically or industrially. Scott and Ludlow argue that Victorian doctors, whose grievances concern unsafe rostering and understaffing that directly threaten patients, cannot advance through a union structure that accepts the legal framework as legitimate, and call for rank-and-file committees able to coordinate action independently of ASMOF and across the health workforce. The conclusion is programmatic: the defense of public healthcare requires the socialist reorganization of society, not better-drafted strike notices.

### Jacobin and DSA

Jacobin devoted two of its three notable pieces to the Wisconsin Democratic primary defeat of Francesca Hong. **"The Socialist Electoral Project Still Hasn't Hit Its Ceiling"** presents the loss as evidence of expanding left strength, measured by vote share within a Democratic primary. **"Five Takeaways From Francesca Hong's Near-Win in Wisconsin"** extracts tactical lessons on the same terrain — canvassing operations, endorsement dynamics, the role of the state party.

The unexamined premise of both is that the vehicle for socialist advance is the Democratic ballot line, and the measure of progress is how close a DSA-aligned candidate came to defeating the machine. Neither article poses the question of what a Hong victory would have obligated her to — the Wisconsin Democratic caucus, the party's national fundraising apparatus, its unbroken support for the Gaza and Iran wars. The "ceiling" metaphor is itself revealing: it treats the Democratic Party as a container that can be filled from below rather than a state-integrated institution designed to absorb and dissipate exactly this energy.

**"John Deere Is Testing Its Workers' Resolve"** is stronger, covering the UAW contract fight with genuine sympathy for the shop floor. But it treats the UAW apparatus as a neutral or merely inadequate instrument rather than as management's partner, and offers no perspective for workers beyond hoping the union negotiates harder.

**Assessment:** A day's output almost entirely absorbed in the internal metrics of Democratic Party primaries — the clearest possible expression of the DSA's function as a left flank of the party it claims to be transforming.

### Left Voice

**"Sanders-Backed Flanagan Wins Minnesota Senate Primary, Continuing Progressive Sweep"** reports a Sanders-aligned candidate's victory as part of a broader wave. The framing — a "sweep" of progressive candidates within Democratic primaries — sits uneasily with Left Voice's own stated Trotskyist opposition to class collaboration. The article does not draw the conclusion its politics would require: that a Sanders endorsement is precisely the mechanism by which anti-establishment sentiment is recaptured for the Democratic Party.

**"How Flock Is Building America's Private Surveillance Infrastructure"** is the more valuable piece — a documented account of how the automated license-plate-reader company has built a national surveillance network sold to municipalities, arguing correctly that privatization is not the problem and municipal ownership is not the solution, since the class function of the apparatus is unchanged.

**Assessment:** Capable investigative work coexisting with an electoral commentary that reports Democratic primary results in the vocabulary of the milieu it formally criticizes.

### Liberation News (PSL)

**"Desperate Democratic Establishment Narrowly Defeats Hong in Wisconsin"** is the most class-antagonistic treatment of the primary, naming Governor Evers and the "big-business" Democrats as the opponent and advancing concrete demands — minimum wage increases, healthcare, repeal of the anti-union Act 10. **"Flanagan Wins Big in Primary as Minnesotans Reject AIPAC and ICE"** reads a Democratic primary result as a popular referendum against AIPAC and immigration raids.

The contradiction is structural: the PSL denounces the Democratic Party as an instrument of capital, then reports its primaries as the arena where mass consciousness registers itself. Treating a Sanders- and Warren-backed candidate's win as a mass anti-Zionist verdict inflates an electoral datum into a political mandate and obscures that the winner will caucus with the party funding the war.

**Assessment:** Militant tone, national-reformist content — a program of demands to be won from the existing state, with no perspective of independent working-class power.

### Socialist Alternative

With no output in the immediate window, the most recent significant pieces are **"Support Left Independent Candidates—Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties,"** which rejects both parties by name and cites ICE killings and US backing for the Gaza and Iran wars, and **"South Africa: Xenophobia & The Street War Against Black Immigrants,"** which traces anti-immigrant violence to mass unemployment and indicts the ANC's post-1994 neoliberal turn.

The South Africa piece is a serious class analysis of a national liberation party's integration into capitalist rule. The electoral piece, however, substitutes "left independent" campaigns — the Sawant model — for the construction of a revolutionary party, offering municipal-reform electoralism outside the Democratic Party rather than a break with electoralism as strategy.

**Assessment:** Correct on the party question, reformist on the programmatic one.

### Socialist Worker (SWP UK)

**"Revolt Spreads in India and Pakistan"** celebrates spontaneous mass uprisings across South Asia, paired with anti-monarchist commentary on the Mountbatten-Windsor funeral. **"Sharon Graham Wins After Poor Choice in Unite Union Leadership Race"** applies the classic SWP rank-and-file line — skepticism toward Graham and the Unite machine, a call for shop-floor organization independent of officials.

The limitation is the same one the SWP has carried for six decades: mass struggle is celebrated as self-sufficient, with the party reduced to an auxiliary that encourages spontaneity rather than fighting for a program. "Rank-and-file organization" without a struggle for political leadership leaves the bureaucracy in place.

**Assessment:** Genuine internationalist instinct, no theory of the party.

### Socialist Appeal and the RCP (IMT)

**"Burnham and British Capitalism – Bouncing Into an Abyss"** argues Labour's post-Burnham polling recovery is illusory given the underlying crisis of British capitalism. **"UK Foreign Office Decimated: Britain's 'Soft' Power Gets Even Softer"** reads Foreign Office austerity as symptomatic of British imperialism's terminal decline rather than as underfunded diplomacy.

Both avoid the reformist illusions in Labour that characterized this tendency's earlier entrism, and the imperialist-decline framing is analytically closer to Marxism than most of what appears elsewhere in this survey. What is absent is any programmatic conclusion — the pieces describe capitalist crisis as spectacle, with the revolutionary party appearing only as an assertion at the end.

**Assessment:** Sound diagnosis, propagandistic prescription.
