### 1. Democratic Senate majority climbs toward 56 as Trump says he will "veto" the new Congress

The scale of Tuesday's Republican defeat grew as counting continued. NBC News projects Democrats at about 255 House seats; the WSWS, using AP calls, puts them at 259 to 176 with several dozen races still open. That would be the largest Democratic majority since 1990. NBC also projects a Democratic Senate. Patrick Martin reports a 54–44 Democratic majority with two Republican-held seats still undecided, and Democrats lead narrowly in both:
- **Mississippi:** Scott Colom's lead over Cindy Hyde-Smith has grown from 65 votes to more than 1,000, with thousands of provisional ballots still to count.
- **Alaska:** Mary Peltola leads Dan Sullivan by about 600 votes in a four-way race. Ranked-choice tabulation will not start until the last mail ballots are in, probably late this month.

A 56-seat majority would be the biggest either party has held since 2010. The new Congress convenes January 3.

Trump told Fox News he was "a little bit surprised" by the result. He told the *New York Times* he was "absolutely willing to accept those results." He said he would work with Democrats but would veto what they pass. He blamed weak Republican candidates and high gas prices, which come from his own war on Iran, and insisted he was not on the ballot, even though he had told rallies in 20 states to "pretend I'm on the ballot." In Moscow, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov called the shift "an internal matter for America" and said: "We sincerely hope that he will also continue his peacemaking efforts." In today's WSWS Perspective, Joseph Kishore and David North call Trump "a symptom, not the cause," and reject the *New York Times* editorial board's verdict that "the system has held."

Key facts:
- **Popular vote:** Democrats won the national House vote by about 13 points, the widest margin for either party in half a century (WSWS). Pre-election polls had put the generic-ballot lead at nine points.
- **Exit polls, reported by the WSWS:**
  - About 60 percent said attacking Iran was the wrong decision, and 74 percent called the war a crucial or major factor in their vote.
  - Between 80 and 90 percent rated the economy poor, and two-thirds blamed Trump's policies.
  - Trump's approval stood at 38 percent. Of voters, 43 percent said they voted to oppose him and 16 percent to support him.
- **Notable House flips (NBC):**
  - Pennsylvania: Janelle Stelson ousted Scott Perry (PA-10), Paige Cognetti beat Rob Bresnahan (PA-8), Bob Brooks beat Ryan Mackenzie (PA-7), and Bob Harvie beat Brian Fitzpatrick.
  - North Carolina: Richard Ojeda II upset Richard Hudson, who chairs the House GOP's campaign arm.
  - Virginia: Elaine Luria retook her old seat from Jen Kiggans.
- **Where the swing came from:** Small cities and rural areas swung more than 15 points against Trump. Some Rio Grande Valley counties swung more than 50 points among Hispanic voters. Trump lost every age group (WSWS).
- **DeSantis:** He called the result "close to an extinction-level event" for Republicans in many areas.

### 5. France stages national strike under a 70,000-strong police deployment

France's inter-union coalition led today's national strike and demonstrations over living costs, public services and education. The CGT called the action, student groups joined, and public-sector workers were called out. The unions expected 500,000 to 700,000 people outside Paris and 40,000 to 80,000 in the capital. Domestic intelligence gave the same figure for the provinces. About 70,000 police were deployed nationwide. A Paris court late Wednesday approved the police-proposed route from Place de la République to Place de la Nation, over the organisers' objections. Macron convened a defence and security council on Wednesday. Government spokeswoman Maud Bregeon said: "We are extremely vigilant because it is out of the question to relive October 17."

The strike comes as the state hardens its legal arsenal against protest. A bill already passed by the Senate would make anyone who "knowingly" joined a group that committed damage, or who stayed after two police orders to disperse, liable for up to €10,000 in damages, whether or not they personally caused any. It would allow the state to recover the money by seizing social benefits, including the minimum income (RSA), housing aid and low-income household assistance. Communist senator Fabien Gay calculated that a €57 monthly deduction would take 14 years to reach €10,000. Amnesty International called the bill a "deeply regressive step." The Magistrates' Union, the lawyers' union SAF and the Human Rights League have taken a complaint to France's highest court over an "authoritarian shift." The bill now goes to the National Assembly.

Key facts:
- **October 17 riots:** The Chamber of Commerce counts nearly 2,500 businesses damaged and more than 1,000 looted or burned. The free-market IFRAP foundation puts the cost near €1 billion.
- **CGT line:** CGT leader Sophie Binet called on workers to "generalize" the strike but not to call a general strike (WSWS).
- **Coming dates:** One-day national strikes are scheduled in Spain (November 11), Italy (November 20), Belgium (November 23) and Greece (November 25) (WSWS).
- **Media:** A petition to ban a French right-wing television channel after a racist killing has passed 600,000 signatures (France 24).

### 6. Ebola deaths pass 5,300 as US bars entry to anyone, citizens included, recently in Congo

The Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak that began in the Democratic Republic of Congo in May has killed more than 5,300 people. The Africa CDC has confirmed 10,444 cases, which makes it the largest and fastest-growing outbreak in the country's history. The Bundibugyo strain kills about half of those it infects, and there is no licensed vaccine against it. WHO officials report 50 to 80 new cases a day. About 80 percent of new cases cannot be linked to a known chain of transmission, a sign that contact tracing has broken down across eight provinces. Kenya has confirmed 9 cases and 2 deaths, and Nigeria has one confirmed case. Sequencing shows both countries' index patients passed through Uganda's Entebbe airport on October 3.

WHO expert groups endorsed two new vaccine trials on Monday, led by Congo's National Institute for Biomedical Research. One candidate was developed by Oxford University, the Serum Institute of India and Moderna. Results are not expected before early 2027. A trial that began last month uses Ervebo, which is licensed only against the Zaire strain. Since November 3 the CDC has required every passenger boarding a US-bound international flight to attest that they have not been in the DRC in the previous 21 days. The rule bars US citizens and nationals as well and runs to December 18.

Key facts:
- **Tedros:** He said travel bans "punish countries for doing the right thing" and warned that vaccines "are not substitutes for the proven measures that can save lives now."
- **Africa CDC:** Director-General Jean Kaseya warned the virus could reach the UK and Europe. "It is the first time three African regions have been affected," he said.
- **UK:** An infected British health worker is being evacuated from the DRC and is due to arrive today.
- **CDC:** The agency rates the risk to Americans as low and has about 500 staff on the response, more than 120 of them deployed abroad.

### Ontario government's slashing of student assistance fuels mounting opposition across university and college campuses — Dylan Lubao

The Ford government has cut the grant share of the Ontario Student Assistance Program, used by about 450,000 students, from 85 percent to 25 percent, leaving working-class students tens of thousands of dollars in debt. It has also suspended all OSAP payments to students at private career colleges and ended the seven-year tuition freeze. Ontario already spends the least per university student of any province, about $10,500 in 2023–24, roughly $7,000 below the national average. The CFS-led "Hands Off Our Education" coalition, backed by ETFO and CUPE Ontario, called a province-wide student strike for November 5.

Lubao describes the coalition as provincialist, oriented to lobbying Ford and to electing the NDP in 2029. He contrasts it with the 2012 Quebec student strike, which the union leaders steered into electing a Parti Québécois government that went on to impose its own austerity. He connects the cuts to Carney's pledge to raise military spending to 5 percent of GDP, more than $150 billion a year, and to Ontario's plan to triple its military-industrial workforce. He calls on students to link up with the 1,200 strikers at National Steel Car, the 1,300 at Teck's Highland Valley Copper mine, and the 9,000 Stellantis workers who voted 97 percent to strike.

### NALC election installs new "reform" officials to oversee USPS contract arbitration and historic cuts — Tom Hall

James Henry of the Concerned Letter Carriers slate won the National Association of Letter Carriers presidency with 23,077 votes. Turnout was 21.4 percent, 61,120 of 285,881 members, so Henry won the backing of about 8 percent of the membership. His salary as president will be about $350,000. Corey Walton, a podcaster who made his name criticising contract violations, becomes executive vice president at about $280,000. Carriers rejected a tentative agreement in 2025 by 63,680 to 26,304, after which arbitrator Dennis Nolan imposed nearly identical terms, including a two-tier wage structure. The new leadership is expected to send the contract that expired in May to binding arbitration again, with no membership vote.

At the same time, the USPS Inspector General has set out options including a 10 percent cut in the workforce, worth up to $5.6 billion a year, and moving door-to-door delivery to curbside and cluster boxes. Postmaster General David Steiner has threatened to review the closure of thousands of post offices. Convention delegates voted 2,583 to 694 against a campaign to restore postal workers' right to strike. Hall criticises the Build a Fighting NALC caucus for promoting the slate and points to rank-and-file committees already formed in southern Pennsylvania, St. Paul and Springfield, Massachusetts.

### "There was no lockout/tagout system": Worker from the floor details conditions behind Bradley Clouse's death — Milo Stevens

Bradley Clouse, a 33-year-old maintenance worker, was crushed on September 2 inside a pneumatic varnishing machine at the non-union Astemo plant in Berea, Kentucky. The plant is jointly owned by Honda, Hitachi and JIC Capital, and Governor Beshear had celebrated a $112 million expansion there the previous day. A worker told the WSWS:
- The plant had no lockout/tagout system at all, and safety training lasted "at most 20 minutes."
- The machine broke down about once a week and had malfunctioned several times in the days before the death.
- The first response he heard was "call the safety manager," not "call 911."
- The same machine is still running. Workers were given a Chick-fil-A gift card.

OSHA cited the plant for machine-guarding violations in 2016 and again in 2020. The company contested the 2020 citation, and the case closed in November 2025. Astemo made nearly $400 million in profit last fiscal year. About 80 percent of the 600,000 US auto parts workers have no union. The article lists other recent deaths at Ford Sharonville, Applied Materials in Austin and McWane in New Jersey.

### Mexico's President Sheinbaum throws governor a lifeline over Guadalajara water protests — Andrea Lobo

The water utility SIAPA has 627 Guadalajara neighbourhoods on rotating cuts. Some residents blocked a Day of the Dead parade route on November 1 after going 32 days without water. On October 11 police used tear gas and water cannon on about 10,000 marchers, made 39 arrests, and face accusations of torture. Sheinbaum and Morena first called for Movimiento Ciudadano Governor Pablo Lemus to resign. On October 28 they stood beside him to announce a 25-billion-peso aqueduct plan whose main works run to late 2028.

Lobo documents the breakdown of the system. Between 40 and 50 percent of the water in the 8,000-kilometre network is lost to leaks. Of 1,477 neighbourhood reports, 84 percent described turbid or foul-smelling water, and of 784 formal complaints SIAPA resolved six. One family spent about 40 percent of a minimum wage over six weeks on bottled and trucked water. She traces the problem to the 1992 National Water Law, which turned concessions into corporate assets. She argues that Morena and MC closed ranks to keep the protests from linking up with strikes elsewhere in Mexico, and calls for the expropriation of the bottlers, breweries and developers that hoard water.

## Pseudo-left press review

- [Jacobin / DSA](#jacobin-dsa) — It treats the Democratic sweep as proof that "the Left can win" inside the Democratic Party and proposes a socialist caucus within it, not a break from it.
- [Left Voice](#left-voice) — It is sharper on the Democrats' lack of answers and on the DSA's fusion with Hochul, but the teasers propose no party independent of the trade union bureaucracy.

- [Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)](#socialist-appeal-rcp-imt) — It asks "when will it be Britain's turn?" as if revolution arrives by contagion, and leaves out the question of leadership.

### Jacobin / DSA

- **<a href="https://jacobin.com/2026/11/midterms-socialists-democrats-el-sayed" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"In last night's midterms, the left proved it can win"</a>** by Branko Marcetic. Marcetic argues the results refute Democratic leaders who claim socialist policies lose votes, pointing to El-Sayed and the DSA candidates. **Position:** The Democratic Party is treated as a contested terrain to be won. Missing is that the "left" winners were elected on a ticket headed by Jeffries, who promises "bipartisan common ground," and that El-Sayed retreated on Gaza and Iran during the campaign.
- **<a href="https://jacobin.com/2026/11/democratic-party-congress-socialist-caucus-dsa" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"We need a socialist congressional caucus"</a>**. The author, French, proposes that DSA members of Congress organise as a bloc to "build power." **Position:** It is the "party within a party" strategy that has kept socialists inside the Democratic Party since Michael Harrington's realignment project. The caucus would sit inside a Democratic majority whose members vote for Jeffries as speaker, as the WSWS Perspective notes. It is a plan to give the Democrats left cover, not to organise the working class.
- **<a href="https://jacobin.com/2026/11/trump-republicans-midterms-defeat-pacs" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"The Donald Trump era in American politics is ending"</a>** by Heideman. It reads Trump's decline as more room for the left. **Position:** It shares the bourgeois press's "the system has held" view, treating Trump as an electoral aberration that is now passing rather than as the product of the financial oligarchy, which remains in power.

**Assessment:** Jacobin's line is now openly about taking a share of a Democratic majority. It does not raise the war, which 74 percent of voters called a major factor, as something the new Congress must end.

### Left Voice

- **<a href="https://www.leftvoice.org/trump-lost-the-midterms-but-the-fight-against-war-ice-and-austerity-continues/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"Trump lost the midterms, but the fight against war, ICE, and austerity continues"</a>.** It says the Democrats have no answer to the crisis that produced Trump and calls for continued struggle. **Position:** The criticism of the Democrats is real, but "continued struggle" is offered without a party or a program. The teaser does not mention building a working-class party independent of both capitalist parties and of the union apparatus.
- **<a href="https://www.leftvoice.org/dsa-won-big-in-new-york-but-so-did-the-democratic-establishment/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"DSA won big in New York, but so did the Democratic establishment"</a>.** It notes that Mamdani-endorsed candidates won alongside Hochul. **Position:** It describes the DSA-Democratic merger as a problem of the DSA "establishment" while still treating the DSA as part of the left to be pressured. Its French co-thinkers in Révolution permanente take the same stance toward the CGT, which the PES statement criticises today.

**Assessment:** Left Voice is critical in tone but advances no alternative, and its French sister group pressures the union bureaucracy in the same way.

### Liberation News / PSL

- **<a href="https://liberationnews.org/psl-statement-the-democratic-wave-and-the-fight-ahead/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"PSL statement: The Democratic wave and the fight ahead"</a>.** It presents the November 3 results as a defeat for Trump and the Republicans and stresses the role of billionaire money. **Position:** The search snippets suggest a "people versus billionaires" framing that blurs the class character of the Democratic Party, which is itself financed by billionaires. The PSL ran its own candidate in Ohio (Greg Levy), but its electoral work is a protest-reformist platform tied to the party's Stalinist "multipolar" foreign policy, which presents capitalist Russia, China and Iran as anti-imperialist forces.

**Assessment:** The PSL presents itself as independent of the Democrats while promoting the class-neutral "anti-billionaire" populism that Democratic campaigns also use.

### Socialist Worker (SWP)

- **<a href="https://socialistworker.co.uk/news/healthworkers-say-we-want-to-end-palantirs-nhs-contract/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"We want to end Palantir's NHS contract"</a>.** Health workers campaign against the US surveillance and military-AI firm's data contract with the NHS. **Position:** The opposition to Palantir is legitimate, but it is presented as a single-issue campaign aimed at a Labour government that is being asked to reverse course. It is not tied to Palantir's role in the Gaza genocide and the Iran war, or to Starmer's rearmament drive.

**Assessment:** The SWP's output stays at the level of campaigns and protest. Its November 20 youth strike agitation, reviewed yesterday, has the same weakness: the movement is invited to pressure Labour rather than break with it.

### Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

- **<a href="https://communist.red/europe-in-revolt-when-will-it-be-britains-turn/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">"Europe in revolt: When will it be Britain's turn?"</a>** by Adam Booth. This editorial on the European movement asks when Britain will follow. **Position:** The headline frames revolution as something that spreads by example, waiting for British workers to "catch" France's mood. It avoids the decisive question the PES statement raises: the trade union bureaucracies and LFI-type parties, as in 1968 and 2023, will try to wind the movement down, and committees of action independent of them are needed. The IMT's history of decades inside the Labour Party and its recent rebranding as a "communist" party do not answer the question of leadership. They show the RCP adapting to radicalised youth without settling accounts with its own past.

**Assessment:** The tone is revolutionary but the content is spontaneist. Nothing in the teasers takes up the role of the union bureaucracy or the need for an international party.

**Convergence and references:** No direct attacks on the ICFI or WSWS appeared. Jacobin and the bourgeois press share the "Trump era is ending, the system has held" view, and that is the day's most significant convergence.
