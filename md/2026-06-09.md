### 1. 100th day of US-Israeli war: ceasefire shatters in worst missile exchange since April

The April 2026 ceasefire between Israel and Iran collapsed over the weekend in the most serious exchange of fire in two months, on the eve of the 100th day of the US-Israeli war of aggression launched February 28. Iran fired ballistic missiles at Israeli cities — the first such direct bombardment since the April truce — in response to Israeli airstrikes on Beirut's southern suburbs targeting Hezbollah. Israel retaliated with what it called a "large-scale strike on strategic defense systems belonging to the Iranian regime," sending dozens of fighter jets against air defenses, missile launchers and a Revolutionary Guards petrochemical facility in central and western Iran.

Air-raid sirens sounded across Israel, including Jerusalem, before Iran announced a "suspension" of attacks and Netanyahu declared the front "contained." Iran's armed forces said they had "delivered a painful response." Trump publicly urged restraint to protect a second 60-day negotiating round meant to address Iran's nuclear program and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, but threatened, "I'm going to blow the hell out of them" if talks fail.

Writing on the WSWS, Andre Damon noted that after 100 days, "the Trump administration has failed to achieve any stated war objective" — neither overthrowing the Iranian government, crippling its military, nor opening the Strait of Hormuz.

Key facts:
- Iran's missile barrage wounded at least 15 people in Israel.
- The Lebanon front has killed more than 3,600 people since March and displaced over one million.
- House members voted **324–92** to block a War Powers resolution requiring withdrawal of US forces, demonstrating bipartisan support for the war.
- The Wall Street Journal editorial board demanded greater escalation.

### 2. Global armed conflicts hit highest level since World War II

Uppsala University's Conflict Data Program (UCDP) reported that 2025 saw **65 active armed conflicts** — the highest total since the end of World War II — and **244,600 conflict deaths**, the most since the 1994 Rwandan genocide and up sharply from 187,000 in 2024. The number of interstate (state-versus-state) conflicts doubled to **eight**, the highest since UCDP began collecting data in 1946.

The data captures, in statistical form, the breakdown of the post-1945 order that the WSWS has analyzed as the eruption of a third world war. Researchers warned 2026 shows no signs of any reduction.

Key facts:
- **13 conflicts** reached "war" status, defined as 1,000-plus battlefield deaths per year.
- Deadliest: Russia-Ukraine (77,700 Russian and 14,000 Ukrainian deaths), Israel-Hamas (14,400) and Sudan (12,200).
- Senior analyst Shawn Davies: "We are seeing a clear increase in conflicts between states," previously a rare phenomenon.
- Therése Pettersson: "We see a dramatic increase in violence directed against civilians, particularly in Sudan."

### 5. Federal judge strikes down Trump's $100,000 H-1B visa fee

US District Judge Leo Sorokin of Boston issued a 42-page ruling vacating Trump's $100,000 H-1B visa fee nationwide, concluding it constitutes an **unlawful tax that Congress never authorized** and an instance of executive-branch overreach. The fee, announced in September 2025, had replaced the prior $2,000–$5,000 per-application cost. The lawsuit was brought by 20 Democratic state attorneys general; the Justice Department said it would appeal.

The ruling deals a blow to Trump's campaign to restrict foreign-worker hiring in the tech sector. As a socialist analysis would stress, the H-1B regime is used by tech capital to suppress wages across the sector regardless of the fee, while immigration restriction fuels nationalist divisions among workers.

### 6. US Apache helicopter crashes near Strait of Hormuz, rescued by sea drone in military first

A US Army AH-64 Apache attack helicopter went down off the coast of Oman near the Strait of Hormuz on June 9. Both crew members were rescued within roughly two hours — notably by **unmanned sea drones**, described by the US military as the first such sea-drone rescue operation ever conducted. Both soldiers are in stable condition; Trump posted, "Our pilots are fine."

Apache helicopters have been central to US enforcement of the oil blockade on Iranian crude exports, patrolling tanker lanes and pressuring Tehran. The cause remains under investigation, with no hostile fire confirmed. The incident underscores the human cost of sustaining a blockade that has spiked global energy prices.

### Four migrant farmworkers burned alive in Italy in escalating wave of arson

On June 1 in Amendolara, Calabria, four agricultural workers — Waseem Khan (Pakistani, 29), Amin Fazal Khogjani (Afghan, 28), Ullah Ismat Qiemi (Afghan, 19) and Safi Iayjad (Afghan, 27) — were locked in a minivan and burned alive by gangmasters who had withheld their wages since April 20. CCTV allegedly shows suspects pouring flammable liquid inside, jamming the doors and igniting the vehicle; a fifth worker escaped through the rear window with severe burns.

The four were among an estimated 400,000–430,000 farmworkers in Italy trapped in the *caporalato* system of criminal labor intermediation. The killings followed 14 other vehicle arsons targeting Pakistani migrants in Calabria in preceding months. New EU asylum regulations taking effect June 12 permit the detention of migrants — including families with children — for up to 30 months.

### USMCA renegotiation faces hurdles ahead of July 1 deadline

The July 1 USMCA renegotiation deadline is unlikely to produce a final accord, with analysts expecting annual review processes extending up to ten years and creating persistent uncertainty for roughly $2 trillion in trilateral trade. The central dispute is the US demand for 50% American-sourced vehicle content and an overall regional-content threshold rising from 75% to 82% — a requirement Mexico's economy minister called "unsustainable" and a Toyota executive described as a "poison pill."

Canada initially declined to participate before its trade minister recommended full 16-year renewal. Mexico, facing stalled growth, has already conceded by imposing 50% tariffs on Chinese goods at US insistence. The WSWS frames the renegotiation as a US drive to rebuild domestic manufacturing and counter China at workers' expense.

### US measles cases top 2,030 across 40 states, elimination status at risk

As of June 4, the US has recorded **2,030 confirmed measles cases** in 2026 across 40 states and 30 outbreaks. Some 93% of cases are outbreak-associated, 92% involve unvaccinated individuals, and 127 patients (6%) have been hospitalized. MMR vaccination coverage among US schoolchildren has slipped from 95.2% in 2019–20 to 92.5% in 2024–25. The 2026 total is approaching the 2025 record of 2,288 cases — the highest since 1991 — and authorities will formally review the country's measles elimination status in November.

### H5N1 airborne transmission confirmed on US dairy farms

A May 2026 study sampling 14 infected dairy farms detected H5N1 virus in the air during milking, in cows' breath and in farm wastewater, pointing to multiple transmission routes beyond direct milk contact. Total US human cases stand at **71 (two deaths)** — 41 linked to dairy cows and 26 to infected poultry. A NEJM case series documents clinical presentations ranging from mild conjunctivitis to severe respiratory illness; WHO logged 13 avian/swine influenza human cases globally in Q1 2026, including a fatal pediatric case in Bangladesh. Airborne aerosol detection sharply raises biosafety concerns for farm workers, even as overall public risk is assessed as low.

## Arts and culture

The Tribeca Film Festival (June 3–14) has accepted **_Dreams of Violets_**, a feature produced entirely by the AI studio Fountain 0 — the first fully AI-generated film to screen at a major American festival, with a jury that includes director Mira Nair and rapper Nas. The selection forces a long-deferred reckoning over authorship, craft and what counts as cinema. Far from a neutral technical advance, the embrace of wholesale machine-generated film arrives as studios seek to eliminate the living labor of writers, performers and crews — the very workers whose 2023 strikes warned of this trajectory. The question is not whether the tools exist but in whose interest, and against whom, they are deployed.

The death of **Marjane Satrapi** at 56 in Paris on June 4 is a major loss for graphic literature and dissident art. Her _Persepolis_ — chronicling a childhood under the Iranian theocracy and exile in Europe — remains one of the most widely read works about life under political repression, a humane and unsparing account that refuses both Western orientalism and apology for clerical reaction. Her family cited grief over the recent loss of her husband. Her death falls in the 100th day of an imperialist war waged in the name of the very Iranian people whose suffering she chronicled — a war that bombs Iran while invoking its dissidents.

Roundup:
- **Literature:** Yáng Shuāng-zǐ's **_Taiwan Travelogue_**, translated from Chinese by Lin King, won the International Booker Prize at London's Tate Modern, spotlighting a Taiwanese literary tradition rarely reaching Western readers amid acute geopolitical tension over the island.
- **Theater:** National Queer Theater's **Criminal Queerness Festival** opened June 9 at HERE Arts Center, running through June 27 as the official theater event of NYC Pride, platforming LGBTQ+ playwrights from countries that criminalize same-sex identity — directly linking the stage to political repression.
- **Arts defunding:** More than **$27 million in NEA grants** have been terminated nationwide, the Kennedy Center's Social Impact division dissolved, and artist Amy Sherald canceled her National Portrait Gallery exhibition over censorship concerns — a federal assault on the arts now rivaling the 1990s NEA culture wars in scope, but with broader structural consequences.
- **Music:** Two-time Grammy winner **Peabo Bryson** died June 2 at 75; his voice on "Beauty and the Beast" and "A Whole New World" defined a golden era of film balladry and mainstream romantic R&B.
- **History:** Pulitzer-winning historian **Gordon S. Wood** died at 92 (see WSWS coverage below).

## WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

**Perspective published:** No Perspective carried a June 9 date. The most recent Perspective is "**100 days of the US-Israeli war of aggression against Iran**" by the WSWS Editorial Board (URL dated June 8, body dated June 7).

The editorial situates the war in world-historical terms. The Board frames Iran's clerical regime as reactionary and nationalist but insists that unconditional working-class opposition to imperialist aggression is obligatory, because the war represents not a contest between "democracy and theocracy" but an assertion of US imperial dominance in the Middle East. It documents the military trajectory — 3,593 dead in Lebanon, over one million displaced, and 3,468 Iranians killed, including children — while none of the fundamental US-Israeli objectives have been achieved: Iran's government has not been overthrown, its military has not been crippled, and the Strait of Hormuz has not been opened. This failure, the Board argues, exposes the limits of imperialist power even when wielded with overwhelming technological advantage.

The editorial catalogs the economic consequences: global gold reserves have surpassed the euro as the world's second-largest reserve asset, reflecting flight from dollar instruments as US credibility erodes; the US national debt has passed $39 trillion; and gas prices have risen more than 50 percent. Trump's negotiating posture is characterized as fraudulent — his threat that "I'm going to blow the hell out of them" is cited as proof no genuine diplomatic track exists, the administration's demands being designed to be rejected as pretexts for escalation.

A central theme is the relationship between war and the class struggle. The Board argues the war has "deepened the global capitalist crisis and escalated the global class struggle," surveying Long Island Rail Road strikes, JBS meatpacking walkouts and US teacher strikes, alongside general strikes in Belgium and Italy and labor actions in Kenya and India, as manifestations of the same systemic crisis that produced the war. It rejects pacifism that stops at "ending the war" without confronting the system that generates war, and criticizes social-democratic and Stalinist tendencies for tailing nationalist resistance. Citing the 324–92 House vote as numerical proof of bipartisan war consensus, the Board concludes that the task is to "build an international socialist movement capable of transforming opposition to war into a conscious political movement aimed at abolishing capitalism itself," with the World Party of Socialist Revolution (Fourth International) as its organizational embodiment.

### Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin's output centers on the June 23 New York Democratic primaries. "**NYC Socialists Are Trying to Expand Their Electoral Wins**" promotes a DSA slate of state-legislative and congressional candidates, riding momentum from Zohran Mamdani's election as mayor and AOC's endorsements of three challengers. A companion piece, "**We Need a Left-Labor Presidential Candidate**," argues the working class needs a standard-bearer inside the Democratic primaries, dovetailing with DSA's national-convention resolution to run a 2028 Democratic presidential candidate.

The line is the consummate expression of the pseudo-left's strategic orientation: the subordination of working-class political independence to the Democratic Party — the very party that the same week voted 324–92 to continue the Iran war. By presenting electoral capture of Democratic ballot lines as "socialism," Jacobin demobilizes opposition to war and austerity and channels it back into the institutions responsible for both. Mamdani's mayoralty — which Jacobin itself describes as caught between "managing capitalism" and "overturning it" — is the demonstration in practice that administering the capitalist state means enforcing its priorities.

### Left Voice

Left Voice's most prominent recent piece, "**The Left Must Defend the PSL and All of Those Facing State Repression**," correctly opposes state attacks on left organizations. But the framing — a defense of "the left" as an undifferentiated bloc — blurs precisely the political distinctions a Marxist must draw, lumping a Stalinist-influenced formation together with genuine revolutionary opposition and substituting movementist solidarity for a struggle over program. Left Voice's coverage of the Iran war continues to orient toward street mobilizations and the academic-left milieu rather than the independent political mobilization of the working class.

### PSL / Liberation News

The PSL's statements — "**Stop Trump's War on Iran!**" and "Why Trump failed in his criminal war against Iran" — denounce the bombing but present the war as essentially Trump's personal crime, to be reversed by sustained "pressure." Absent is any reckoning with the bipartisan character of the war or with US imperialism as a systemic product of capitalism; the implicit horizon is a return to a more "rational," multilateral US foreign policy. The orientation tails Iranian and bourgeois-nationalist forces and frames the antiwar struggle as a single-issue campaign detached from the fight against the capitalist order.

### Socialist Alternative

Socialist Alternative (ISA) continues to treat the Gaza genocide and the war on Iran as discrete questions, evading the reality of a single, escalating imperialist offensive across Lebanon, Gaza and Iran — a separation the WSWS specifically polemicized against in its April critique. Its coverage emphasizes building "mass movements" and pressure campaigns while avoiding a programmatic break with the union bureaucracy and the Democratic Party milieu it courts. The result is an adaptation to spontaneity that leaves the political leadership of struggles in reformist hands.

### SWP (UK) and IMT/RCP

The British SWP frames the Iran war as fuel for protest-building, subordinating analysis to the next demonstration and its perennial front campaigns. The IMT/RCP, meanwhile, channels radicalizing youth toward parliamentary-reformist regroupment around figures such as Corbyn and Sultana — what the WSWS has identified as the substitution of left rhetoric for a genuine fight to build an independent revolutionary party. Both tendencies treat the danger of wider war as an organizing opportunity rather than as the central strategic question demanding the political independence of the working class.
