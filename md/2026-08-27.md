### 1. Himalayan glacier collapse kills more than 350 in Nepal and Tibet

A catastrophic glacial collapse on the Nepal–Tibet border on August 26 has killed more than 350 people and left over 1,300 missing, including foreign trekkers and pilgrims travelling the Kathmandu–Kerung corridor. Satellite imagery analyzed by glaciologists shows that a substantial section of the lower tongue of a glacier detached at roughly 5,200 metres elevation and fell some 1,200 metres onto the valley floor, gathering rock and sediment as it descended and striking the Lhende river about 20 km northeast of the Nepal–China border crossing. The debris dammed the river; when the natural dam failed, it released a wall of water, mud and rock several stories high through the Lhende Khola valley.

More than 5,000 Nepalese army and police personnel are engaged in search and recovery. Helicopters are supplying tents and food to villages cut off by destroyed roads. Officials expect the toll to climb sharply as crews reach areas still buried under meters of mud.

Key facts:
- Water levels in the valley rose approximately 27 feet in 30 minutes, according to survivor accounts and hydrological reconstruction
- Nepal has lost close to one-third of its total ice volume over the past three decades; the Hindu Kush–Himalaya region is warming faster than the global average
- Nepalese authorities reported 157 bodies recovered and 54 people rescued in the first 24 hours; Anadolu put the confirmed toll at 160 with 680 missing on Thursday morning before it climbed further
- This is the worst disaster in the region since the 2015 earthquake, and follows the 2024 Thame flood and the 2025 Uttarakhand flash flood in the same glacial-lake-outburst pattern
- No functioning early-warning system existed on the Lhende; glacial-lake and ice-collapse monitoring in the region remains almost entirely dependent on episodic international grant funding

### 4. Supreme Court clears the way for Trump's mail-in voting order

The Supreme Court on August 24 lifted, 6-3 along ideological lines, a Massachusetts district court injunction that had blocked core provisions of Trump's March executive order restricting mail-in voting. The order directs the Department of Homeland Security to compile a federal list of citizens deemed eligible to vote and instructs the US Postal Service to accept mail ballots only from voters appearing on that list. The injunction had covered California and 22 other states that sued.

The majority acted on procedural standing grounds rather than the merits. Justice Sotomayor, joined by Kagan and Jackson, wrote in dissent that the decision "does not address whether the President's attempts to interfere with states' administration of the November 2026 elections are lawful," and warned that the ruling permits the government to foment chaos in the run-up to the vote. A second injunction remains in force, so the order is not yet fully operative — but the practical effect is to hand the executive branch a mechanism for policing ballot access ten weeks before the midterms.

Key facts:
- The executive order was issued in March 2026; the district court injunction dated from June
- Roughly a third of all ballots cast in recent US federal elections have been mail ballots
- Barry Grey argues on the WSWS that the ruling is a step in a strategy aimed at overriding or suspending the midterm elections, and that the Democratic Party's response has been confined to litigation and press statements

### 5. Trump administration designates Palestine Action a "global terrorist" organization

The US Treasury on August 26 designated the British-based direct action group Palestine Action a "specially designated global terrorist," calling it a "violent far-left terrorist network." The designation freezes any US-linked assets and criminalizes the provision of funds, goods or services to the organization. It is the first time a British left-wing organization has been targeted under US terrorism sanctions.

Treasury's statement accused the group of promoting "its violent, criminal, and terrorist acts on social media" and encouraging "the use of similar tactics internationally," including inside the United States and at the US–Mexico border. Palestine Action's actions have consisted principally of property damage at arms plants supplying Israel — most prominently at Elbit Systems facilities.

Key facts:
- Two additional organizations were designated the same day: Autistici/Inventati, an Italian collective that Treasury described as supplying "digital architecture, tools and services" to antifascist networks, and Masar Badil, which Treasury alleged operates as a front for the Popular Front for the Liberation of Palestine
- The Autistici/Inventati listing is the first US terrorism designation of a European digital-infrastructure and encrypted-communications provider
- The designation extends the extraterritorial reach of US terrorism law to political organizations lawfully constituted in allied countries, and creates direct exposure for anyone in the US who donates to, hosts, or provides services to them

### 6. Meta settles child-addiction suits for $17 billion

Meta agreed on August 26 to pay up to $17 billion over ten years and to impose design changes on Facebook and Instagram, ending a landmark trial brought by state attorneys general. The settlement resolves claims filed by 47 states and cuts short the trial then underway involving California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey — four of the 29 states that sued in 2023. The states had alleged Meta deliberately engineered features to addict children and concealed internal research on the resulting harm. Some wire reports put the headline figure at $18 billion including related state claims.

Key facts:
- The settlement requires Meta to cap under-18 users at two hours of daily use, block overnight access, restrict usage during school hours, add age verification and expand parental controls
- Meta reported 2025 revenue of $201 billion; the entire ten-year payout amounts to roughly one month of current revenue
- The underlying litigation had sought damages of up to $1.4 trillion
- No individual executive faces liability, and the agreement contains no admission of wrongdoing

### Islamabad hospital nursery fire kills 14 newborns

A fire tore through the nursery of the Pakistan Institute of Medical Sciences (PIMS) in Islamabad at around 7 a.m. local time on August 26, killing 14 of the 15 newborns inside. Relatives reported that the nursery was locked, and grief turned rapidly to anger outside the country's largest government hospital. Pakistan's public hospital system has been subject to more than a decade of IMF-conditioned austerity, with maintenance and fire-safety budgets among the first casualties.

### Volkswagen breakup plan meets resistance across German plants

Volkswagen's "Group Target Picture 2030" — the largest restructuring in the company's 89-year history — schedules the end of production at Zwickau and Emden in 2031, at the Hanover commercial vehicle works in 2032, and at Audi Neckarsulm in 2034, with up to 100,000 jobs eliminated group-wide. Dietmar Gaisenkersting reports on the WSWS that VW has meanwhile identified a previously undisclosed €6 billion cash position and paid CEO Oliver Blume €7.4 million in 2025. ### US measles reaches a 35-year high with first deaths of 2026

The CDC's latest tally records 2,777 confirmed measles cases across 47 jurisdictions in 2026 — the highest US total since 1991, and more than the entire cumulative US count for 2000 through 2024. Some 94% of cases (2,614) are linked to 36 active outbreaks, and 93% are in unvaccinated individuals, overwhelmingly children and adolescents.

Benjamin Mateus argues on the WSWS that this is not an accident of "hesitancy" but the outcome of a bipartisan dismantling of public health infrastructure, compounded by the removal of vaccines from the federal recommended childhood schedule.

## Arts and culture

**Péter Nádas, 1942–2026.** The Hungarian novelist died at his home in Gombosszeg on the morning of August 26, aged 83. Nádas began as a photojournalist and reporter under the Kádár regime and turned to fiction after the censors made journalism impossible for him. *A Book of Memories* (1986), written over eleven years and withheld from publication by Hungarian censorship, interleaves three narrative strands to reconstruct the psychic texture of life under Stalinist rule; Susan Sontag called it "the greatest novel written in our time, and one of the great books of the century." *Parallel Stories* (2005), a 1,200-page work spanning from 1939 Berlin to post-1989 Budapest, is among the most ambitious attempts in postwar European literature to render the twentieth century's catastrophes — fascism, the Holocaust, Stalinism, and the restoration of capitalism — as they were registered in individual bodies and consciousnesses. Nádas refused both the nationalist mythology promoted by the Orbán government and the liberal triumphalism of 1989. He was a perennial Nobel contender who never received the prize.

**Dolly Parton, 1946–2026.** The country singer and songwriter died in Nashville on August 25 at 80, after what her publicist described as a brief battle with cancer following admission to Vanderbilt University Medical Center with kidney and autoimmune complications. Parton was the fourth of twelve children born into rural poverty in Sevier County, Tennessee, and her strongest work drew directly on that material: "Coat of Many Colors," "In the Good Old Days (When Times Were Bad)," and "9 to 5," which remains one of the few genuinely popular songs in the American canon organized entirely around the experience of wage labor and the resentment of the boss. Her later career was substantially given over to the Dollywood enterprise and to a carefully maintained apolitical public persona, which the music industry rewarded and which flattened the class content of her best songwriting. The recorded work from 1967 through the mid-1970s is nonetheless a significant body of American vernacular art.

**Tim Curry, 1945–2026.** The English actor died at his Los Angeles home on August 25 at 80. Curry trained in repertory and came to prominence in the original 1973 London stage production of *The Rocky Horror Show*, whose 1975 film adaptation made him a permanent fixture of a subculture built around the film's midnight screenings. His range — from the sinister precision of *Clue* to the malevolence of *It* — was consistently underused by an industry that could not classify him. A stroke in 2012 confined him largely to voice work; his memoir *Vagabond* appeared last year.

### Philippine DHL workers strike against warehouse closure — Dante Pastrana

More than 430 DHL Supply Chain workers in Muntinlupa City have been on strike since August 17 against a warehouse closure. DHL attributed the closure to a lost client contract; the union states the contract was in fact renewed. The newly certified union was hampered by mandatory legal cooling-off requirements that delayed strike action while the company proceeded with the shutdown. Pastrana argues that the SENTRO labor federation and Akbayan channelled the dispute into negotiations that had no leverage, and calls for rank-and-file committees linking DHL workers internationally — the company operates in over 220 countries, and its Philippine logistics operations are integrated into the same global network as its European and North American facilities.

### Chilean students protest strip-searches as Kast government, media remain silent — Our reporter

Students at a lyceum in Quellón, on Chiloé Island, walked out after police strip-searched two classmates over an unsubstantiated theft allegation. The action came days after President José Antonio Kast's "Protected Schools" law embedded police directly in school disciplinary procedures. Government data show more than 56,000 police interventions in Chilean schools during 2025 alone. The article notes that the incident was ignored by national media as well as by the government. Its political conclusion is that students cannot obtain redress through the same state apparatus that is constructing the policing machinery directed at them, and that the defense of democratic rights in the schools requires a turn to the working class.

### Review into fatal New Zealand landslide finds multiple warnings were ignored — Chris Egan

A judicial review has found the January 2026 Mount Maunganui landslide, which killed six people at a council-run campground, was preventable. Hazard assessments identifying the slope risk date back to 1999. An evacuation plan had been drafted and costed at a modest sum but was never implemented. On the morning of the collapse there were multiple visible warning signs, none of which triggered closure of the site. Egan's argument is that the deaths follow the standard logic of council-operated commercial facilities under fiscal pressure — revenue from peak-season occupancy weighed against a capital expenditure that was repeatedly deferred.

### Montréal airport ramp worker Oscar Robayo killed; coworker denounces safety violations — WSWS reporting team

Two articles: the initial report and a follow-up interview. Robayo was crushed during an Airbus A350 pushback operation at Montréal-Trudeau. ### Jacobin / DSA

Jacobin's lead piece, **"Democrats Are Losing the Billionaire Primary"** (Conor Lynch), argues that wealthy donors are migrating to the Republicans in response to anti-billionaire sentiment in the Democratic base, and that this forces the party to choose "between pursuing major donors or building grassroots movements." The framing takes as given that the Democratic Party is an appropriate vehicle for working-class politics and that its class character is determined by its funding sources rather than by its role as a party of American imperialism. The question of the party's function — as the second party of Wall Street and the Pentagon — does not arise.

**"The United Auto Workers Are Voting for Their Top Leadership"** (Luis Feliz Leon and Jane Slaughter) presents the election as a referendum on whether "reformist president Shawn Fain continues restructuring the union," reproducing the Labor Notes framework in which the UAW's problems are matters of leadership quality and internal democracy rather than the bureaucracy's structural integration into corporate management. **"The Fight Over Data Centers Is Dividing the Labor Movement"** (Ginny Hogan) treats the conflict between construction trades pushing for data-center buildout and workers facing its energy and water costs as a problem of finding "common ground among labor unions" — an appeal to inter-bureaucratic negotiation rather than an independent working-class program on AI infrastructure.

*Assessment:* The day's output orients entirely toward the internal management of the Democratic Party and the union apparatus, with no reference to the mail-in voting ruling, the Palestine Action designation, or the boat strikes.

### Left Voice

Left Voice published three pieces in a single morning, all on DSA's crisis. **"What Does It Mean to Engage in Mass Politics? A Response to Eric Blanc"** is the most substantial: it correctly observes that Blanc "makes electoral reach the principal test of a correct political line" and that his framework "revises the program around what those officials can plausibly defend while governing and running as Democrats." But Left Voice's own conclusion is to fight for a better program *inside* the DSA — to connect "immediate anti-carceral fights to the need to abolish the capitalist system" — rather than to draw the conclusion that a party running candidates on the Democratic ballot line cannot be reformed into an instrument of socialist revolution.

**"AOC and Mamdani Should Apologize for Their Anti-Black Comments"** is a straightforward capitulation to racial politics. The article's grievance against Ocasio-Cortez's repudiation of "defund" is framed in terms of racial constituency arithmetic — "you will not get there without the support of African American women and Afro-Latinx people," citing Pew's estimate of 6 million Afro-Latino adults. The demand is an apology, not a break. **"The Labor Movement Must Fight for TPS Recipients Facing Deportation"** makes a correct call for permanent status and full rights for all immigrants, but addresses that demand to "the labor movement" — that is, to the AFL-CIO apparatus that has facilitated the very insecurity described.

*Assessment:* Left Voice's criticisms of the DSA right wing are often accurate in detail while remaining wholly within the DSA's orbit; the racialist framing of the AOC article is a direct adaptation to petty-bourgeois identity politics.

### Liberation News / PSL

*Assessment:* The PSL functions as a left cover for the Democratic Party's progressive wing while its own program remains confined to municipal reform demands.

### Socialist Alternative

**"Support Left Independent Candidates — Build An Alternative To The Two Corporate Parties"** advances an electoral formula that, in practice, means backing candidates emerging from the DSA-progressive milieu. **"Trump's DHS Goes After Left Groups & Unions: We Won't Be Intimidated"** correctly identifies the state repression directed at left organizations, but its answer is defensive protest rather than a political struggle against the Democratic Party's complicity in constructing the apparatus. **"Massachusetts Rent Control: Betrayals & Lessons For The Working Class"** documents Democratic betrayals at the state level without drawing organizational conclusions.

### 7. The death of Péter Nádas and the fate of the twentieth-century European novel

The Hungarian novelist, who died August 26 at 83, produced in *A Book of Memories* and *Parallel Stories* one of the most serious literary reckonings with fascism, Stalinism and capitalist restoration written in Eastern Europe.
