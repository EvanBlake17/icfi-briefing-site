### 1. Spanish high-speed rail collision kills at least 68 after safety system fails

At about 8:53 a.m. on Wednesday, October 28, a southbound Renfe AVE high-speed train from Madrid to Seville ran into the rear of a stationary train run by the private operator Iryo. The crash happened near Urda in Toledo province, about 150 km south of Madrid. Two Iryo carriages derailed and fell down an embankment. The Castilla-La Mancha regional government puts the dead at **at least 68**. Regional president Emiliano García-Page said about 80 people were injured, around 50 seriously and 15 critically, and warned that some might not survive. The WSWS, citing hospital figures, reports 107 hospitalized. More than 200 troops of the Military Emergencies Unit were deployed. The rail infrastructure operator ADIF has suspended all high-speed service between Madrid and Andalusia.

Transport Minister Óscar Puente said the **LZB automatic train-control system failed at about 8:30 a.m.** along a 63 km (39-mile) stretch, affecting six trains. Control passed to the Atocha control post, which cleared the Renfe train to continue as normal. According to Puente, the train was manually authorized past a red signal. He called this "inexplicable" and put responsibility on the control centre, which is run by ADIF, a state body under his own ministry. He has refused to resign. Iryo disputes part of his account. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez declared three days of official mourning (October 29–31) and said "two train accidents in one year" were unacceptable. He insisted the causes "appeared unrelated." The crash comes nine months after the Adamuz disaster on the same line, involving the same two operators. Al Jazeera puts the Adamuz death toll at 46 and the WSWS at 42. Spain goes to snap elections on November 29.

As Santiago Guillen and Alex Lantier write on the WSWS, the crash follows years of rail liberalization, which "multiplied traffic without a matching rise in maintenance capacity." Over the same period military spending doubled to €34 billion.

Key facts:
- Passengers: 397 on the Renfe train, 147 on the Iryo train (regional spokeswoman Esther Padilla).
- Engineer Ricardo Díaz Martínez: "human error cannot by itself cause such a tragedy because there are automated security systems."
- José Trigueros of the Civil Engineering Association: "I hold the minister responsible."
- The CSIF civil servants' union demands Puente's resignation. It says it warned in September about excessive working hours at ADIF. It has also defended the presumption of innocence for ADIF's head of traffic.
- After Adamuz, the government promised safety checks on all lines nationwide.
- Ursula von der Leyen: "Today, Europe mourns with you." Emmanuel Macron also sent condolences.

### 2. Israeli election produces deadlock as Likud claims vote was "stolen by Arab parties"

Israel's October 27 election, the first since October 7, 2023, has given neither bloc a majority. With about 90 percent of votes counted:

- Netanyahu's bloc (Likud, Otzma Yehudit, Religious Zionism, Shas, United Torah Judaism) has **53–54 seats**.
- The bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief, has **47–48**.
- The Joint List and Ra'am together have a projected **record 19**.

A majority requires 61 of 120 seats. Between 480,000 and 500,000 "double-envelope" ballots, mostly from soldiers, police and diplomats, have yet to be counted. Final results could come Thursday evening, and the official certification is set for November 4. Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister. President Isaac Herzog begins consultations next week, and whoever he designates gets up to 42 days to form a coalition.

The Arab vote decided the outcome. Turnout among Palestinian citizens rose from about 55 percent in 2022 to **over 80 percent**, almost doubling the Arab parties' 2022 total of 10 seats. Likud responded with a statement charging that "the elections in the State of Israel were stolen by Arab parties that support terrorism," citing "bribery, threats, violence, forgeries and prohibited software." Netanyahu and Eisenkot have both ruled out a minority government that depends on Arab parties. Eisenkot said that joining with them "is against my principles," and that his aim is "a Zionist mobilisation government." Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen said his alliance could back an anti-Netanyahu government from outside but would not join one. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera expect a Netanyahu-Eisenkot unity government. The Tel Aviv 125 index fell as much as 3.5 percent on Wednesday morning.

Chris Marsden writes on the WSWS that the result "decisively refuted" the "concept of a more progressive form of Zionism." The Western-backed Eisenkot alternative has collapsed, he argues, leaving Netanyahu free to pursue "a renewed war against Iran planned with Trump," possibly once the US midterms are over.

Key facts:
- The parties in Eisenkot's bloc won 46 seats in 2022. Their current projected totals are Yashar 21, Together 11, Yisrael Beiteinu 7–8, Democrats 8.
- Shas fell from 11 seats to about seven after ultra-Orthodox voters stayed home in protest at the failure to pass a yeshiva draft exemption.
- Before the vote Trump told Israelis that choosing "the wrong Leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi," would force the US to act against Iran itself.
- Eisenkot wrote the "Dahiya doctrine" of disproportionate force against civilian areas. He sat in Netanyahu's war cabinet for eight months of the Gaza genocide and rejects a two-state solution.
- A Channel 12 poll found that 74 percent of first-time voters aged 18–22 call themselves right or centre-right.

### 4. Flavio Bolsonaro escapes Brazil on foot and declares himself "true president" from Miami

Brazil's Supreme Court had ordered him to hand over his regular and diplomatic passports so he could not leave before being investigated for embezzlement and money laundering. Brazil and the US have an extradition treaty, but Trump has called the Bolsonaros "fine people," and Holman expects no quick return. His father made the same trip to Florida after losing in 2022, and his supporters stormed Brasília's government buildings while he was there. Jair Bolsonaro is now under house arrest.

### 5. UN experts condemn French repression as Macron threatens state of emergency

Four experts appointed by the UN Human Rights Council, including Gina Romero, special rapporteur on freedom of peaceful assembly, and Farida Shaheed, issued a statement on Wednesday condemning the "excessive use of force" by French authorities against the nationwide student protest movement. "The scale of the arrests and detention, affecting several thousand people, mainly minors, appears disproportionate," they wrote. They called serious and sometimes irreversible injuries to underage protesters "unacceptable and unjustifiable." They also criticized the government's mainly security-based response for sidelining the protests' "root causes," and repeated their call for a full review of French policing. In Lille earlier this month, a teenager lost a hand when a grenade exploded during clashes with police.

The movement began in mid-September over crumbling high-school buildings, overcrowded classrooms and teacher shortages. It has since merged with "yellow vest" protests over energy prices and the cost of living. Interior Ministry figures: last Saturday **410,000** people marched nationwide and **70,000** security forces were deployed, with 1,000 arrests. The week before there were 2,700 arrests. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez promised to be "extremely vigilant, extremely firm." On a visit to Marseille, Macron said a state of emergency was not off the table: "We are upholding the Republic and public order without a state of emergency."

Alex Lantier writes on the WSWS that the ruling class is "adopting measures from the National Rally's program before the party holds power." A state of emergency would allow warrantless searches, protest bans, house arrests and press censorship.

Key facts:
- The Interior Ministry has recorded about 3,700 protest-related arrests since October 17.
- Pending legislation: a police "presumption of legitimate use of weapons" bill passed its first reading on July 7. More than 720,000 people signed a petition against it, and Amnesty calls it a "license to kill." A Senate "rioter pays" bill would fine anyone present where property is damaged up to €10,000.
- Union Étudiante spokesperson Alice Cohen-Morzadec called the movement "a general outburst of resentment against" Macron.
- Education budgets are being cut as part of planned fiscal consolidation.

### 6. Federal judge blocks Trump bid to void 20,000 California ballots as subpoenas hit 13 states

Six days before the November 3 midterms, US District Judge David Carter refused a Justice Department request to temporarily stop California from counting mail ballots from about 20,000 people the administration says are noncitizens. "Never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election," Carter wrote, sought to "coerce a state and local government to abandon their own Constitutional elections procedures." He found that the request violated "longstanding constitutional principles mandating that states have control over their elections." The department, which filed suit on October 15, immediately appealed to the Ninth Circuit.

Reuters found that the California case is one part of a much larger operation. The administration has issued **more than 100 subpoenas in at least 13 states**, both Republican- and Democratic-led, demanding records on tens of thousands of voters. Most were sent in the past three months, and many had not been reported before. Legal experts say the records could be used to challenge close results. In a court filing, a government whistleblower warned that the department's "Unlawful Voter Initiative" is "knowingly sweeping known US citizens in." Democracy Docket found that one in four people on the Los Angeles County "noncitizen" list were born in the United States.

This comes alongside the third criminal case against James Comey in 13 months, a search of Rep. Ilhan Omar's home, and a suit over Alaska's Senate certification. Together these amount to an attempt to prepare grounds for contesting the midterm results. The Democrats' response has been confined to the courts.

Key facts:
- Carter is a 1998 Clinton appointee.
- An October 9 ruling had already found the administration's use of voter data unlawful.
- There is "scant evidence" of widespread noncitizen voting, and it is already illegal in federal elections.
- California says the federal list rests on "suspect data."

### Anthropic study finds experts follow AI's lead 86 percent of the time

In a study published Tuesday, Anthropic researchers Amaç Herdagdelen, Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin gave 50 professional analysts a policy task for a fictional farming cooperative. Without telling them, each analyst's AI assistant was set to favour one of two defensible options. The analysts chose the AI's preferred option **86 percent** of the time, where chance would give 50. Those who already had a view **changed it 60 percent** of the time when the AI disagreed. **77 percent** used the AI's arguments in their explanations, but only **15 percent** credited it. The authors warn that this deference is "hard to identify, even for the experts themselves." They say that AI's built-in "priors" could shape consequential policy, including on AI safety itself. The study used one task and deliberately biased assistants.

The findings matter because frontier models are rapidly being deployed in government, military and corporate decision-making, and the companies that control them decide what priors the models carry. Anthropic is preparing an IPO valued at up to $2 trillion.
