### 1. CIA director's secret Moscow mission signals NATO-Russia war danger

CIA Director John Ratcliffe traveled secretly to Moscow this week — the first known visit by a sitting CIA director to Russia since before the Ukraine war — to deliver a warning against any Russian attack on NATO territory. The trip became public on Tuesday after a US Air Force jet was spotted landing at a Moscow airport and a diplomatic motorcade was tracked through the city. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov confirmed Ratcliffe met Russian intelligence counterparts but not Vladimir Putin. Trump described the mission as "semi-routine."

The stated rationale is remarkable: US intelligence officials assess that Russia's *weakness* in Ukraine, not its strength, could prompt it to move against NATO. Ratcliffe also raised Iran, threatening additional sanctions if the Strait of Hormuz is not reopened, and pressed Moscow to cut ties with Tehran. Writing on the WSWS, Andre Damon argued the trip is evidence that "Washington itself believes open warfare between NATO and Russia is now a real and imminent danger," manufactured by an escalating campaign of NATO-guided strikes deep inside Russia.

Key facts:
- Ukraine mounted the largest air raid of the war on the night of August 15–16, sending roughly 600 drones over the Moscow region; at least six people were killed across Russia
- Strikes hit the Lukoil refinery at Kstovo — 500 miles inside Russia, producing up to 11 percent of the country's gasoline — and a Wildberries logistics warehouse in Tambov. The *Financial Times* reported in July that refinery strikes are "guided by American and French targeting intelligence"
- British PM Andy Burnham, in Kiev on August 24, released the secret specifications of the Storm Shadow missile so Ukraine and France can produce it domestically, and signed an agreement giving Britain first foreign access to the combat data training Ukraine's strike-guiding AI
- The European Commission approved €6.1 billion for further Ukrainian missile and air-defense purchases, part of a €90 billion war loan agreed in December
- Sweden, the Netherlands, Spain and Poland are pressing to seize roughly €200 billion in frozen Russian central bank assets held at Euroclear in Brussels. Belgian PM Bart De Wever warned at Davos in January: "You cannot simply confiscate money. That is an act of war"

### 2. Russia test-fires mobile ICBM as US missile stocks in Europe run dry

The ministry said the launch confirmed the system's "tactical, technical and flight characteristics" and that the missile held a "flawless trajectory." It did not identify the missile type. ### 3. Trump announces US majority control of 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil

Trump announced Friday that the United States has secured majority control over more than 65 billion barrels of proven Venezuelan oil reserves, calling it the "biggest oil deal in world history." The arrangement was negotiated by Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth together with private business partners and Venezuela's unelected "interim president" Delcy Rodríguez, installed after the US military campaign against the Maduro government. Rodríguez granted a newly created private company 100-year development rights over 17 fields.

The deal gives Washington a 55 percent effective share of output, including an ownership stake and the right to purchase oil at cost. Rodríguez called it a "historic milestone," projecting more than $100 billion of investment and $209 billion in tax revenue for Caracas. The White House emphasized the arrangement comes "at no cost to American taxpayers." In practical terms it more than doubles US proven reserves and is explicitly framed by the administration as a response to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz.

Key facts:
- Venezuela holds the world's largest proven crude reserves; the concession covers a century of extraction rights
- The announcement was made while Brent crude trades around $88, some 31 percent higher year-on-year, largely because of the Iran war
- Trump separately told reporters that Venezuela would hand over up to 50 million barrels to the US in an initial tranche

### 4. Nepal-Tibet glacier collapse kills more than 620 as barrier lake overflows

The death toll from Wednesday's catastrophic glacier and bedrock collapse on the Nepal-China border rose past 620 on Friday, with Nepali authorities reporting 616 bodies recovered and Chinese authorities confirming seven deaths and 554 missing on the Tibetan side. Roughly 2,400 to 2,500 people remain unaccounted for; about 1,550 have been rescued. Entire villages along the Bhote Koshi were buried when a surge of ice, rock, water and mud swept down Himalayan river valleys, destroying roads, bridges and hydropower installations.

Rescue operations in Rasuwa district were briefly suspended Friday after a barrier lake formed upstream at the confluence of the Chhochen Khola and Purepu Tsangpo rivers began to overflow. Chinese authorities put the lake volume at roughly 2–3 million cubic meters and warned that a breach probability remains high through August 30. Scientists analyzing satellite imagery concluded that bedrock beneath the glacier failed first, carrying part of the ice mass into the valley — a combined bedrock-and-glacier collapse rather than a simple glacial lake outburst.

Key facts:
- International aid pledged so far: $500,000 from the United States, €2 million from the European Union, A$5 million from Australia
- Kevin Reed noted on the WSWS that the Australian pledge amounts to roughly the cost of eight hours of that country's record $60 billion annual military budget
- Nepal and China halted joint rescue work for several hours on August 28 as the barrier lake spilled over

### Norway's King Harald V dies at 89

King Harald V of Norway, Europe's longest-serving and oldest reigning monarch, died Friday morning shortly after 6:35 a.m. following a rapid deterioration linked to a blood disorder and fluid accumulation. He reigned 35 years, having succeeded his father Olav V in 1991. As a child he fled the Nazi occupation of Norway in 1940, spending the war years in exile in the United States and Britain. His son becomes King Haakon VIII.

Harald's reign is being memorialized in the Norwegian and international press as one of "modernization." That framing sits uneasily beside the reality of a hereditary head of state presiding over a NATO member state that has become one of Europe's principal war-profiteering economies through gas exports since 2022, and over a monarchy whose reputation has been battered by criminal proceedings involving members of the royal household.

## Arts and culture

The death of **Yayoi Kusama** at 97, announced by the David Zwirner gallery on August 26 (she died August 14 in a Tokyo hospital of multiple organ failure), closes one of the most singular careers in postwar art. Kusama's seven decades of work — the obliterating polka dots, the infinity rooms, the accumulation sculptures — grew from a genuinely modernist impulse to represent the dissolution of the isolated individual into a larger material world, an impulse rooted in her wartime childhood in militarist Japan and her subsequent decades of voluntary residence in a psychiatric institution. Her New York years in the 1960s placed her at the center of an avant-garde that included anti-Vietnam War "happenings." That her late work was absorbed into the luxury-branding apparatus of the art market — Louis Vuitton collaborations, ticketed selfie rooms — is a comment on that market, not on the work.

Hungarian novelist **Péter Nádas** died on August 26 at 83. Nádas belonged to the generation of Central European writers formed by the Stalinist suppression of the 1956 Hungarian revolution and the long stagnation that followed. *A Book of Memories* (1986) and the vast *Parallel Stories* (2005) attempted, through an exhaustive attention to bodily and psychological experience, to render the interior life of people living under a regime that falsified everything. Nádas's achievement was to insist that the historical catastrophes of the twentieth century are lived in individual nervous systems and not merely in documents.

**Dolly Parton** died on August 26 at 80. Whatever the layers of commercial packaging accumulated over six decades, Parton emerged from genuine rural poverty in East Tennessee and wrote a body of songs — "Coat of Many Colors," "Jolene," "9 to 5" — that gave direct voice to the experience of working-class women in the American South. "9 to 5," written for a film about a workplace rebellion, remains one of the very few genuinely popular songs about the exploitation of labor.

## Pseudo-left press review

- [Jacobin / DSA](#jacobin--dsa) — trade-war coverage frames Trump's tariffs as an opening for a "democratic industrial policy," i.e., a left-nationalist protectionism
- [Left Voice](#left-voice) — publishes a guest post defending Bogdan Syrotiuk, while simultaneously deepening its adaptation to racial identity politics
- [Socialist Alternative](#socialist-alternative) — responds to DHS targeting of left groups and unions with appeals for a broad defensive coalition rather than an independent class strategy
- [Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)](#socialist-appeal--rcp-imt) — attacks the Burnham government over Ukraine while presenting the crisis as a matter of British national mismanagement

### Jacobin / DSA

**"Trump's Art of the Bad Deal Comes to Canada"** (Stuart Trew, August 28) argues that the collapse of trade negotiations with Trump creates an opening for Canada to abandon free trade in favor of "democratic industrial policy alternatives." The framework is explicitly national-reformist: the working class appears only as a constituency to be protected by a better-managed Canadian capitalist state. There is no suggestion that Canadian and American autoworkers — employed by the same corporations, facing the same tariff-driven job destruction — might organize jointly across the border. This is protectionism with progressive adjectives, and it prepares workers for the "shared sacrifice" that every trade war demands of them.

**"Faced With Record Fires, France Skimps on Fire Crews"** (Marlon Ettinger, August 29) documents the French state's reliance on volunteer firefighters amid record wildfires rather than funding professional crews. The reporting is useful; the political conclusion is a demand that the government spend more — with no reference to the rearmament budgets absorbing exactly those funds, and no challenge to the union federations that have contained every mobilization against Macron's austerity.

**"The Customer Is Always Watching"** (Alex N. Press, August 28) examines how Meta's Ray-Ban camera glasses extend employer surveillance by deputizing customers as monitors. The description of the technology's effect on retail and service workers is concrete and accurate. What is absent is any conclusion about who controls the technology and to what end; the piece stops at description because the alternative — expropriation of the tech monopolies under workers' control — falls outside Jacobin's horizon.

*Assessment:* The day's output holds to the established pattern — solid descriptive labor journalism married to political conclusions that terminate in demands on the existing state and, in the Canadian case, in outright economic nationalism.

### Left Voice

**"Free Bogdan Syrotiuk: Defend a Socialist Imprisoned for Opposing Imperialist War"** (August 28, published as a guest post) takes up the case of the imprisoned Ukrainian Trotskyist, tagged by the site under "Repression," "Trotskyism" and "War in Ukraine." This is a notable development: the Morenoite PTS/Left Voice tendency has for two years largely ignored a campaign built by the ICFI, and its decision to publish now — after the 15-year sentence and after the petition passed 6,700 signatures — registers pressure from its own readership. The editorial board should note both the opening and the method: the case is taken up as a generic civil-liberties question rather than as a defense of the specific programmatic content of Syrotiuk's stand, namely the characterization of the war as imperialist on both sides and the perspective of the Young Guard of Bolshevik-Leninists.

**"AOC and Mamdani Should Apologize for Their Anti-Black Comments"** (August 27) demands apologies from two Democratic Party politicians for racial offense. The article's frame concedes the essential point — that these figures are legitimate objects of moral appeal rather than political opponents representing a capitalist party — and reduces the critique of the DSA's electoral orientation to a question of racial etiquette.

**"The Fight Over Para Pay Shows the Limits of Mamdani and the UFT Leadership"** (August 26) is sharper, criticizing both the mayor and the UFT bureaucracy over paraprofessional wages, but the remedy remains pressure on the union apparatus to fight harder rather than the construction of organizations independent of it.

*Assessment:* A genuine step on Syrotiuk alongside a continued slide into racial identity politics as the organizing category of political analysis.

### Socialist Alternative

**"Trump's DHS Goes After Left Groups & Unions: We Won't Be Intimidated"** responds to the Department of Homeland Security's designation and investigation of left organizations and unions. The call is for a broad defensive coalition of "the left" and the unions — the same union apparatuses that have suppressed opposition to the administration and, in the case of the AFL-CIO leadership, sought accommodation with it. The article does not raise the independent mobilization of the working class or the political break with the Democratic Party that any serious defense of democratic rights requires.

**"Massachusetts Rent Control: Betrayals & Lessons For The Working Class"** attributes the defeat of rent control to the betrayal of individual Democratic legislators, treating the outcome as a failure of political will rather than as the predictable result of a party financed by real estate capital.

*Assessment:* Correct identification of the danger, followed by a proposed remedy — coalition with the very forces facilitating it — that disarms those who accept it.

### Socialist Appeal / RCP (IMT)

**"Buying time with Ukrainian blood: Burnham recommits to the slaughter"** (August 28) condemns the Burnham government's escalation in Ukraine, including the arms commitments made in Kiev. The criticism of Labour is real, but the analysis presents British policy as a failure of national strategy — "buying time" — rather than as the expression of British imperialism's structural dependence on the US alliance and its own predatory interests.

**"UK prison overcrowding: Burnham grapples with the crisis of the British state"** (August 28) describes the collapse of the prison system as a symptom of general state decay, with the political conclusion confined to the assertion that only "communism" can resolve it — a formula that substitutes maximalist rhetoric for any concrete transitional programme or organizational orientation to workers in struggle.

**"Burnham, Binface, or Bolshevism – How do we defeat Farage?"** (August 26) counterposes party-building to the establishment's containment strategies, but the recruitment appeal functions as a substitute for a worked-out programme against the far right.

*Assessment:* Militant phraseology combined with a national framework and an abstentionist attitude toward the concrete organizational tasks in the workplaces.

*Note:* Liberation News (PSL) and Socialist Worker (UK) could not be retrieved during this review period; both sites returned access errors.
