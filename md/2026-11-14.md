### 1. Trump, Xi and Putin announce first three-way summit, in Shenzhen next week

The White House said Friday that Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin will hold an unprecedented trilateral meeting. It will close Trump's visit to the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen late next week. Press spokeswoman Olivia Wales said Trump "will discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world." A senior US official told Reuters and AP that Iran will be discussed but Ukraine will not. Expectations are low: "We do not expect to get to any kind of agreement," the official said, calling it the start of a conversation. Beijing's state media has reported only the Trump–Xi bilateral on Thursday, their fourth meeting since 2025, and not the three-way session. The Guardian reports that China is already trying to push nuclear disarmament off the agenda.

The meeting has been widely compared to the 1945 Yalta conference. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov rejected the comparison but called the meeting "extremely significant." Moscow's own officials have been more open. Russia's lead Ukraine negotiator, Vladimir Medinsky, told Russian TV: "The task is to create a comprehensive system of comprehensive security in Europe that will become a new modern Yalta or Potsdam." Foreign ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova said the summit could mark "the start of a return from this slide into absolute chaos to stabilisation." Volodymyr Zelenskyy has warned the meeting could settle Ukraine's fate behind Kyiv's back.

Key facts:
- New START expired in February. Trump rejected Russia's offer of a one-year extension and demanded a "new, improved and modernized" deal that includes China (Al Jazeera).
- Last month the administration made a deal to import Russian diesel and suspended sanctions on the biggest buyers of Russian oil and gas, including China. Both moves alarmed European capitals (BBC).
- Itinerary: the ASEAN summit in Manila and a meeting with Ferdinand Marcos Jr on Tuesday, then Shenzhen from late Wednesday. Elon Musk will travel with Trump but will not attend the trilateral.
- The Guardian's diplomatic editor writes that Ukraine, eastern Europe, the Baltic states, Taiwan "and even Iran" have reason to fear "a complex geopolitical trade-off that Putin envisions, and Trump seems to endorse."

The prospect of a meeting among three nuclear powers to divide spheres of influence is a sign of how far the postwar order has decayed. Talk of "arms reduction" comes while all three are rearming and while US–China rivalry over chips (see the Nvidia story below) sharpens.

### 2. Iran strikes US radar in Bahrain as Trump declares "we don't need a deal"

Iran's Revolutionary Guards (IRGC) said they targeted and destroyed a US early-warning radar in Bahrain. They called it retaliation for a US airstrike on a communications tower at Sirik, overlooking the Strait of Hormuz. US Central Command denied the radar was destroyed and said it intercepted two Iranian drones headed for Bahrain. It was Iran's first direct attack on a US military installation in months. The IRGC called it "a serious warning to the aggressor enemy to end its aerial mischief and oil smuggling in the strait of Hormuz." Early Saturday the IRGC said two oil tankers, one a supertanker, "exploded and caught fire after striking a naval mine" while trying to cross by an "unauthorized route." Al Jazeera counts four such incidents in 24 hours. The US says it has cleared the main routes of mines.

The day before, Trump ended efforts to restart negotiations. "We don't need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed," he wrote on Truth Social. "We've got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we've got their Strait!" He told JD Vance, who had been talking "almost daily" with Qatari and Pakistani mediators, to turn his "complete and total attention" to "Massive Corruption and Crime taking place in Democrat run Cities and States." Trump said Gulf allies prefer "the status quo" and that an attack "seems to be quite unlikely" but the US is "locked and loaded." He also said he was disappointed in the American public, because higher diesel prices were a small price to pay.

Key facts:
- In effect the war is now a standing blockade. The US blockades Iranian ports and escorts tankers along the Omani coast, while Iran requires permission to transit Hormuz (NPR).
- Iran's Assembly of Experts became the first state institution to call for a review of defence doctrine, which could lead to religious sanction for pursuing a nuclear weapon (Guardian). Iranian MPs are also debating leaving the NPT (Euronews), and the IAEA is pressing for access to a key nuclear site (CBS).
- Vance has led Republicans in blaming the war for the party's midterm losses (Guardian).
- UAE official Anwar Gargash said the region must "prepare for a prolonged period of uncertainty, in which neither a decisive resolution nor a stable regional order can be taken for granted."
- Qatar and Türkiye signed a mutual defence pact pledging "a unified stance in confronting any external armed attack targeting either party" (France 24, Al Jazeera).
- A YouGov poll found 65 percent of Britons oppose the US resuming the war and only 14 percent support it. Among 2024 Labour voters, 79 percent are opposed.

### 4. Nvidia scandal reaches the cabinet as Bessent blames Lutnick

The fallout from Jensen Huang's resignation as Nvidia CEO on Thursday has become an open conflict inside the Trump cabinet. ### 5. Nationwide FAA ground stop, the longest since 9/11

At 3:54pm EST Friday the Federal Aviation Administration grounded every departing flight in the US, after radar and communications at air traffic control centres became unstable at 3:36pm. It reopened the airspace at 8:15pm, more than four hours later. It was the longest nationwide stoppage since September 11, 2001. More than 3,500 aircraft were in the air. FlightAware counted more than 11,000 US flights delayed or cancelled. Eurocontrol suspended all US-bound traffic from its 42 member states, and Sydney grounded US-bound departures.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy was blunt: "What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system. Our routers actually got overloaded with data… and because of that, we shut the system down." Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was "no indication as of yet that this was a nefarious attack." The White House briefing quickly became an appeal to Congress to "fully fund a full build of a brand new air traffic control system."

Key facts:
- More than 1.5 million passengers face a second day of disruption (Al Jazeera). Duffy said unwinding the backlog will take "a few days."
- Delays forced NBA, NHL and college teams to reschedule travel. The Mississippi–Oklahoma football game was moved to Sunday.
- The outage hit on the same day as an 18-hour global Microsoft outage (see Science), and on the night a tanker hit a riverboat in New Orleans (see United States).

The failure exposes how degraded basic infrastructure has become in the world's richest country. Trillions have gone to war and to stock buybacks while the air traffic control system runs on obsolete equipment.

### 6. France mobilises a fifth wave of student protests as Europe's social upheaval spreads

France 24 reports 50 to 60 marches expected across France today, in the fifth wave of student-led protests. The New York Times calls them "mass antigovernment demonstrations." They follow Wednesday's 24-hour general strike in Spain, the first since 2012, called over housing costs and falling real wages just weeks before the November 29 snap election. The Times places the protests in a wave across Belgium, Germany, Greece, Italy and Portugal. It frames them as a "generational clash" between pension spending and spending on schools. Housing protests in Portugal have now run for more than 30 days.

The WSWS reports that at least 4 million workers struck in Spain. Participation reached 90 percent in the car industry and 100 percent among Algeciras dockers. Police recorded 919 incidents and made at least 60 arrests. It notes the strike comes ahead of one-day national strikes in France (November 17), Italy (November 20) and Greece (November 25). As the WSWS put it, the strike showed "an outpouring of opposition not only to the PP and Vox but also to the record of militarism and austerity of the PSOE and its political and trade union allies."

Key facts:
- The Guardian reported "millions" joined Spain's strike, which the CCOO and UGT federations called after mass housing protests in early October.
- Marches: Madrid 120,000 by the state's count and 400,000 by the Tenants' Union's; Barcelona 55,000 officially and 400,000 by organisers (WSWS).
- The NYT's "generational" framing, which pits pensioners against students, deflects from where the money is actually going: military budgets are rising across the EU.
