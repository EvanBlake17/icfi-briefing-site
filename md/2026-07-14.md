### 1. US resumes bombing of Iran and reimposes Strait of Hormuz blockade

The United States has entered a renewed and far more dangerous phase of its war against Iran, three weeks after the June 17 ceasefire collapsed. After Iran fired on a commercial ship transiting the Strait of Hormuz over the weekend and declared the waterway "closed until further notice," US Central Command launched three consecutive nights of strikes. Iran said it struck two tankers; the US said it hit scores of targets — coastal surveillance systems, drone and missile sites — and Iran fired toward Jordan, Qatar, Kuwait and Oman.

Trump then reimposed a naval blockade of Iranian ports, set to resume at 4 p.m. ET Tuesday, and announced via Truth Social that Washington would provide protection for shipping through Hormuz "at the rate of 20% on all cargo shipped." He told a radio host, "We're going to hit them very hard tonight... and there's not a damn thing they can do about it." Crossings through the strait have fallen by more than half week-on-week.

As Andre Damon wrote in the WSWS Perspective, a blockade is "inherently an act of war whose logic points toward invasion," vindicating a June 15 warning that the 2026 ceasefire "will pave the way for the war that follows." No faction of the political establishment opposes the war's predatory aims — only its results.

Key facts:
- US forces struck **300+ targets** across Iran over three nights; two carrier strike groups, two amphibious groups and ~**50,000 troops** are massed in the region
- Trump threatens to seize **Kharg Island**, through which ~90% of Iran's oil exports flow, and to destroy power/desalination plants serving 90 million people
- The war, launched in February, has failed after **136 days** to topple Tehran, dismantle its nuclear program or seize Hormuz
- Democratic complicity: Schumer called the June deal the "art of surrender"; Pence (WSJ op-ed) called it "appeasement" and urged Trump to "let the armed forces finish the job"

### 2. June CPI lands as the year's most-watched inflation reading

The Bureau of Labor Statistics released June Consumer Price Index data at 8:30 a.m. ET Tuesday — widely billed as "the most important data point of the year" because it is the last major inflation print the Federal Reserve will weigh before its July 28–29 meeting. Consensus called for headline CPI to *fall* 0.1% month-on-month, dragging the annual rate down from 4.2% in May to roughly 3.9%, driven by a ~10% June drop in gasoline prices tied to the earlier ceasefire reopening of Hormuz.

Economists cautioned against reading the soft headline as a sign of easing: core CPI (ex-food and energy) was expected to hold near 2.9% year-on-year — still well above the Fed's 2% target — with the renewed Hormuz blockade now driving oil sharply higher and threatening to reverse the gas-price relief in July.

Key facts:
- Headline CPI consensus: **−0.1% m/m**, ~**3.9% y/y** (from 4.2%); core ~**+0.2% m/m**, **~2.9% y/y**
- Probability of a quarter-point Fed rate **hike** on July 29 jumped to **43.3%** from 34.2% Friday, largely on oil-driven inflation risk
- Data precedes June PCE (July 25); together the "most important inflation inputs" for the FOMC

### 3. ICE agent kills Colombian father in Maine, triggering protests

Joan Sebastian Guerrero, a 26-year-old husband and father from Colombia, was shot and killed by an ICE agent around 7:30 a.m. Monday, July 13, in Biddeford, Maine — the second fatal ICE-involved shooting in less than a week. According to DHS, agents were surveilling the last known address of a different man with a final removal order; when a vehicle attempted to flee an attempted stop, an officer "fearing for public safety" opened fire. Guerrero was **not** the target of the operation.

Maine lawmakers, citing DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin, confirmed that Guerrero was authorized to work in the US and had been issued a Social Security number. There was no body-camera footage; agents were not wearing cameras. About 200 people rallied in Biddeford at noon demanding ICE "stop killing people and leave Maine." As Jacob Crosse noted on the WSWS, this is the 11th deadly ICE shooting under Trump, amid over 63,000 immigrants currently detained, with officials offering shifting accounts.

### 5. Bangkok pub fire kills 27, most from smoke inhalation

A fire tore through a popular music bar in Bangkok's Chatuchak district on Saturday, July 12, killing 27 people and injuring 73, including 25 critically. The blaze started near the stage, cut power, and filled the windowless room with smoke; most of the dead were found in the toilets, having panicked in the dark. Firefighters brought it under control in about half an hour.

Bangkok's disaster administration said an initial assessment pointed to an electrical short circuit in a ceiling air conditioner, while police are investigating whether exits were obstructed or locked and whether flammable stage decorations accelerated the fire — the recurring hallmarks of deregulated, profit-driven nightlife venues across Southeast Asia.

### New Zealand slashes emergency housing as rough sleeping doubles

New Zealand's National-ACT-NZ First government has cut emergency-motel housing from **3,141 households in December 2023 to just 471 by May 2026**, hitting its 75% reduction target five years early — after revelations that frontline Ministry of Social Development staff were rated on how far they drove the numbers down. Auckland rough sleeping more than doubled between September 2024 and September 2025 (426 to 940), and the government keeps no record of where those denied housing go; for 16 straight months, inquiries to MSD exceeded formal applications by over 1,000 a month. Emergency housing has become a flashpoint in the 2026 election, with Labour vowing to scrap the targets. As Fred Borman wrote on the WSWS, neither major party will resolve the crisis.

### US measles nears worst level since 1991

The US has recorded **2,231 confirmed measles cases** in 2026 — nearly matching all of 2025 in half the time, and almost four times the 2020–2024 total combined. As Benjamin Mateus reported on the WSWS, the surge follows RFK Jr.'s purge of vaccine experts and cuts to the childhood immunization schedule. Kindergarten MMR coverage has slipped to **92.5%**, below the herd-immunity threshold, and the US is poised to lose the measles elimination status it has held since 2000 when PAHO's re-verification commission reviews the record, potentially by November 2026. At a congressional hearing RFK Jr. absurdly claimed the administration had "done better in preventing [measles] than any country in the world," even as it spreads vaccine misinformation and fails to coordinate with state health officials.

### Cyclospora outbreak spreads to 31+ states

A rapidly expanding cyclosporiasis outbreak has produced **843 confirmed domestically-acquired cases across 31 states** since May 1, with the CDC aware of more than 1,500 additional cases pending confirmation and 86 hospitalizations (no deaths). State counts run far higher: Michigan — which typically logs 40–50 cases a year — has surpassed 1,500 and, by Michigan's own count, more than 3,000 reports. Lettuce and salad greens are the leading suspects, but no grower or supplier has been identified. The outbreak is outpacing a CDC weakened by staffing and funding cuts.

### Climate and environment

- **The Lancet Public Health** published the 2026 Europe report of the *Lancet Countdown*, finding a marked rise in heat-related health harm: **99.6%** of 823 monitored European regions recorded more heat-attributable deaths in 2015–24 than in 1991–2000, warning of a "narrowing window for decisive health action."
- **Nature Climate Change** published work (July 7) documenting systematic "pluralistic ignorance" on climate — people underestimate how common pro-climate attitudes and behaviors actually are, a bias that suppresses collective action; a companion study found low carbon-dioxide-removal pathways distribute air-quality health benefits more equitably across the US.

### Sam Neill dies at 78

Sam Neill, the New Zealand actor whose career spanned four decades, died in Sydney on Monday, July 13, at 78. His family called the loss "sudden and unexpected," noting he had announced in April he was cancer-free after years of treatment for blood cancer. Neill achieved international recognition as paleontologist Dr. Alan Grant in Steven Spielberg's *Jurassic Park* (1993), a role he reprised across the franchise.

But the enduring measure of Neill's artistry lies in more demanding work. In Jane Campion's *The Piano* (1993) he played the repressed colonial husband Alisdair Stewart, and in Andrzej Żuławski's *Possession* (1981) he gave one of the era's most harrowing portrayals of psychological disintegration. He anchored the Australian miniseries *The Dismissal* and brought a wry humanism to television and independent films alike. A serious, self-effacing craftsman who spent much of his later life running a vineyard in Central Otago, Neill embodied a generation of actors formed by the flowering of Australian and New Zealand cinema in the 1970s–80s — a national film culture built with public investment now being eroded across the Anglophone world.

### Court rejects Trump bid to keep his name on DC arts institution

The DC Circuit Court of Appeals denied all of the Trump administration's arguments seeking to block the removal of the president's name from a Washington arts institution — a small but telling episode in the administration's broader effort to bend cultural institutions, from the Kennedy Center to the Smithsonian, to its political program. The politicization and defunding of public arts and humanities bodies proceeds in parallel with the university austerity documented elsewhere in today's briefing, part of a coordinated assault on independent cultural and intellectual life.

### Jacobin / DSA

- **"Everybody Should Welcome Nationalizing AI"** endorses Bernie Sanders' proposal for the state to take equity stakes in AI firms via a sovereign wealth fund and urges the left to push further. The orientation is statist and reformist: it routes the demand for social ownership through existing capitalist state mechanisms and a Democratic-aligned senator, rather than through the independent mobilization of the working class and the expropriation of the tech monopolies under workers' control.
- **"Samsung Workers' Fight Is Just Beginning"** offers labor-solidarity reporting on South Korean semiconductor subcontractor workers, framed as part of a global labor upsurge — sound as reportage but detached from any revolutionary program, presenting trade-union struggle as an end in itself.

Assessment: Jacobin continues to present the transformation of society as a matter of enlightened legislation channeled through the Democratic Party's left flank.

### Left Voice

- **"Promises, Promises: AOC, Israel, and the DSA"** sharply criticizes AOC and the DSA for remaining inside the Democratic Party and softening on Palestine, arguing reliance on "squad" politicians undermines independent working-class action.

- **"NATO in Ankara: Lessons from the Summit"** opposes NATO as an instrument of inter-imperialist rivalry.

Assessment: The PTS-aligned Left Voice offers the clearest verbal break with Democratic Party politics in this set, but its Morenoite "from below" spontaneism substitutes movementism for the fight to build a revolutionary party.

### Liberation News / PSL

- **"SCOTUS greenlights Trump's assault on TPS for Haitians and Syrians"** denounces the 6-3 ruling but frames it as Trump-administration repression rather than a bipartisan feature of the capitalist state.

Assessment: PSL's campism and national-democratic framing subordinate proletarian internationalism to alliances with "anti-imperialist" states and a moralized reading of American history.

### Socialist Alternative
