### 1. US strikes Iran after drone attack on cargo ship in Strait of Hormuz

The United States launched air strikes against Iranian military targets on June 26, just nine days after the two governments signed a Memorandum of Understanding extending a pause in hostilities for 60 days. The strikes followed a June 25 attack in which Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps launched at least four drones at vessels in the Strait of Hormuz, one of which struck the Singapore-flagged cargo ship *Ever Lovely* off the Omani coast, damaging its bridge windows. No casualties were reported in either the drone attack or the US response.

The episode shatters the fiction that the June 17 deal represented a genuine peace. As Kevin Reed wrote on the WSWS, Vice President JD Vance's statement that "violence will be met with violence" exposes the MOU as "political cover for continued military escalation rather than a genuine peace framework."

**Key facts:**
- Six US land-based aircraft struck four targets along the Strait and on Iran's Qeshm Island, hitting missile and drone storage facilities and coastal radar sites; CENTCOM called it "a powerful response to Iran's dangerous behavior."
- Trump called the drone attack "a foolish violation" of the ceasefire and, asked about consequences, told reporters: "You'll find out."
- The June 17 MOU required Iran to ensure "safe, toll-free" Strait passage "using best efforts"; Tehran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority responded that ships using "unapproved routes" would not receive safe-passage guarantees.
- On June 27, Trump said he would bomb Iran again "without question" if intelligence showed dangerous uranium enrichment.

### 2. Supreme Court guts asylum and TPS, expands gun carry in 6-3 rulings

The US Supreme Court's six-justice conservative majority issued a cluster of rulings on June 26 that dismantled core protections for immigrants and refugees and broadened the reach of the Second Amendment, all splitting along the same ideological lines. Two of the immigration rulings were authored by Justice Samuel Alito.

In *Mullin v. Doe*, the Court barred federal courts from reviewing the Secretary of Homeland Security's Temporary Protected Status determinations — clearing the way for the Trump administration to revoke TPS from immigrants who fled war and disaster. As John Burton noted on the WSWS, the ruling threatens roughly 1.3 million people, including some 350,000 Haitians, 50,000 Syrians, and hundreds of thousands from Venezuela, Somalia, and Ethiopia. In *Mullin v. Al Otro Lado*, the Court held that asylum seekers do not legally "arrive in the United States" until they physically cross the border, reinstating "metering" and nullifying the right to apply for asylum at ports of entry.

**Key facts:**
- Justice Sotomayor warned from the bench that "more people will die," invoking the *M.S. St. Louis*, the ship of Jewish refugees turned away from the US in 1939.
- In *Wolford v. Lopez* (6-3), the Court struck down Hawaii's requirement that concealed-carry permit holders obtain property-owner permission before carrying handguns in restaurants, parking lots and other publicly accessible private spaces. Alito wrote that "the Second Amendment has the same meaning in all parts of the United States."
- Justice Jackson, dissenting on the gun case: "Today's decision makes one thing clear: The Court's objective is protecting guns."
- Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissented in all three immigration matters.

### 6. Volkswagen plans 100,000 job cuts and four German plant closures

- VW's General Works Council and IG Metall pledged to resist the cuts "with all their might" — a posture the WSWS calls hollow given the union's record of negotiating concessions.

### John Bolton pleads guilty to retaining classified national defense information

Former national security adviser John Bolton, 77, pleaded guilty on June 26 in US District Court in Greenbelt, Maryland, to one count of illegally retaining national defense information under 18 U.S.C. § 793(e), which carries a maximum 10-year penalty. According to the court filing, Bolton shared over 1,000 pages of diary-style notes about his NSA duties with family members, and an Iranian-linked hacker subsequently accessed classified material from his personal email account.

The plea agreement caps the recommended sentence at five years and requires Bolton to pay a $2.25 million fine, submit to intelligence debriefings, perform up to 100 hours of community service, and forfeit his federal retirement pay. Sentencing is set for October 28. Bolton — a prominent Trump critic who backed his 2024 opponent — was charged under the second Trump administration, even as Trump's own classified-documents case was dismissed, underscoring the selective application of national-security law against political enemies within the ruling elite.

As of June 25, the CDC has recorded 2,134 confirmed measles cases across 41 states in 2026, with 93% tied to 30 active outbreaks and roughly 92% involving unvaccinated individuals or those of unknown vaccination status. Three deaths have been confirmed — two unvaccinated children in Texas and one adult in New Mexico — the first US measles deaths since 2015. The elimination status the US has held since 2000 is now under its gravest threat in 25 years, and the WHO will formally reassess in November 2026.

The crisis is inseparable from the collapse of public-health authority and the mainstreaming of anti-vaccine ideology under the current administration's health leadership.

**Roundup of other developments:**
- **H5N1 receptor mapping:** University of Pittsburgh researchers, writing in *Science Advances*, found that H5N1 binds to N-linked sialic acid receptors densely expressed in bovine mammary cells but "virtually absent" in cow airway tissue — explaining the necrotizing mastitis seen in dairy herds since 2024 and why infections went undiagnosed for weeks. The framework can pre-screen other species for susceptibility.
- **Pancreatic cancer breakthrough:** The Phase 3 RASolute 302 trial, published in *NEJM* and presented at ASCO, showed daraxonrasib (a KRAS-ON inhibitor) extended median overall survival to 13.2 months versus 6.7 on chemotherapy in previously treated metastatic pancreatic cancer (HR 0.40, p<0.0001) — a 60% reduction in death risk. Over 90% of pancreatic cancers carry KRAS mutations.
- **Hepatitis B:** Pooled Phase 3 data on bepirovirsen (an antisense oligonucleotide) in *NEJM* showed a 19% functional cure rate overall and 26% in patients with lower baseline surface antigen, versus 0% on placebo — a milestone toward an elusive cure for a virus affecting 254 million people.
- **COVID-19:** As of June 16, transmission is growing or likely growing in 2 US states, declining in 36, and flat in 11; the WHO reports 7,114,321 confirmed global deaths, with true excess-death estimates of 19–36 million.
- **AI policy:** The White House's June 2 executive order, "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security," establishes a voluntary framework for developers to give the federal government pre-release access to frontier models for security assessment — a pivot from its prior deregulatory orders. Colorado's state AI Act takes effect June 30.

## Arts and culture

The most politically charged cultural story of the day is the Trump administration's "Great American State Fair" on the National Mall, dissected by David Walsh on the WSWS as a "grotesque" and "sparsely attended" spectacle from which most scheduled performers withdrew, leaving Trump to headline himself alongside military flyovers. Walsh situates the event within a structural assault on the arts: the administration proposed eliminating the National Endowment for the Arts, the National Endowment for the Humanities and the Institute of Museum and Library Services, while the Department of Education was cut by $8 billion below the prior year. Walsh invokes Eisenhower's 1953 warning that military expenditure constitutes "a theft from those who hunger and are not fed," and argues the climate of threat is inducing de facto self-censorship among federally funded arts organizations.

**Roundup of other developments:**
- **Wolfgang Tillmans wins the Roswitha Haftmann Prize.** The German photographer received Europe's most financially significant arts award — 150,000 Swiss francs (about $190,000), with no strings attached — for a four-decade career and his record of social advocacy. Tillmans's work, from intimate portraiture to political campaigning against Brexit and the far right, represents a strand of contemporary art that has tried to keep faith with social engagement rather than retreat into the market.

Davis shaped the commercial careers of artists from Janis Joplin, Santana and Bruce Springsteen to Whitney Houston and Aerosmith — a figure whose life spanned the transformation of popular music into a corporate industry, with all the creative possibilities and commercial constrictions that entailed.
- **Death of James Burrows (85).** The television director, who shaped the modern American sitcom across more than 1,000 episodes — from *The Mary Tyler Moore Show* to *Cheers* and *Will & Grace* — died June 19.

Taken together, the day's cultural news poses sharply the contradiction the WSWS repeatedly identifies: a society of immense artistic and creative capacity, embodied in the careers of figures like Davis and Burrows and in artists like Tillmans, whose public cultural institutions are being deliberately starved and instrumentalized for nationalist spectacle. The "Great American State Fair" is not merely a failed event but a symptom of a ruling class that has nothing to offer culturally except militarism and the cult of the leader.
