### 1. Israel's election ends without a majority as Palestinian citizens turn out in record numbers

Tuesday's Knesset election left neither bloc with a governing majority. With just over 55% of ballots counted (Times of Israel figures cited by Al Jazeera), Netanyahu's Likud-led coalition had 56 of 120 seats, Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc 49, and the unaligned Arab-led parties 15. Exit polls differed. Channel 12 gave Netanyahu 52 and Eisenkot's Yashar alliance 49. Channel 13 had them tied at 51. Likud, at about 27 seats, remains the largest single party. Arab-led lists were projected at about 19 seats, nearly double their previous 10. Turnout was 77.7% according to the Central Elections Committee, the highest since 1999 and 7.1 points above 2022.

The deadlock leaves the Palestinian citizens of Israel holding a block of seats that neither Zionist camp will accept as partners. Netanyahu argues that Eisenkot cannot govern without them. Eisenkot has ruled out any arrangement with Arab parties, citing a "lack of shared values." On election day Netanyahu posted on X that Arab voters were "Muslim Brotherhood supporters" who were "raiding the polling stations and stealing the elections." He shared a photo of a long queue outside a polling station and called the vote "a matter of life and death for us." The legal center Adalah reported slowed or stopped queues in Arab-majority towns and missing Arabic ballot slips, and filed complaints with the election committees. WTI crude moved back above $100 early Wednesday, and analysts cited the deadlock as one factor.

Key facts:
- **Netanyahu:** "Likud party is the largest party – nobody can compete with us." He has called for a "strong, right-wing" government.
- **Eisenkot:** "The old era is over, and Israel is starting a new era." He said the projections showed "complete lack of confidence in Netanyahu and the October 7 government."
- **Yair Lapid:** "The Netanyahu bloc has suffered a crushing defeat… a total collapse." Foreign Minister Gideon Saar called opposition claims "premature."
- **Arab parties:** Mansour Abbas (United Arab List) called Netanyahu's posts a "scare tactic to mobilise right-wing voters." Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen said Netanyahu knows "we can put an end to his bloody reign." Faten Gattas (Joint List), per Reuters: "We refuse the occupation and racism, and we will remain here." Balad's Sami Abu Shehadeh: "There are racists for whom these pictures are frightening."
- **Timeline:** The final tally is due Friday and official results on Nov. 4. President Herzog then nominates a prime minister-designate, who has up to 42 days to form a coalition. Analysts expect smaller parties to decide the outcome.

The election offers the working class no way out of the war. Netanyahu and Eisenkot differ over who should run it, not over whether to wage it.

### 2. Ebola passes 10,000 cases as unpaid health workers strike in Congo

The Democratic Republic of Congo's Ministry of Public Health reports **10,060 confirmed cases and 4,887 deaths** in the Bundibugyo-strain outbreak declared on May 15. It is the DRC's deadliest and fastest-growing Ebola outbreak and the second-largest on record, after West Africa in 2014–16. CIDRAP calls it the fastest-growing outbreak ever recorded. It now covers 66 health zones in eight provinces. There is no approved vaccine or treatment for the Bundibugyo strain. The US CDC's slightly lagging count is 9,964 cases and 4,841 deaths, a case-fatality rate of about 48%.

Containment is failing. About 80% of new cases fall outside known transmission chains. Africa CDC says at least 95% of new cases must come from known contacts for the outbreak to be controlled. Health workers have struck over wages unpaid since August, and treatment centres are overwhelmed. Maternal deaths have doubled in some provinces as the outbreak crowds out other care. A nurse was killed and another health worker attacked outside an Ebola clinic in Tshopo province. WHO Director-General Tedros: "Everyone working to protect communities from this deadly disease must be able to work in dignity and safety."

Key facts:
- **Spread:** Nigeria is the fourth African country affected. Its index patient, a 42-year-old Ugandan woman married to a Swiss national, has been airlifted to Switzerland with her asymptomatic husband, and 52 contacts are being monitored. Africa CDC says both the Kenyan and Nigerian index patients passed through Uganda's Entebbe International Airport on Oct. 3. It is investigating the airport as a possible site of transmission, since the Nigerian patient never travelled to the DRC.
- **Kenya:** 9 confirmed cases and 2 deaths; 20 contacts released after 21 days and 98 still in quarantine.
- **Vaccine trial:** A three-arm trial began in Bunia on Friday. It tests Merck's Ervebo, which was developed for the Zaire strain, against experimental vaccines from Moderna, Oxford and the Serum Institute of India. Regeneron has extended its BARDA contract for the antibody treatment Inmazeb, which is also proven only against Zaire.

Five months in, the front-line workers fighting the outbreak have gone unpaid since August, and none of the available countermeasures were designed for this strain.

Key facts:

### 5. France debates a "rioter pays" law as Europe's protest wave meets emergency powers

France's National Assembly is debating a "rioter pays" bill this week. Rights groups and trade unionists say it guts the right to protest. Anyone who "knowingly joined a group" would be jointly liable for damage caused by any member, up to **€10,000**. The state could recover the money by seizing welfare benefits from those convicted, or from parents' benefits when the defendant is a minor. The Human Rights League (LDH) and trade unionists signed a column in *Le Monde* demanding its withdrawal. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez defends it as "joint responsibility – not a collective one." The National Rally supports the principle. RN senator Edwige Diaz objected only to the €10,000 cap. Nuñez offered his resignation last week after security forces were overwhelmed, and Macron refused it. Speaking in Marseille on Oct. 26, Macron said order was being upheld "without a state of emergency" but that one was not "taboo" if things deteriorated.

Student representatives have vowed to keep protesting despite the government's education package. The package caps some class sizes, offers €4 billion in school renovation loans and €1 billion for campuses, indexes grants to inflation, and adds 15,000 university places. Nuñez said 1,435 people were arrested at Saturday's demonstrations. A larger national day of action is called for Nov. 5. The beneficiary in the polls is the far right. One survey puts Le Pen at 32–33.5% against Mélenchon at 17%, with a runoff expected in May 2027.

Key facts:
- **Arrests:** about 3,700 since Oct. 17, citing Interior Ministry figures (WSWS).
- **Police bill:** A "presumption of legitimate use of weapons" bill passed its first reading on July 7 and goes to the Senate this autumn. Amnesty International calls it a "license to kill."
- **Italy:** President Mattarella has signed a decree that allows arrests on the basis of video footage. It extends preventive custody of up to 12 hours to anyone aged 16 or over, creates a "violent participation" offence carrying up to five years and €20,000 in fines, and makes parents liable for their children. On Saturday, tens of thousands marched in Milan, Rome, Turin, Bologna, Florence, Naples, Genoa and smaller cities, called out by the online "Movimento 17 Ottobre." Milan received 5,000 police, 500 paratroopers, armoured vehicles and drones (WSWS).
- **Spread:** The WSWS reports the movement extending to Spain, Belgium, Serbia, Albania and Greece.

Alexandre Lantier wrote on the WSWS that the 2015 emergency, imposed under Hollande, allowed warrantless searches, protest bans and house arrests, and that its curfew and protest-ban powers were moved into ordinary law in 2017. On that basis he warns that Macron is preparing repression while the RN leads the polls.

### Egyptian court orders Mada Masr shut

The New Cairo Misdemeanour Court on Tuesday ordered the closure of Mada Masr, one of Egypt's last prominent independent news outlets, and the confiscation of its equipment. Editor-in-chief Lina Attalah was fined 3 million Egyptian pounds (about $53,400) for running an unlicensed website under Article 105 of the 2018 media law. She was taken into custody and released hours later, once the outlet paid. On her release she learned she had been sentenced in absentia in 2024 to one year in prison for "false news." A retrial is set for Nov. 11.

- **Licence:** Mada Masr applied for a licence in 2018 and 2020, learned of a rejection only in 2022, and has had a new application pending since June 2023. The site has been blocked inside Egypt since 2017, and this is the first of six cases against it in six years to reach trial.
- **Trial:** The court refused to let the defence review the case file, submit evidence or call witnesses. With eight of Attalah's lawyers present, the judge appointed a defence lawyer of the court's own choosing over her objection.
- **Reactions:** Amnesty's Yasmine Ahmed called it "a crushing blow to press freedom in Egypt." The Journalists' Syndicate will appeal. The same day, prosecutors renewed the detention of six journalists from the fact-checking site Matsadaash.

### CDC: drug-resistant infections now kill 50,800 Americans a year

A new CDC antimicrobial-resistance threats report, based on 2023 data, estimates **9.7 million drug-resistant infections and 50,800 deaths** a year in the US. The 2019 report estimated 2.8 million infections and 35,000 deaths, though the CDC says changed methods make direct comparison unreliable. Hospital-associated resistant infections alone caused more than 42,000 deaths and cost more than $12 billion.

- ***Candida auris* (urgent):** 4,523 infections in 2023, a tenfold increase since 2019. Resistant infections are fatal in 29–62% of cases.
- **Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (urgent):** 14,878 cases, 67% of them acquired in the community.
- **Gonorrhoea:** added to the urgent list, with few effective antibiotics left.
- **Meningococcus:** ciprofloxacin-resistant cases more than doubled from 2019 to 2023. The first macrolide-resistant pertussis cases since 2018 were found in 2024 and 2025.

The report follows Merck's withdrawal of an antibiotic for multidrug-resistant infections from the US market. Drug makers earn little from antibiotics, so the pipeline keeps shrinking even as resistance spreads.

## Arts and culture

### Kennedy's "Arabs don't produce literature" and the record he erased

The most significant cultural statement of the past 24 hours came from a cabinet secretary. Campaigning in Grand Rapids, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. declared that Arabs "don't produce science" and "don't produce literature." Today's WSWS Perspective answers with the record. Arabic and Islamic scholarship transmitted and extended the classical heritage, and gave European languages "algebra" and "algorithm." The modern record includes Naguib Mahfouz's Cairo trilogy and the poetry of Mahmoud Darwish and Adonis. Jokha Alharthi's *Celestial Bodies* won the International Booker in 2019, and Zaha Hadid won the Pritzker. The Perspective also lists what US and Israeli wars have done to that culture: the burning of Iraq's National Library, the killing of hundreds of Iraqi academics, and the destruction of every university in Gaza. The slur is aimed at Arab-American voters in Michigan, but it also serves a cultural policy that treats an entire civilization as having nothing to contribute.

On the same day, Mahmoud Khalil presented *No Land to Stand On: Notes from Detention* at Columbia (see United States). The memoir belongs to a growing body of prison writing that documents state repression of opposition to the Gaza genocide.

### Pawlikowski's *Fatherland*: Thomas Mann in the ruins of 1949

Paweł Pawlikowski's *Fatherland* stars Hanns Zischler as Thomas Mann and Sandra Hüller as his daughter Erika. It follows Mann's 1949 return to a Germany split in two, the trip during which Klaus Mann, who had asked Erika for protection, takes his own life. A *Jacobin* review by Corey Robin reads it as a tragedy of liberal humanism: "noble" but powerless before barbarism, with Mann a "preening moralist" whose refusal to collaborate cost him nothing. That reading leaves out the real history. Mann's postwar position was shaped by the onset of the Cold War, which within three years drove him out of the United States. A film that sets the Manns against the background of a partitioned, rearming Europe deserves attention now, as Berlin reintroduces conscription.
