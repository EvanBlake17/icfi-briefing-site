1. Trump, Xi and Putin announce first three-way summit, in Shenzhen next week

The White House said Friday that Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin will hold an unprecedented trilateral meeting. It will close Trump’s visit to the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen late next week. Press spokeswoman Olivia Wales said Trump “will discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world.” A senior US official told Reuters and AP that Iran will be discussed but Ukraine will not. Expectations are low: “We do not expect to get to any kind of agreement,” the official said, calling it the start of a conversation. Beijing’s state media has reported only the Trump–Xi bilateral on Thursday, their fourth meeting since 2025, and not the three-way session. The Guardian reports that China is already trying to push nuclear disarmament off the agenda.

The meeting has been widely compared to the 1945 Yalta conference. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov rejected the comparison but called the meeting “extremely significant.” Moscow’s own officials have been more open. Russia’s lead Ukraine negotiator, Vladimir Medinsky, told Russian TV: “The task is to create a comprehensive system of comprehensive security in Europe that will become a new modern Yalta or Potsdam.” Foreign ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova said the summit could mark “the start of a return from this slide into absolute chaos to stabilisation.” Volodymyr Zelenskyy has warned the meeting could settle Ukraine’s fate behind Kyiv’s back.

Key facts: - New START expired in February. Trump rejected Russia’s offer of a one-year extension and demanded a “new, improved and modernized” deal that includes China (Al Jazeera). - Last month the administration made a deal to import Russian diesel and suspended sanctions on the biggest buyers of Russian oil and gas, including China. Both moves alarmed European capitals (BBC). - Itinerary: the ASEAN summit in Manila and a meeting with Ferdinand Marcos Jr on Tuesday, then Shenzhen from late Wednesday. Elon Musk will travel with Trump but will not attend the trilateral. - The Guardian’s diplomatic editor writes that Ukraine, eastern Europe, the Baltic states, Taiwan “and even Iran” have reason to fear “a complex geopolitical trade-off that Putin envisions, and Trump seems to endorse.”

The prospect of a meeting among three nuclear powers to divide spheres of influence is a sign of how far the postwar order has decayed. Talk of “arms reduction” comes while all three are rearming and while US–China rivalry over chips (see the Nvidia story below) sharpens.

2. Iran strikes US radar in Bahrain as Trump declares “we don’t need a deal”

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards (IRGC) said they targeted and destroyed a US early-warning radar in Bahrain. They called it retaliation for a US airstrike on a communications tower at Sirik, overlooking the Strait of Hormuz. US Central Command denied the radar was destroyed and said it intercepted two Iranian drones headed for Bahrain. It was Iran’s first direct attack on a US military installation in months. The IRGC called it “a serious warning to the aggressor enemy to end its aerial mischief and oil smuggling in the strait of Hormuz.” Early Saturday the IRGC said two oil tankers, one a supertanker, “exploded and caught fire after striking a naval mine” while trying to cross by an “unauthorized route.” Al Jazeera counts four such incidents in 24 hours. The US says it has cleared the main routes of mines.

The day before, Trump ended efforts to restart negotiations. “We don’t need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed,” he wrote on Truth Social. “We’ve got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we’ve got their Strait!” He told JD Vance, who had been talking “almost daily” with Qatari and Pakistani mediators, to turn his “complete and total attention” to “Massive Corruption and Crime taking place in Democrat run Cities and States.” Trump said Gulf allies prefer “the status quo” and that an attack “seems to be quite unlikely” but the US is “locked and loaded.” He also said he was disappointed in the American public, because higher diesel prices were a small price to pay.

Key facts: - In effect the war is now a standing blockade. The US blockades Iranian ports and escorts tankers along the Omani coast, while Iran requires permission to transit Hormuz (NPR). - Iran’s Assembly of Experts became the first state institution to call for a review of defence doctrine, which could lead to religious sanction for pursuing a nuclear weapon (Guardian). Iranian MPs are also debating leaving the NPT (Euronews), and the IAEA is pressing for access to a key nuclear site (CBS). - Vance has led Republicans in blaming the war for the party’s midterm losses (Guardian). - UAE official Anwar Gargash said the region must “prepare for a prolonged period of uncertainty, in which neither a decisive resolution nor a stable regional order can be taken for granted.” - Qatar and Türkiye signed a mutual defence pact pledging “a unified stance in confronting any external armed attack targeting either party” (France 24, Al Jazeera). - A YouGov poll found 65 percent of Britons oppose the US resuming the war and only 14 percent support it. Among 2024 Labour voters, 79 percent are opposed.

3. Russia demands a new European security order as EU names its negotiating team

After talks in Moscow with US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, the Kremlin set out its aim: “a new, long-term architecture of indivisible security and peace.” The BBC’s Steve Rosenberg says Moscow has taken its December 2021 “draft treaties” with the US and NATO “out of the geopolitical filing cabinet.” Those drafts would bar Ukraine and Georgia from NATO forever and roll back the alliance’s military infrastructure in central and eastern Europe. Putin aide Yuri Ushakov said Moscow will “add certain new considerations” because of Western efforts “to inflict a strategic defeat on Russia.” Putin also said Russia would target any foreign troops sent to Ukraine.

After the latest round of US–Russia–Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi, Kushner said both sides had agreed to bring the Europeans into the next round. Moscow quickly contradicted him. At Friday’s EU summit in Dublin, European Council President António Costa announced a team made up of the EU institutions and the “E5”: Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Poland. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas warned against “walking into the traps that Russia is setting.” German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said Europe’s security architecture “already exists” and does not need to be discussed. Medinsky said it was “unclear what there is to discuss” with Europe.

Key facts: - A Russian drone hit the National Institute of Traumatology and Orthopedics in central Kyiv at 3am, near the Danish and Romanian embassies, injuring three. Russian strikes on Ukraine killed at least three and injured more than 20 on Friday (Euronews). - Russia said it shot down nearly 1,200 Ukrainian drones overnight, one of Kyiv’s largest swarms. It reported at least four killed in Volgograd region. - Zelenskyy said SpaceX’s Starshield, the Pentagon-overseen military version of Starlink, has been throttled again. Interceptors that depend on it cannot reach the speeds needed to down Russian drones. The White House has not explained the slowdown. - A car bomb in Moscow severely injured Sergei Zavorotniy, 72, a former aide to exiled Ukrainian ex-premier Mykola Azarov whom a US Senate report identified as an SVR officer. It came a day after a car bomb in St Petersburg killed a senior Russian military official. Both attacks are attributed to Kyiv.

4. Nvidia scandal reaches the cabinet as Bessent blames Lutnick

The fallout from Jensen Huang’s resignation as Nvidia CEO on Thursday has become an open conflict inside the Trump cabinet. Huang quit after reports of two affairs, one with Alice Huang, a former executive at the cloud firm Megaspeed whom US intelligence reportedly believes was recruited by Beijing. On CNBC, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said several agencies “were in favour of putting Megaspeed on the entity list for months.” He called reports of chips leaking to China “very frustrating” and said “the real story” is whether Nvidia knowingly sold to “obviously pass-through shell companies.” The Financial Times reported that Howard Lutnick’s Commerce Department delayed listing Megaspeed. The Guardian reports that Lutnick now faces the sack.

Senate Banking Committee chair Tim Scott has asked Huang to testify voluntarily on December 3 and has summoned Lutnick for December. A Guardian analysis of Trump’s financial disclosures, drawing on the group DeKleptocracy, found that the president bought $1–5 million of Nvidia stock in the days before his administration rescinded the Biden-era AI chip “diffusion rule” in May 2025. He made at least three more purchases of $250,001–$500,000 each right after approving H200 sales to China in December 2025.

Key facts: - Nvidia has lost $550 billion in market value in five days and is still valued at nearly $4 trillion (BBC). CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO. - China warned Washington against new chip export controls. The row threatens to overshadow next week’s Trump–Xi meeting (CBS, Euronews). - A source told CBS that Singapore held Alice Huang’s passport for eight months and received “scant help” from the US. - The WSJ reports that a businessman who introduced Huang to Alice Huang was in fact her husband, Kang Wei.

The episode shows the fusion of state and tech oligarchy under Trump. US policy on the world’s most strategic industry has turned on the private conduct of one billionaire and the president’s personal stock trades.

5. Nationwide FAA ground stop, the longest since 9/11

At 3:54pm EST Friday the Federal Aviation Administration grounded every departing flight in the US, after radar and communications at air traffic control centres became unstable at 3:36pm. It reopened the airspace at 8:15pm, more than four hours later. It was the longest nationwide stoppage since September 11, 2001. More than 3,500 aircraft were in the air. FlightAware counted more than 11,000 US flights delayed or cancelled. Eurocontrol suspended all US-bound traffic from its 42 member states, and Sydney grounded US-bound departures.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy was blunt: “What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system. Our routers actually got overloaded with data… and because of that, we shut the system down.” Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said there was “no indication as of yet that this was a nefarious attack.” The White House briefing quickly became an appeal to Congress to “fully fund a full build of a brand new air traffic control system.”

Key facts: - More than 1.5 million passengers face a second day of disruption (Al Jazeera). Duffy said unwinding the backlog will take “a few days.” - Delays forced NBA, NHL and college teams to reschedule travel. The Mississippi–Oklahoma football game was moved to Sunday. - The outage hit on the same day as an 18-hour global Microsoft outage (see Science), and on the night a tanker hit a riverboat in New Orleans (see United States).

The failure exposes how degraded basic infrastructure has become in the world’s richest country. Trillions have gone to war and to stock buybacks while the air traffic control system runs on obsolete equipment.

6. France mobilises a fifth wave of student protests as Europe’s social upheaval spreads

France 24 reports 50 to 60 marches expected across France today, in the fifth wave of student-led protests. The New York Times calls them “mass antigovernment demonstrations.” They follow Wednesday’s 24-hour general strike in Spain, the first since 2012, called over housing costs and falling real wages just weeks before the November 29 snap election. The Times places the protests in a wave across Belgium, Germany, Greece, Italy and Portugal. It frames them as a “generational clash” between pension spending and spending on schools. Housing protests in Portugal have now run for more than 30 days.

The WSWS reports that at least 4 million workers struck in Spain. Participation reached 90 percent in the car industry and 100 percent among Algeciras dockers. Police recorded 919 incidents and made at least 60 arrests. It notes the strike comes ahead of one-day national strikes in France (November 17), Italy (November 20) and Greece (November 25). As the WSWS put it, the strike showed “an outpouring of opposition not only to the PP and Vox but also to the record of militarism and austerity of the PSOE and its political and trade union allies.”

Key facts: - The Guardian reported “millions” joined Spain’s strike, which the CCOO and UGT federations called after mass housing protests in early October. - Marches: Madrid 120,000 by the state’s count and 400,000 by the Tenants’ Union’s; Barcelona 55,000 officially and 400,000 by organisers (WSWS). - The NYT’s “generational” framing, which pits pensioners against students, deflects from where the money is actually going: military budgets are rising across the EU.

Middle East

Israeli strike guts surgery wing of Gaza’s Nasser hospital

An Israeli airstrike on Nasser hospital in Khan Younis killed six people, including a disabled man, and wounded 28, according to hospital director Dr Ahmed al-Fara. The Israeli military said it struck a Hamas commander and gave no evidence. Radiology, laboratories, surgical operations and inpatient surgical wards were all suspended. Among the dead was general surgeon Rizk Abu Shaira, 52, who appeared in the documentary American Doctor. Separately, the UN said Israel could have prevented much of the death and destruction from Gaza’s winter storm had it allowed in enough aid.

Eisenkot accuses Netanyahu of a “racist” campaign as Arab parties emerge as kingmakers

Gadi Eisenkot, the prime ministerial hopeful trying to form a government after last month’s election, accused caretaker premier Benjamin Netanyahu of fuelling “a racist and aggressive campaign of delegitimisation” against Arab citizens. Houthi siege of Taiz traps nearly two million

The Houthis cut the last major supply route into government-held Taiz in early October, after seizing territory along the Red Sea coast in September. The Yemeni government says nearly two million people have been without regular food, clean water, medicine and fuel for more than a month. Health officials warn that falling numbers of recorded infections may hide a worsening disease crisis. Human Rights Watch has said the siege may amount to a war crime. UN deputy spokesman Farhan Haq said at least 30 people were killed in airstrikes on a market, one of the deadliest attacks since the Houthi–Saudi-backed government fighting resumed. Civilians across Yemen are increasingly being hit “in their homes, markets and other places once considered safe.”

Europe

Bundestag gives German spy agencies offensive hacking powers

The Bundestag voted 308–190, with 71 abstentions, to let the BND foreign intelligence service and the BfV domestic intelligence service move from gathering intelligence to actively “disrupting” threats. That includes hacking and deleting data on foreign servers. The CDU/CSU–SPD coalition voted for it; the AfD and Die Linke voted against. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt called it the “reform of the century.” Analysts tie it directly to the Zeitenwende, the militarist turn since 2022. The BfV, which lists socialist organisations as “extremist,” will now have powers to intervene and not only to watch. Separately, a Bundeswehr study found that more than 60 percent of female soldiers have experienced harassment or abuse. It comes as the army tries to expand rapidly.

Hungary finds millions in cash buried in forests in Orbán-era graft probe

Hungary’s new anti-corruption office (NVVH) found 5.07 billion forints, €2.66 million and $1.26 million. The cash was “buried in forests, hidden in agricultural and warehouse buildings” and in safes, and belonged to Miklós Seszták, Orbán’s development minister from 2014 to 2018. He is accused of taking bribes on overpriced secondhand buses for the state transport company. Finance Minister András Kármán declared “the end of the Fidesz-KDNP mafia.” Two other ex-ministers and Orbán’s brother are in custody. Prime Minister Péter Magyar won in April promising to clean up, but he governs on the same pro-business basis.

UK probation officers vote for industrial action

Napo members in England and Wales backed overtime bans and other action short of a strike, likely from November 23, with full strikes planned later. The 5 percent offer compares with the 12 percent the union sought. Probation officers are already stretched by the Burnham government’s early-release scheme, which will free about 2,500 offenders by year’s end to ease prison overcrowding. Separately, the government’s Milburn welfare review will propose moving some under-25s off sickness benefits.

Asia-Pacific

Indonesian vice president Gibran resigns over diploma ruling

Gibran Rakabuming Raka, 39, eldest son of former president Joko Widodo, resigned after the Constitutional Court found “no convincing evidence” that he ever finished high school, which is a legal requirement for the office. He stepped down as about 120 MPs moved to open an impeachment inquiry. President Prabowo Subianto, who “regretfully” accepted, has 14 days to nominate two replacements. The upper house will choose within 60 days. The resignation formalises the breakdown of the Prabowo–Jokowi pact behind the 2024 ticket, and it follows last week’s arrest of three police generals over illegal wiretapping.

Africa

Drones become the leading killer of civilians in Sudan

Drones are now by far the leading cause of civilian deaths in Sudan’s civil war. On October 18, a “double-tap” strike on a transport hub in Al-Dibab, West Kordofan, killed at least 139 people, with a second drone hitting rescuers ten minutes after the first. A strike on a Nyala market eight days later killed 103, according to Emergency Lawyers. The UN counted at least 306 drone deaths in the first three weeks of October alone. Estimates of the war’s total death toll range from 150,000 to more than 400,000. UN refugee chief Barham Salih called the war “shameful.” The regional and imperialist powers arming the Sudanese Armed Forces and the Rapid Support Forces go unnamed in most coverage.

International law and the Americas

ICC drops investigation of US troops in Afghanistan

ICC judges halted the prosecutor’s inquiry into alleged crimes by US forces and the CIA in Afghanistan, and into the ISIS-K affiliate. They told the office to focus on the Taliban since 2021, citing “significant limitations” on gathering evidence. The prosecutor’s office has appealed. The ruling follows Washington’s sanctions on 13 current and former ICC staff over the Afghanistan and Israel cases, and Rubio’s October pledge to “dismantle the ICC, brick by brick.” It amounts to imperialist impunity, written into law.

Nvidia fallout drags tech lower as rate-hike bets build

Chip stocks set the tone on Friday. Nvidia slid further after an HSBC downgrade, capping its worst week since April last year. It had fallen 4.34 percent Thursday, after opening as much as 14 percent lower. Cisco fell 6.2 percent despite beating forecasts. The selloff spread across Asia, where Shanghai’s ChiNext index dropped 3.32 percent on AI hardware stocks. The underlying driver is monetary. The 10-year Treasury yield rose above 5 percent, inflation has beaten expectations for a third straight month, and markets price about an 88 percent chance of a 25-basis-point Fed hike in December. The Fed raised rates in September for the first time since 2023 and now sits at 3.75–4 percent (Guardian).

The squeeze lands on workers. The 30-year mortgage rate is about 7.18 percent, close to a three-year high. October existing home sales fell to 3.75 million, the lowest since August 2010 and down 8.8 percent on the year, while the median price rose to $416,700.

US measles deaths reach 10 as Kennedy keeps them out of the federal count

Pennsylvania and Wisconsin each reported one more measles-associated death on Friday. One was an infant in Wisconsin; the other an unvaccinated patient in Pennsylvania. That brings the state-reported total for 2026 to 10: seven in Pennsylvania, two in Wisconsin, and New York’s first in more than 30 years. The CDC lists only six. Al Jazeera reports that Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr asked CDC director Erica Schwartz not to count the others, claiming no evidence they were measles-associated. This rejects the standard WHO and CDC definition of a death within 30 days of symptoms. The CDC counts 5,412 confirmed cases in 47 jurisdictions and 43 outbreaks, more than double 2025’s 2,289. Senator Ron Wyden, incoming Finance Committee chair, has opened an investigation into whether Kennedy “unlawfully lied” about his 2019 trip to Samoa, months before measles killed 83 people there, mostly young children.

Also on H5N1: a cluster in Yakima County, Washington, produced a second death and a third confirmed case on November 1. The CDC’s count stood at 74 human cases nationally as of November 5.

Ebola spreads to eight Congolese provinces

Six months after it was detected in May, Congo’s Ebola outbreak has spread to eight provinces across an area “about the size of Britain.” The NYT calls it “the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak in history.” In Biakato, which at one point accounted for a quarter of new national cases, bodies are stacked on racks and three-quarters of patients have died, because medicine, IV fluids and protective equipment are so scarce. “It’s as if we were starting from zero,” said Dr Jean-Jacques Muyembe, co-discoverer of the virus. The epicentre lies in a zone of Islamic State-linked violence. The collapse of US and European global health aid has left it with little support.

Arts and culture

M. John Harrison’s The End of Everything has become the first science fiction novel to win the Booker Prize. The Guardian’s editorial calls it “a novel for our age of permacrisis.” Harrison, a veteran of the British New Wave who has spent decades dissolving the line between genre and “literary” fiction, characteristically said that “writing science fiction is the last thing I want to do.” The award matters less as a victory for a genre than as a sign of the times. Serious writers are reaching for the apocalyptic and the uncanny to register a world that realist conventions struggle to contain. It is worth reading for whether Harrison’s vision of collapse points toward historical causes or settles into fatalism.

Perspective published: “Mississippi elects a black senator: A blow to racial politics” by Patrick Martin — read in full on wsws.org. (URL dated November 14; page dateline November 13.)

Martin argues that Colom’s victory undermines the claim, promoted by the Democrats and the New York Times’ 1619 Project, that race is the fundamental division in American society: “The fundamental division in capitalist society is class, not race.” He reviews Mississippi’s history of racial terror: Emmett Till (1955), the riots against James Meredith (1962), the murder of Medgar Evers (1963), and the killings of Chaney, Goodman and Schwerner (1964). He explains that the northern ruling class abandoned Reconstruction out of fear of the rising working class, and that the union bureaucracy never organised the South across racial lines. His data: Colom won by about 7,000 of more than 950,000 votes, in a state just under 38 percent Black. He carried about six white-majority counties and narrowly won Lafayette County (Ole Miss). Exit polls ranked fuel and grocery prices first (42 percent) and threats to democratic rights second (26 percent). Interracial marriage has risen from 3 percent of couples in 1967 to 19 percent of new marriages. Martin cites Obama and Clarence Thomas as proof that Black officeholders leave exploitation intact. He notes that Colom campaigned on “working across the aisle,” and calls for “the independent political mobilization of the working class.”

What the WSWS should cover today

1. The Shenzhen “Yalta”: Trump, Xi and Putin meet as the arms-control order collapses

The first-ever US–China–Russia summit comes nine months after New START expired. Moscow openly calls it a “new modern Yalta or Potsdam,” and Ukraine, Taiwan and Iran fear being bargained away. The WSWS should explain the summit as an attempt by the major powers to redivide spheres of influence amid a world war that is already underway, not as a step toward peace or disarmament.

Recommended reading Guardian Yalta became a byword for betrayal. Will Trump, Xi and Putin enact another carve-up?

2. Trump’s Nvidia trades and the oligarchy that runs US chip policy

The president bought millions in Nvidia stock before handing the company export concessions. His cabinet is now fighting in public over chips diverted to China, and the world’s most valuable chipmaker has been thrown into turmoil over its CEO’s private life. The story exposes the fusion of the state with a handful of tech billionaires, and it can be tied to the AI bubble and preparations for war with China.

Recommended reading Guardian Trump bought Nvidia stock as he helped chipmaker with change to export rule

3. The great ground stop: America’s crumbling air traffic system

The longest nationwide flight shutdown since 9/11 was caused by routers that “got overloaded” in an “incredibly old” system, according to the transportation secretary himself. It hit on the same day as a global Microsoft outage that disrupted NHS hospitals. Both make a sharp case about the decay of social infrastructure under a ruling class that spends trillions on war and stock buybacks. Air traffic controllers, understaffed and overworked, should be interviewed.

Recommended reading BBC What we know about the longest ground stop since 9/11

4. Measles deaths reach 10 as Kennedy suppresses the official toll

States report 10 measles deaths this year, but the CDC lists six because Kennedy told its director not to count the rest. Cases are more than double last year’s, and a Senate inquiry now examines Kennedy’s role in Samoa’s 2019 outbreak. This continues the WSWS’s coverage of the destruction of public health, and Congo’s record Ebola outbreak could be added for international context.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Measles-associated deaths reach 10 in US after two more deaths reported

5. Germany’s spy agencies get powers to “disrupt”: the Zeitenwende turns inward

The Bundestag has authorised the BND and BfV, the same BfV that monitors socialists as “extremists,” to hack and intervene actively, as youth protest against conscription and university cuts spreads. The SGP should analyse the law as part of the build-up of a police-state apparatus to accompany rearmament.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera German spies granted sweeping new powers

6. Brooklyn collapse: immigrant workers and children killed in a building reported as cracked

Eight people died, including two children and at least three construction workers, several of them undocumented. City records show the building’s cracks had been reported before. The story links landlord and contractor negligence, the city’s failure to act on complaints, and the war on immigrants, and could be told through interviews with families and construction workers.

Recommended reading Guardian Officials name victims of fatal Brooklyn building collapse as investigation widens

7. Sudan’s drone massacres and the powers arming both sides

“Double-tap” drone strikes are now the leading killer of civilians, hitting markets and transport hubs packed with displaced people. Coverage should identify the regional and imperialist powers whose weapons supplies and gold trade sustain both the army and the RSF.

Recommended reading Guardian Terror from the skies: drones are redefining warfare in Sudan
