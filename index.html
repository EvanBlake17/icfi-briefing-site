1. Trump declares Iran war “effectively over” as Tehran demands US “surrender” to its terms

After a closed-door White House meeting on Monday with Vice President JD Vance and Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani, Trump said the US-Israeli war on Iran is militarily finished: “Iran militarily is effectively over.” He called a resumption of strikes “quite unlikely” and cited “positive momentum.” He also made clear that the war’s economic machinery stays in place. The US naval blockade of Iranian ports continues, and so does the newly announced Task Force Iron Gate, which aims to cut Iran’s trade by land and across the Caspian Sea. Trump called the blockade “a continuation of the greatest naval blockade in the history of our country” and said US forces are “locked and loaded, ready to go for any reason that changes.” Sheikh Mohammed carried a two-day-old Iranian proposal to end the war and posted about “positive discussions on reaching a deal.”

Tehran dismissed Trump’s remarks as “fake news.” Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of the Supreme National Security Council, called on Washington to “surrender” to Iran’s seven conditions and said the war “has already wrecked the US government.” President Masoud Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei wants an end to a situation of “neither war nor peace,” and added: “We will not surrender to force and bullying.” The talk of de-escalation is about locking in the gains of a war of aggression through economic strangulation. Trump boasted that Iran has been “very much decapitated.”

Key facts: - Iran’s seven conditions include lifting US sanctions, releasing frozen funds, recognising a new Strait of Hormuz shipping route negotiated with Oman, and ending the war “on all fronts,” including Israel’s attacks on Lebanon. - The war began on February 28, 2026. Iran moved quickly to close Hormuz and struck US sites in Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and the UAE. Israel occupied southern Lebanon. US strikes have become sporadic, with recent “minor strikes” on Qeshm Island. - The US has posted a $20 million reward for information on Iran’s nuclear programme. In June 2025 Trump claimed enrichment had been “obliterated,” yet he has made it a central war aim again. - Monday’s oil drop came mainly from Trump’s remarks (see World economy).

2. US-brokered Russia-Ukraine talks open in Abu Dhabi as Europe demands a seat

Russian and Ukrainian delegations meet in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11 under US mediation led by Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. It is the third round in the UAE this year, and the first three-way meeting since Geneva on February 18. Ukraine’s delegation is led by Rustem Umerov, now head of the Foreign Intelligence Service. Russia is sending Kremlin aide Vladimir Medinsky, Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov and senior officer Yuri Zhigarlovsky. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said the talks will focus on security guarantees and stay three-way. France, Germany and the European Commission are sending officials to Abu Dhabi anyway. Trump told reporters he sees a “very strong inclination” to end the war, and he had told 60 Minutes that Ukraine will probably “end up with a little bit different shape.”

At the G7 foreign ministers’ meeting in Paris, Rubio’s first with allies since the midterms, European ministers objected to being sidelined. German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul said changing Europe’s security architecture is “certainly neither necessary, nor right, nor appropriate,” and that “Europe belongs at this table.” Ukraine’s Andrii Sybiha said: “No one has the right to decide our fate behind our backs.” Rubio, for his part, said guarantees for Kyiv “must also be acceptable to Russia” and that “the United States is not a combatant in that war.” The dispute is between rival imperialist interests over how Ukraine is carved up. In the WSWS Perspective, Patrick Martin notes that the main criticism of Trump in the US establishment press is that he is not aggressive enough toward Moscow. Thomas Friedman invoked Neville Chamberlain after Trump approved a deal allowing sales of Russian diesel.

Key facts: - On territory and guarantees, a US official says both sides have privately shown “some flexibility” on a territorial exchange. A guarantee modelled on NATO’s Article 5 is under discussion, and territory would likely be settled last. Russia occupies about 20 percent of Ukraine. Putin demands all of the Donbas. - Zelensky claims Russia is suffering “record losses” and that long-range strikes on Russian oil facilities are strengthening Kyiv’s hand. The Kyiv Independent reports a second record daily Russian loss figure in a month and Ukrainian strikes on a St. Petersburg missile-fuel plant. - The G7 meeting (November 9–10) also includes ministers from Saudi Arabia, Egypt, South Korea and Brazil. Iran is on Tuesday’s agenda, and G7 allies have voiced scepticism about the US-Israeli war. - Zelensky asked India’s Modi to help open dialogue, citing food security.

3. Trump alleges midterm fraud while Democratic leaders preach “moderation”

Trump on Monday reversed his public acceptance of the November 3 results, in which Democrats took both chambers of Congress. “Does anybody really believe that this ‘Election’ was honest,” he wrote on Truth Social, asking “How could the Polls be so wrong???” He offered no evidence and shared graphs questioning turnout data. Only days earlier he had told the New York Times he “absolutely” accepted the results. He told 60 Minutes he had congratulated Democrats and was open to working with them. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer called the turn “inevitable.” Megyn Kelly posted “Just stop.” Pew Research found that polls had understated the Democratic margin by at least two points in 23 of 27 governor and Senate races, the opposite of what Trump implied.

The Democratic leadership’s response has been to offer cooperation. In the WSWS Perspective, Patrick Martin dissects a Monday New York Times editorial, “This Winning Combination Is the Future of American Politics.” It tells Democrats to run as “economic populists and cultural moderates,” and it backs “well-funded police departments” and voter ID. Martin notes that Stephen Miller, architect of the mass deportation drive, became White House chief of staff the morning the editorial ran. He also notes that the editorial never mentions Iran, Gaza, Venezuela or Ukraine. As he writes, the vote expressed sentiment that is “far to the left of the Democratic Party.”

Key facts: - The NYT’s models include Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, who credited Trump with a fall in border crossings, and Kansas Senator-elect Adam Hamilton, whose ad attacked “socialism and open borders.” Its third example is Iowa’s Josh Turek, who voted with Republicans more than 700 times. - House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries has pledged to seek “common ground.” DSA member and New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani spoke of shared purpose with the president (WSWS). - Polling cited in the editorial (via WSWS): three-quarters of Americans are dissatisfied with the country’s direction. Only 38 percent view Republicans favourably and 39 percent Democrats. Fifty-three percent say people worth $100 billion or more take more from society than they contribute. - Two security breaches near the White House on Monday triggered a brief lockdown. A vehicle crashed at Lafayette Park and a second person jumped a barrier (Al Jazeera).

4. Massie files impeachment articles against Hegseth, Jeffries signs on

Outgoing Kentucky Republican Thomas Massie reintroduced eight articles of impeachment against War Secretary Pete Hegseth as the House opened its first post-election session on Monday. Speaker Mike Johnson dodged a vote on the same articles in September by sending members home early. Massie, who lost a Trump-backed primary this year, urged Hegseth to resign before Democrats take control in January. Jeffries, the expected incoming Speaker, backs the resolution and says Hegseth “engineered this reckless and costly war of choice in Iran.” Hegseth says he will litigate the military actions “all day long.” Trump said Hegseth is “guilty of two things, basically winning two wars.” Rubio called the Iran war “necessary” but conceded it was not necessarily popular.

Martin’s Perspective argues that Jeffries’s embrace of a Republican-initiated resolution against a subordinate is a deliberate way of avoiding any move against the president. The word “impeachment” does not appear in the Times editorial.

Key facts: - The charges described are waging war on Iran without congressional authorisation in violation of the War Powers Resolution, and a “warmaking culture” that disregarded rules of engagement. Massie cites the US bombing of a school in Minab, Iran, at the start of the war, which killed more than 150 people, including about 120 children. The articles also cover extrajudicial strikes on alleged drug boats in the Caribbean and Pacific, the abduction of former Venezuelan president Nicolás Maduro, restrictions on Pentagon press access, and an unlawful war against the Houthis. - The arithmetic: removal requires 67 Senate votes. Democrats are expected to hold about 54 seats from January. - Hegseth is scheduled to hold a press conference on Tuesday.

5. Winter floods kill five in Gaza tent camps as Israel blocks shelter

A rain-soaked sandbank collapsed onto a tent in Deir el-Balah on Sunday, killing four children and a woman. Two people remain missing. Gaza’s first winter storm hit on Friday and flooded camps for displaced people. It also weakened buildings already damaged by Israeli air raids. Because Israel restricts specialist rescue equipment, residents dug with their bare hands. Gaza’s Government Media Office held Israel, the United States and the “international community” “fully and directly responsible.” It also condemned the Trump-chaired “Board of Peace” and the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza. As Jacob Crosse writes on the WSWS, the deaths are a product of policy, not weather: the population has been denied durable shelter.

Key facts: - About 70,000 tents were flooded over the weekend (Al Jazeera). UNICEF says more than 106,000 people were directly affected (WSWS). - Save the Children says more than half a million children live in “critical or catastrophic” shelter conditions. Israel has repeatedly blocked the caravans the ceasefire envisaged as an alternative to tents. - Under the “ceasefire” in force since October 2025, Israel has killed more than 1,600 Palestinians. The toll since October 2023 exceeds 74,600.

Drone kills worker at Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant

The IAEA reported on November 9 that a drone killed an employee of the Russian-occupied Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, Europe’s largest. A second drone killed a worker at the nearby thermal plant. Director General Rafael Grossi condemned “unacceptable attacks violating indispensable nuclear safety pillars” but did not say who carried them out. On November 8 the plant lost its sole 330-kilovolt external line for about 10 hours and relied on diesel generators for cooling. A welder was killed in a drone strike on October 29. Separately, state generator Centrenergo warned of “extremely serious” consequences after a Russian strike on the Trypillia thermal power plant near Kyiv, as the capital braces for what officials call another “brutal” winter.

European powers rehearse a postwar Ukraine deployment and expand war financing

The British- and French-led “coalition of the willing” began Exercise Koalicja in Poland on November 9. It runs to November 22 under the Multinational Force Ukraine. About 2,800 troops from France, the UK, Poland, Belgium, Estonia, Germany, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden and Turkey are taking part. They are testing command and control and troop movement. Turkish forces are practising Black Sea demining, and allied jets are training to “secure Ukraine’s skies.” UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said the drill “shows the UK is stepping up.” London says the force would deploy only after hostilities end and at Kyiv’s request.

  • On financing, the EU plans a new €45 billion for Kyiv in 2027, on top of €45 billion already pledged for 2027 under the €90 billion loan agreed in April. The IMF has begun talks that could release a $1.67 billion tranche, and the EU will activate an emergency mechanism over Ukraine’s energy crisis.
  • German Chancellor Friedrich Merz complained of “unfair burden sharing” and told the EU to prioritise a new Ukraine loan.
  • Canada announced new Russia sanctions aimed at the defence industry and cyber activity. Moldova says it now “buys zero Russian gas.”

New Zealand election ends in deadlock

Preliminary results from the November 7 election leave no clear winner. Labour (29.36 percent) narrowly beat National (29.21 percent) on the party vote, the lowest combined major-party share on record. National nonetheless holds 42 seats to Labour’s 36 because of an electorate “overhang.” The National–ACT–NZ First bloc has 67 of 131 seats, a two-seat majority that roughly 399,000 uncounted special votes (about 14 percent) could erase when the count is completed on November 27. Labour leader Chris Hipkins accuses National of having “manipulated the electoral system” through vote-splitting. He calls democracy not a “plaything for billionaires.” Green co-leader Chlöe Swarbrick accuses Prime Minister Christopher Luxon of “voter suppression” over an enrolment change. Luxon says the result is “too close to call.”

  • The smaller parties: the Greens reached a record 13.3 percent, ACT 10.9 percent and NZ First 9.78 percent. The Opportunity Party (5.96 percent, 7 seats) enters parliament for the first time (WSWS figures).
  • Turnout was 74.6 percent of enrolled voters, the second-lowest on record. More than 1.2 million eligible adults did not vote (WSWS).

Wall Street posts best day since April on Iran de-escalation talk

US stocks staged a broad relief rally on Monday, November 9, after Trump called new strikes on Iran “highly unlikely.” The S&P 500 rose about 2.35 percent to roughly 7,550, its best session since April 8. The Nasdaq Composite gained 2.74 percent and the Dow about 2.15 percent. The rally was very broad: 4,704 issues rose and 1,328 fell, and all 11 sectors closed higher on a median basis, led by basic materials (+4.2 percent) and industrials (+4.0 percent), with energy last (+0.8 percent). Falling crude eased inflation fears ahead of today’s consumer price report.

The rally followed the AI-led crash of November 5–6. Wall Street shed roughly $3.2 trillion, and Tokyo and Seoul fell about 6 percent (the WSWS analysis is summarised below). Monday’s bounce does not show stability. Only 44 percent of stocks sit above their 50-day moving averages, though that is the highest reading in 44 sessions. The bounce came on the promise of lower oil, not any resolution of the leverage built up in AI.

Voters 3-to-1 less likely to back pro-war candidates, poll finds

A YouGov survey for the Eurasia Group’s Institute for Global Affairs, completed on the eve of the November 3 vote and released Monday, quantifies the anti-war vote:

  • 44 percent said a candidate’s support for the US-Israeli war on Iran made them less likely to vote for that candidate. Only 13 percent said more likely, and 26 percent said it made little difference.
  • 56 percent said avoiding higher domestic prices should come before Trump’s military goals. 22 percent said the war should come first.
  • 53 percent said Congress, not the president (29 percent), should decide when the war ends.
  • Republicans were the only group more likely to back a pro-war candidate. About a third said so, and 40 percent said it made no difference.

The institute’s Jonathan Guyer said the result creates an opening for Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed and “the wing of the Democratic Party represented by” Mamdani. Vice President Vance blamed the party’s losses on the war in a lengthy post, and Tucker Carlson and Marjorie Taylor Greene called it a betrayal of Trump’s promises. The poll shows that anti-war sentiment drove the vote, and that both the Democratic left and the “America First” right are trying to channel it.

Supreme Court clears border wall through Big Bend National Park

The Supreme Court on Monday granted the administration’s emergency request to lift District Judge Kathleen Cardone’s injunction against wall construction through a remote section of Big Bend National Park in West Texas. The court said the injunction “impedes the Government from advancing its ‘compelling interests’ in border security.” It found the government “exceedingly likely to prevail” on the argument that the 1996 Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act strips district courts of jurisdiction except over constitutional claims, and it held that “there is no such thing as a ‘constitutional’ major-questions claim.” Only Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson noted dissent, without explanation.

  • Cardone had found that the Big Bend sector is more than 25 percent of the border but accounts for only 0.3–2.2 percent of apprehensions. She warned that the waiver logic would extend to “the entire US-Mexico border.”
  • The plaintiffs were the Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church, and a river guide and landowner. They challenged DHS waivers that bypass the Endangered Species Act, NEPA and the National Historic Preservation Act.

COP31 opens in Antalya amid a war-driven fossil-fuel scramble

The UN climate conference opened on November 9 in Antalya, Türkiye, as the US-Israeli war on Iran exposes the world economy’s dependence on oil and gas shipped through Hormuz. COP31 president and Turkish Environment Minister Murat Kurum launched a non-binding “35-by-35 Global Electrification Pledge.” It aims for electricity to supply at least 35 percent of final energy demand by 2035, up from about one-fifth today according to the IEA. “Electrification is the answer,” Kurum said. Climate advocates point out that the pledge does not require the added electricity to come from low-carbon sources. A coalition including Pacific island states wants a clear roadmap away from fossil fuels. Saudi Arabia and other producers argue that oil and gas can remain “substantial” with carbon capture.

The war has pushed governments in the opposite direction from the conference’s stated aims. Trump is promoting more oil, gas and coal, and South Korea, India and Thailand have increased coal use to replace disrupted supplies. Renewable deployment is also rising, driven by cheap Chinese exports. The summit offers no binding mechanism, and its central pledge can be met by burning more coal.

US measles cases pass 5,000

The CDC’s latest update (data as of November 5) records 5,182 confirmed measles cases in 2026, compared with 2,289 for all of 2025. Of these, 5,164 were in 47 jurisdictions and 18 were among international visitors. There have been 43 outbreaks and four deaths, and about 96 percent of cases (4,993) are outbreak-associated. The CDC counts only confirmed cases reported to it, so true totals are higher.

H5N1 human cases in Washington state

The ECDC’s US tracker reports that as of November 1, two more residents of Yakima County, Washington, have been confirmed with H5N1 avian influenza, and that one has died. As of October 19 the CDC had counted 72 US human cases in 13 states since April 2024. The ECDC page may indicate a third case, and the figures need confirming against the Washington Department of Health. Outside the US, 12 human infections in three countries were reported between August 2025 and June 2026, three of them fatal. PAHO reports that animal outbreaks in the Americas are declining. Human cases are continuing even as animal outbreaks decline, which is why surveillance still matters.

On COVID-19, no current national data was retrieved. The CDC changed its wastewater-level method in August, and the BA.3.2 lineage, which rose from 0.19 to 0.55 percent of US sequences between December and March, remains under watch. Current dashboards should be checked before anything is written on this. The research agent found no major peer-reviewed studies dated this week.

Philippe Jaenada wins the Prix Goncourt

Philippe Jaenada has won France’s most prestigious literary prize for L’Inconnue du quai de Javel. This item was seen only through an aggregator listing of Guardian coverage, so the details should be confirmed. Jaenada is known for long, digressive, archive-driven books, among them La Serpe (2017) and Sulak. In them he reconstructs criminal cases and obscure lives from police files and press clippings, and he questions how official narratives were built. If the new novel follows that method, the award goes to a writer whose subject is the gap between institutional “truth” and lived reality. That is a notable choice amid the youth revolt in France. The Goncourt reliably turns its winner into a bestseller.

Australian Labor government expands deportation and visa ban powers — Mike Head

Ministerial Direction 123 lets Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke deport non-citizens who are convicted, accused, or suspected of supporting designated “harmful ideas,” including “antisemitic” statements as defined by the IHRA. The directive removes earlier weight given to family ties and the risk of persecution on return, and it binds Administrative Review Tribunal members. The tribunal had been overturning 54 percent of appealed cancellations. Cancellations and refusals rose from 29,074 in 2023–24 to 38,827 in 2024–25. Nearly three million people hold temporary visas and 93,000 asylum seekers are on bridging visas. Burke plans for 700,000 fewer residents by 2030–31 than pre-COVID forecasts. Head treats the measure as a weapon against opponents of the Gaza genocide.

Carney’s second budget offers huge tax breaks for big business, tens of billions more for war and austerity for workers — Roger Jordan

Canada’s budget raises defence spending to more than C$157 billion a year by 2030–31, over 4 percent of GDP. That comprises $99 billion for the military and $58 billion for related infrastructure. It includes up to 12 TKMS submarines, $7.6 billion for the NATO battalion in Latvia, and the 800-mile Mackenzie Valley Highway for Arctic deployment. A $36 billion “productivity mega deduction” cuts the effective corporate rate to 6.3 percent, the lowest in the G7. CSIS gets $6 billion more over 10 years, and about 40,000 public-sector jobs are being cut. Debt service is projected to reach $85 billion a year, overtaking health transfers. The Canadian Labour Congress objects mainly to Bill C-39, which would let the government ban strikes and impose contracts, yet it says the budget “takes steps on affordability.” Carney calls the moment “a hinge” in history.

KRANK Berlin (Berlin ER): A realistic hospital drama about profit and staff shortages — Markus Salzmann, Jan Ritter

The eight-part series, developed by former emergency doctor Samuel Jefferson with Viktor Jakovleski, follows Dr Zanna Parker in an understaffed Neukölln ER. To raise revenue she pushes unnecessary operations, and one patient loses a leg. The season ends with the hospital sold to a private investor.   • Thomas Scripps, “Two syndicalists gut the 1926 General Strike of its political lessons,” Part Three. Scripps reviews Callum Cant and Matthew Lee’s The Future in our Past (Verso).   • Kipchumba Ochieng, review of Hakim Adi’s Pan-Africanism and Communism, Part Seven. He then traces the Popular Front’s subordination of colonial struggles to “democratic” imperialists. That runs through France in 1936, Spain, and Italy’s 1935 invasion of Ethiopia, and leads to the founding of the Fourth International in 1938.
