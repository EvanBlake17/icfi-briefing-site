1. Trump threatens to bomb Iran’s Pickaxe Mountain as Gulf war spreads to Kuwait and Hormuz

The US-Iran war is moving back toward full-scale bombing. Speaking at Mar-a-Lago on Thanksgiving, Trump said the US “would absolutely hit Pickaxe Mountain,” a fortified underground complex next to the Natanz enrichment plant. In a holiday call to troops he said the US was “loaded up.” At the same time the war is spreading across the Gulf. Italian Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani said Kuwaiti investigators had confirmed that the drone which killed two Italian citizens in Kuwait was made in Iran. Tehran denies launching it from its territory and has offered to join an Italian-Kuwaiti commission of inquiry. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards (IRGC) say two oil tankers caught fire and exploded after hitting naval mines near Oman’s Khasab port and in the eastern Strait of Hormuz. The IRGC also says it shot down a drone over southeastern Iran.

The US blockade of Iranian ports is tightening. A US aircraft fired into the stern of the Palau-flagged cargo ship Pacific Archer in the Gulf of Oman after it left Bandar Abbas, disabling its engines. Israeli military spokesman Effie Defrin told Kan that Israel is “ready to strike with force at any moment,” including without the Americans. In Washington, Democrats take control of both houses of Congress on January 3 after campaigning partly on ending the war. They are already signalling that they will not use the December 11 funding deadline to cut off war money. As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, the compromise being discussed would bar war funding except for “defense of U.S. troops,” a restriction that keeps “the war machine in place.”

Key facts: - Naval deployment: The USS Theodore Roosevelt arrived in mid-November, joining the George H.W. Bush and George Washington. That makes three carriers, with 13 destroyers and two cruisers in the Arabian Sea and about 2,000 Marines following on amphibious ships (US Navy, via WSWS). - Blockade: By the US military’s count, it has disabled six ships and turned back 158. - Funding math: The current continuing resolution expires December 11. A new one needs 60 Senate votes, and Republicans hold 53. Forty-two Senate Democrats voted for the current one in August. - Shipping: Al Jazeera’s live coverage notes that the war has continued at sea despite the June 17 US-Iran memorandum of understanding on Hormuz.

2. Washington proposes new tariffs on 14 countries and tightens chip controls aimed at China

Three weeks after its midterm defeat, the Trump administration has proposed a new set of tariffs on 14 trading partners. The legal basis is Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act, and the stated grounds are “excess industrial capacity.” The measures replace the emergency “reciprocal” tariffs that the Supreme Court struck down in February. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said they probably won’t take effect for months, but a senior official said duties could begin in January. Although the official target is “overcapacity,” the documents are aimed at China. Capital Economics counted the word “China” 253 times in the USTR’s report on Vietnam and 315 times in the one on the EU. Third countries are being penalized for taking in Chinese investment and goods.

Alongside the tariffs, the Commerce Department now requires data-center operators in third countries, Malaysia above all, to get US export licenses to buy Nvidia chips. That came in the same week Trump said he had persuaded Nvidia’s board to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO, less than two weeks after Huang’s sudden resignation. Nvidia’s stock jumped 31 percent on the news. ABC commentator Laura Tingle described it as a presidential intervention that added a trillion dollars to one company’s market value. As Nick Beams wrote on the WSWS, the Huang episode “was not a concession to Beijing,” and each failed attempt to curb China’s export growth leads Washington to “double down.”

Key facts: - Proposed rates: EU, South Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan start at 15 percent. Vietnam faces 35 percent (20 percent with a deal) and India 25 percent (17 percent with a deal). Singapore’s maximum is 20 percent (CNBC). - China: Capped at 20 percent on top of the existing 25 percent from Trump’s first term. That could rise to 40 percent if the truce is not extended past January 12. Beijing called the probe “a typical act of unilateralism.” - Exemptions: Korean chips are excluded, and Taiwan’s electronics, including phones and networking gear, are exempt. TSMC rose 3.2 percent on Friday. - Europe: The European Commission rejects the overcapacity finding and expects its 15 percent “all-inclusive ceiling” to hold. EU trade ministers speak with Greer on Monday. Citi warns of possible 2027 earnings downgrades for Adidas, H&M, Puma and Primark. - Quote: “The tariff wall keeps changing, but it isn’t coming down” (ING’s Inga Fechner). - Next: Trump and Xi are expected to meet for the fourth time this year at the G20 summit in Miami in December.

3. Israel strikes Doctors Without Borders clinic in Gaza as ceasefire death toll passes 1,700

An Israeli strike on a Doctors Without Borders (MSF) clinic in Gaza City killed two of its staff, both watchmen. MSF said the premises were “clearly marked with MSF logos” and that the Israeli military knew where they were. It called the attack a “direct hit” and said 17 of its staff have now been killed in Gaza. The Israeli military claimed, without evidence, that the two men were Hamas members. WHO Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus called the strike “outrageous and unacceptable.” Earlier in the week another strike killed Adel al-Helou, who had worked for UNRWA for 35 years, while he was delivering water in the Jabalia refugee camp. Three other people were wounded.

Thirteen months into the October 2025 “ceasefire,” the killing continues every day. Israel killed four more people on Thursday, and a strike targeted the son of Hamas leader Khalil al-Hayya, who survived. In Israel itself, a month after the October 27 election, no government has been formed. Gadi Eisenkot, who holds the mandate to form one, accused Netanyahu’s Likud of stalling (see International).

Key facts: - Killed since the ceasefire: at least 1,705 people, according to Gaza’s Health Ministry. The total war death toll is at least 74,772. - Health workers: More than 1,700 have been killed since October 2023. - Aid blockade: In January MSF said Israel had blocked its supplies and refused entry to all its international staff since the start of 2026. - Euro-Med Monitor accused Israel of “systematic targeting of humanitarian personnel and facilities.”

4. Ebola epidemic in Congo passes 12,000 cases as Uganda begins first African mRNA vaccine trial

According to Thursday’s government data, the Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) has reached at least 12,021 confirmed infections and 5,904 deaths since it began in mid-May. That makes it one of the largest Ebola epidemics on record, and it is getting almost no attention in the Western press. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, which is rarer than the Zaire strain and for which no licensed vaccine exists.

On Friday the Uganda Virus Research Institute launched Africa’s first clinical trial of an mRNA Ebola vaccine. It was developed by Moderna and is partly funded by CEPI. The trial will enroll 224 volunteers and is the first to test the vaccine in children aged one and older. CEPI head Richard Hatchett said children are often among the hardest hit by Ebola but “too often the last to benefit from new vaccines.” The other candidate, the Oxford/Serum Institute ChAdOx1 vaccine, begins testing in the DRC on Monday.

Key facts: - Case fatality: about 49 percent of confirmed cases have died (5,904 of 12,021). - Timeline: Gavi hopes trials will conclude in “early 2027.” It signed a purchase commitment last week for 1.5 million ChAdOx1 doses and plans “hundreds of thousands” ready to deploy if the trials succeed and the WHO and DRC approve the vaccine. - Uganda’s own outbreak was declared over on July 28, after 20 infections and two deaths. - Moderna’s first human trial of the vaccine began in Canada in August with adults aged 18–65. - Context: The epidemic has grown while the US has withdrawn from the WHO and cut global health funding.

5. Costa Rica holds UN rapporteur Francesca Albanese at airport as a “terrorist”

Francesca Albanese, the UN special rapporteur on the occupied Palestinian territories, spent a second night held at Juan Santamaría International Airport near San José on Friday. Costa Rican immigration police detained her on arrival from Tunisia via Paris on November 26 and refused her entry. She had come to receive an honorary doctorate from the University of Costa Rica (UCR), which she ended up accepting by video from an airport waiting room. Immigration director Omer Badilla said she had “an international alert” for terrorism links but did not say who issued it. In the habeas corpus proceedings, the only legal basis the government cited was that the US Treasury’s OFAC added her to its sanctions list in July 2025. The Constitutional Chamber (Sala IV) has barred her expulsion while it rules.

The case has become an issue in the race for UN secretary-general. More than 400 people signed an open letter demanding that Rebeca Grynspan respond. Grynspan is Costa Rica’s candidate, a former vice president and the current head of UNCTAD, and she led the November 20 Security Council straw poll. She did not respond to Al Jazeera. As Andrea Lobo noted on the WSWS, the detention amounts to Costa Rica “adopting US sanctions lists as domestic policy,” branding opposition to the Gaza genocide as terrorism.

Key facts: - UN human rights chief Volker Türk urged Costa Rica to reverse the decision “urgently.” - Israel’s UN ambassador Danny Danon praised Costa Rica. Israel was the only government to applaud. - Albanese: defending human rights is “not terrorism.” - Rodrigo Chaves, the former president and now minister of the presidency and finance, called the court’s stay a usurpation by an “arbitrary” judiciary. - Ombudsman (Defensoría): UN rapporteurs are immune from detention for acts within their mandate.

6. Victorian Labor braces for wipeout as One Nation surges

Counting began Saturday evening in Victoria’s state election, held in severe weather that closed or flooded some polling booths. Labor has governed since 2014, and Premier Ben Carroll is seeking a fourth term. He may lose his own seat of Niddrie, which Labor holds on a 6.7 percent margin. Deputy Premier Gabrielle Williams said a Labor result in the teens was “certainly in the window.” The Liberal-National Coalition under Jess Wilson needs 16 more seats to reach the 45 required for government. If she wins, Wilson would be the first woman elected premier of Victoria at a general election. The far-right One Nation has polled at about 20 percent statewide. It expects six or seven lower house seats and up to ten in the upper house. In one very small early sample, the 38 votes from the Dargo booth in Gippsland East, it led the Nationals 55 percent to 30 percent.

The Liberals released their costings two days before the vote, after about two million people had already voted early. They include a partial privatization of Melbourne’s sewerage network, which Wilson called “a lease, not a sale,” abolition of the first-home buyers’ grant, and cuts to the level-crossing removal program. The Parliamentary Budget Office estimates the package would cut net debt by $20 billion over four years. The collapse of Labor’s vote reflects a national cost-of-living crisis, analyzed in the WSWS coverage below.

Key facts: - Seats going in: Labor 56, Coalition 29 and Greens 3, out of 88. - Greens hope to hold Melbourne, Brunswick and Richmond, retake Prahran, and win inner-north seats including Northcote and Footscray. - Steve Bracks, a former Labor premier, said Carroll “came in too late” and predicted a large Liberal win. - The Victorian Electoral Commission will not declare official results tonight.

Spain votes Sunday with the far right poised to enter government

Spain holds its general election on Sunday, November 29. Pedro Sánchez (PSOE) called it on October 5. The right-wing Popular Party (PP) and the fascist Vox have led every poll since then. If they win, the far right would enter a national government for the first time since the end of the Franco dictatorship. Immigration has become a central issue. On Friday Spain’s maritime rescue service found eight to ten bodies aboard a boat carrying about 100 people, 39 nautical miles south of El Hierro. Six survivors were airlifted to hospital in serious condition, and another 84 people from a second boat landed at La Restinga. Canary Islands leader Fernando Clavijo appealed to Madrid and Brussels to act “on the shores of Senegal and Gambia.” The PP and Vox are campaigning for much tighter border controls.

  • Arrivals: A record 2,100 people reached the Canaries in a single day earlier this week, mostly on El Hierro, an island of about 11,400 residents. On November 14 rescuers brought in nearly 1,500. El Hierro declared an emergency earlier in the month.
  • Route: Patrols off Senegal, Mauritania and Morocco have pushed departures south to The Gambia, Guinea-Bissau and Guinea. That makes the crossing longer and deadlier.
  • 2024 record: more than 46,000 arrivals.
  • Social context: Since September a housing movement has grown after police evicted 87-year-old Maricarmen Abascal, who died two weeks later. A one-day nationwide strike was held on November 11 (WSWS).

Serbian student movement names chemistry professor for presidency

The student-led movement that brought down Aleksandar Vučić’s government has nominated Radomir Saičić, 65, for the December 27 presidential election.   • Saičić promised a “sovereign Serbia,” the rule of law and an anti-corruption drive. He said the “outgoing regime is still trying to disintegrate our state.”
  • The program offers institutional “restoration” and no social demands. A mass movement of workers and youth is being channeled into a national-democratic reform government.

Saudi-Pakistani-Turkish “Mecca pact” meets over Houthi attacks

The Strategic Political and Defence Committee of the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement met in Islamabad on Friday. Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Türkiye signed the pact on August 7. Its core clause is that an attack on one member is an attack on all. The members condemned Houthi attacks on Saudi civilians and infrastructure as “persistent and heinous,” in the words of Pakistan’s Ishaq Dar, but announced no new measures. Pakistan will defend Saudi territory but has ruled out strikes inside Yemen. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said “no decision has been made on deploying troops inside Yemen.”

  • Casualties: at least 26 people killed in Saudi Arabia by suspected Houthi drones and missiles since September 23, 23 of them in strikes on airports.
  • Expansion: The members agreed in principle on a roadmap for new members. Egypt, Bangladesh and Syria are considering joining.
  • Ratification: The Turkish parliament ratified the pact on November 5 with votes from the AKP, MHP and the “opposition” CHP (WSWS/SEP Türkiye).
  • Iraq: Separately, Trump endorsed the Iraqi prime minister’s crackdown on oil smuggling, part of Washington’s effort to choke off Iranian revenue.

Thanksgiving storm knocks out power to 900,000 and postpones Macy’s parade

A huge storm system swept the country over the holiday, bringing flooding, water rescues and downed power lines from New York to Virginia. Power outages peaked above 900,000 on Thursday and were still at 264,000 Friday evening, mostly in Pennsylvania and Missouri (poweroutage.us). Bendigo Writers Festival apologizes to Randa Abdel-Fattah

Greater Bendigo City Council and La Trobe University have “unreservedly” apologized to Palestinian Australian author Randa Abdel-Fattah, settling the discrimination suit she filed in June. Three days before the August 2025 festival, where she was due to discuss her book Discipline, the organizers issued a code of conduct. More than 50 authors boycotted in solidarity, and the opening and closing nights were cancelled. Historian Clare Wright, a guest curator who joined the boycott and has since taken a voluntary redundancy from La Trobe, says staff and the other authors are also owed an apology.

The apology is a small but real defeat for the effort to use “antisemitism” definitions to silence opposition to the Gaza genocide in cultural life. AI “micro-dramas” wipe out work for Chinese actors

According to Chinese government figures, AI produced more than 90 percent of China’s new micro-dramas in 2026, about 1,600 new titles a day in the first half of the year. 3. Victorian election: Labor’s collapse and the rise of One Nation

With results due tonight and tomorrow, the WSWS should analyze Labor’s rout, One Nation’s roughly 20 percent vote and the Liberals’ plan to partly privatize the sewerage network. That analysis can build on this week’s coverage of the cost-of-living crisis and the “silence on war.” One Nation’s rise shows the far right taking advantage of social anger that Labor, the Greens and the unions have suppressed.

4. Deaths off El Hierro: Spain’s election and the war on migrants

Eight to ten migrants died on the eve of an election in which the PP and Vox are campaigning on border militarization. The Atlantic route has been pushed south by EU-funded patrols, which has made it deadlier. A follow-up to the EEB statement could expose the “humane” PSOE’s role in EU border policy and the bipartisan consensus on “Fortress Europe.”

5. Thanksgiving storm exposes a crumbling US grid

More than 900,000 customers lost power, 264,000 were still without it a day later, and record tides forced evacuations along the length of California’s coast. The WSWS could connect the storm to decades of underinvestment in privatized utilities, the extreme weather of a record El Niño, and the administration’s dismantling of FEMA and NOAA.

6. South Korea’s irregular school workers strike against precarious employment

The workers who feed South Korea’s schoolchildren and run after-school care struck nationally for 24 hours. In the same week, property tax data showed the Seoul real estate boom pulling 190,000 more people into the top tax bracket. This is an opportunity to cover the class struggle in South Korea, which gets little coverage, and the role of the union bureaucracy in limiting it to a one-day action.

7. AI micro-dramas and the destruction of actors’ livelihoods in China

AI now produces more than 90 percent of China’s new short dramas, and actors are turning to delivery driving and livestreaming. An arts-and-labor piece could connect this to the struggles of Hollywood writers and actors over AI and to the general question of who controls the technology.

8. Norse Atlantic collapse: the Iran war’s first airline casualty

Norse grounded its whole fleet and warned of bankruptcy, citing jet fuel costs since the Iran war began. Its crews and ground staff face sudden unemployment. The WSWS could use the case to report on the war’s economic effects on aviation workers internationally.
