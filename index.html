1. Spanish high-speed rail collision kills at least 68 after safety system fails

At about 8:53 a.m. on Wednesday, October 28, a southbound Renfe AVE high-speed train from Madrid to Seville ran into the rear of a stationary train run by the private operator Iryo. The crash happened near Urda in Toledo province, about 150 km south of Madrid. Two Iryo carriages derailed and fell down an embankment. The Castilla-La Mancha regional government puts the dead at at least 68. Regional president Emiliano García-Page said about 80 people were injured, around 50 seriously and 15 critically, and warned that some might not survive. The WSWS, citing hospital figures, reports 107 hospitalized. More than 200 troops of the Military Emergencies Unit were deployed. The rail infrastructure operator ADIF has suspended all high-speed service between Madrid and Andalusia.

Transport Minister Óscar Puente said the LZB automatic train-control system failed at about 8:30 a.m. along a 63 km (39-mile) stretch, affecting six trains. Control passed to the Atocha control post, which cleared the Renfe train to continue as normal. According to Puente, the train was manually authorized past a red signal. He called this “inexplicable” and put responsibility on the control centre, which is run by ADIF, a state body under his own ministry. He has refused to resign. Iryo disputes part of his account. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez declared three days of official mourning (October 29–31) and said “two train accidents in one year” were unacceptable. He insisted the causes “appeared unrelated.” The crash comes nine months after the Adamuz disaster on the same line, involving the same two operators. Al Jazeera puts the Adamuz death toll at 46 and the WSWS at 42. Spain goes to snap elections on November 29.

As Santiago Guillen and Alex Lantier write on the WSWS, the crash follows years of rail liberalization, which “multiplied traffic without a matching rise in maintenance capacity.” Over the same period military spending doubled to €34 billion.

Key facts: - Passengers: 397 on the Renfe train, 147 on the Iryo train (regional spokeswoman Esther Padilla). - Engineer Ricardo Díaz Martínez: “human error cannot by itself cause such a tragedy because there are automated security systems.” - José Trigueros of the Civil Engineering Association: “I hold the minister responsible.” - The CSIF civil servants’ union demands Puente’s resignation. It says it warned in September about excessive working hours at ADIF. It has also defended the presumption of innocence for ADIF’s head of traffic. - After Adamuz, the government promised safety checks on all lines nationwide. - Ursula von der Leyen: “Today, Europe mourns with you.” Emmanuel Macron also sent condolences.

2. Israeli election produces deadlock as Likud claims vote was “stolen by Arab parties”

Israel’s October 27 election, the first since October 7, 2023, has given neither bloc a majority. With about 90 percent of votes counted:

  • Netanyahu’s bloc (Likud, Otzma Yehudit, Religious Zionism, Shas, United Torah Judaism) has 53–54 seats.
  • The bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief, has 47–48.
  • The Joint List and Ra’am together have a projected record 19.

A majority requires 61 of 120 seats. Between 480,000 and 500,000 “double-envelope” ballots, mostly from soldiers, police and diplomats, have yet to be counted. Final results could come Thursday evening, and the official certification is set for November 4. Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister. President Isaac Herzog begins consultations next week, and whoever he designates gets up to 42 days to form a coalition.

The Arab vote decided the outcome. Turnout among Palestinian citizens rose from about 55 percent in 2022 to over 80 percent, almost doubling the Arab parties’ 2022 total of 10 seats. Likud responded with a statement charging that “the elections in the State of Israel were stolen by Arab parties that support terrorism,” citing “bribery, threats, violence, forgeries and prohibited software.” Netanyahu and Eisenkot have both ruled out a minority government that depends on Arab parties. Eisenkot said that joining with them “is against my principles,” and that his aim is “a Zionist mobilisation government.” Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen said his alliance could back an anti-Netanyahu government from outside but would not join one. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera expect a Netanyahu-Eisenkot unity government. The Tel Aviv 125 index fell as much as 3.5 percent on Wednesday morning.

Chris Marsden writes on the WSWS that the result “decisively refuted” the “concept of a more progressive form of Zionism.” The Western-backed Eisenkot alternative has collapsed, he argues, leaving Netanyahu free to pursue “a renewed war against Iran planned with Trump,” possibly once the US midterms are over.

Key facts: - The parties in Eisenkot’s bloc won 46 seats in 2022. Their current projected totals are Yashar 21, Together 11, Yisrael Beiteinu 7–8, Democrats 8. - Shas fell from 11 seats to about seven after ultra-Orthodox voters stayed home in protest at the failure to pass a yeshiva draft exemption. - Before the vote Trump told Israelis that choosing “the wrong Leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi,” would force the US to act against Iran itself. - Eisenkot wrote the “Dahiya doctrine” of disproportionate force against civilian areas. He sat in Netanyahu’s war cabinet for eight months of the Gaza genocide and rejects a two-state solution. - A Channel 12 poll found that 74 percent of first-time voters aged 18–22 call themselves right or centre-right.

3. Washington opens “medical supplies” channel to Tehran as Iran presses seven-day Hormuz plan

The US-Israeli war on Iran, which began on February 28 and killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei on its first day, is now in its eighth month. Diplomatic activity has picked up. Trump said he approved Vice President JD Vance’s request to contact Tehran over Iran’s “need for help with medical supplies.” He denied that he had told Vance to restart peace talks. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Iran remains “in close and ongoing contact” with mediators about carrying out its seven-day plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. About 20 percent of world oil and LNG trade normally passes through the strait. Iran closed it after the war began, and it has stayed largely shut despite a June 17 US-Iran memorandum of understanding.

The diplomacy comes alongside renewed strikes. Al Jazeera reports that US-Iran exchanges of fire have resumed, and Iran claims to have captured an unmanned US submarine. Energy markets reported a missile attack on Saudi Arabia’s largest refinery, and Brent crude climbed to about $115–116 a barrel on October 28, up from $106.65 on October 12. Oil eased in Asian trading on Thursday after news of Vance’s outreach.

The “humanitarian” framing hides how the war is being negotiated, not how it is ending. Israel’s election result leaves Netanyahu in place with Trump’s open endorsement. The WSWS has repeatedly warned that a new escalation is being prepared for after the US midterms on November 3.

Key facts: - The war began February 28. Iran had already fought a 12-day war with Israel and the US in June 2025. - The war is driving energy inflation worldwide. Canada, the UK and the US all cited energy prices in policy decisions this week (see World economy).

4. Flavio Bolsonaro escapes Brazil on foot and declares himself “true president” from Miami

Days after losing Brazil’s presidential election to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, the son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, landed at Opa-locka Executive Airport near Miami at 9:30 a.m. on October 28 with his wife and two daughters. Brazil’s Supreme Court had ordered him to hand over his regular and diplomatic passports so he could not leave before being investigated for embezzlement and money laundering. Argentine immigration authorities say the family crossed into Argentina late Tuesday. Al Jazeera’s John Holman says they crossed “on a rope bridge, on foot.” Within an hour they boarded a Baires Fly private jet to Guayaquil, Ecuador, and then flew on to Miami. Holman called the route “stepping stones among allies,” since Argentina’s Milei and Ecuador’s Noboa are both close to Trump. The US State Department issued the family visas.

In Miami, Bolsonaro claimed to be “the true elected president of Brazil” and thanked Trump. He alleged fraud without offering evidence and described a “state of exception” in Brazil. He said he would lead the opposition from the US. Meanwhile his own party has accepted the result. He gave up his Senate seat to run for president, so his parliamentary immunity ends in January. Brazil and the US have an extradition treaty, but Trump has called the Bolsonaros “fine people,” and Holman expects no quick return. His father made the same trip to Florida after losing in 2022, and his supporters stormed Brasília’s government buildings while he was there. Jair Bolsonaro is now under house arrest.

Andrea Lobo calls the episode a US-backed attempt to set up a “parallel government” on US soil. It follows Washington’s 50 percent tariffs on Brazil, later cut to 25 and 12.5 percent, and Trump’s open campaigning for the Bolsonaro family.

Key facts: - Lula’s margin was 4.4 million votes. Bolsonaro won 59.8 million votes (WSWS). - Florida has the largest Brazilian population in the US.

5. UN experts condemn French repression as Macron threatens state of emergency

Four experts appointed by the UN Human Rights Council, including Gina Romero, special rapporteur on freedom of peaceful assembly, and Farida Shaheed, issued a statement on Wednesday condemning the “excessive use of force” by French authorities against the nationwide student protest movement. “The scale of the arrests and detention, affecting several thousand people, mainly minors, appears disproportionate,” they wrote. They called serious and sometimes irreversible injuries to underage protesters “unacceptable and unjustifiable.” They also criticized the government’s mainly security-based response for sidelining the protests’ “root causes,” and repeated their call for a full review of French policing. In Lille earlier this month, a teenager lost a hand when a grenade exploded during clashes with police.

The movement began in mid-September over crumbling high-school buildings, overcrowded classrooms and teacher shortages. It has since merged with “yellow vest” protests over energy prices and the cost of living. Interior Ministry figures: last Saturday 410,000 people marched nationwide and 70,000 security forces were deployed, with 1,000 arrests. The week before there were 2,700 arrests. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez promised to be “extremely vigilant, extremely firm.” On a visit to Marseille, Macron said a state of emergency was not off the table: “We are upholding the Republic and public order without a state of emergency.”

Alex Lantier writes on the WSWS that the ruling class is “adopting measures from the National Rally’s program before the party holds power.” A state of emergency would allow warrantless searches, protest bans, house arrests and press censorship.

Key facts: - The Interior Ministry has recorded about 3,700 protest-related arrests since October 17. - Pending legislation: a police “presumption of legitimate use of weapons” bill passed its first reading on July 7. More than 720,000 people signed a petition against it, and Amnesty calls it a “license to kill.” A Senate “rioter pays” bill would fine anyone present where property is damaged up to €10,000. - Union Étudiante spokesperson Alice Cohen-Morzadec called the movement “a general outburst of resentment against” Macron. - Education budgets are being cut as part of planned fiscal consolidation. - Marine Le Pen leads every presidential poll.

6. Federal judge blocks Trump bid to void 20,000 California ballots as subpoenas hit 13 states

Six days before the November 3 midterms, US District Judge David Carter refused a Justice Department request to temporarily stop California from counting mail ballots from about 20,000 people the administration says are noncitizens. “Never before in American history has the federal government, on the eve of an election,” Carter wrote, sought to “coerce a state and local government to abandon their own Constitutional elections procedures.” He found that the request violated “longstanding constitutional principles mandating that states have control over their elections.” The department, which filed suit on October 15, immediately appealed to the Ninth Circuit.

Reuters found that the California case is one part of a much larger operation. The administration has issued more than 100 subpoenas in at least 13 states, both Republican- and Democratic-led, demanding records on tens of thousands of voters. Most were sent in the past three months, and many had not been reported before. Legal experts say the records could be used to challenge close results. In a court filing, a government whistleblower warned that the department’s “Unlawful Voter Initiative” is “knowingly sweeping known US citizens in.” Democracy Docket found that one in four people on the Los Angeles County “noncitizen” list were born in the United States.

This comes alongside the third criminal case against James Comey in 13 months, a search of Rep. Ilhan Omar’s home, and a suit over Alaska’s Senate certification. Together these amount to an attempt to prepare grounds for contesting the midterm results. The Democrats’ response has been confined to the courts.

Key facts: - Carter is a 1998 Clinton appointee. - An October 9 ruling had already found the administration’s use of voter data unlawful. - There is “scant evidence” of widespread noncitizen voting, and it is already illegal in federal elections. - California says the federal list rests on “suspect data.”

Europe

Healey’s first budget funds war while households get £100

Chancellor John Healey delivered the first budget of Andy Burnham’s three-month-old Labour government on October 28. He called it “a Budget for the working people of Britain.” The Office for Budget Responsibility forecasts no growth in real earnings next year and has cut its 2027 growth forecast from 1.5 percent to 1 percent. Economist James Meadway of Verdant warned it could be “the worst Parliament for living standards in modern history.” He said the budget offered “nothing like enough.”

  • Households: a one-off £100 energy payment to 20 million homes in council tax bands A–D (cost £2.6 billion), the VAT cut on energy extended to July 1, a £2 bus-fare cap from January 2027, and a continued fuel-duty freeze. Cornwall Insight forecasts a £276 (16 percent) rise in the January price cap, to £1,999.
  • War: an extra £10 billion in economic and military aid to Ukraine, on top of £25 billion already committed. The £4.7 billion hole in the Defence Investment Plan will be filled by cutting departmental capital budgets and taking £1 billion from the Warm Homes Plan. NATO’s Mark Rutte praised the plan’s “strong commitment.” Healey did not say how the 3.5-percent-of-GDP target for 2035 will be funded.
  • Banks: the bank corporation-tax surcharge rises from 3 to 6 percent for 15 months only, raising £1.5 billion. Lloyds shares rose 4.2 percent.
  • Wages: minimum-wage rates rise 3.9 percent in April. Healey broke Labour’s 2024 manifesto pledge to abolish youth rates.
  • Debt: Debt interest is £14 billion higher this year because of war-driven borrowing costs, and fiscal headroom has fallen to £15 billion.

Robert Stevens on the WSWS: “The £100 will be swallowed up within weeks.”

Romanian appeals court keeps Georgescu in custody

On Wednesday the Bucharest Court of Appeal extended the pre-trial detention of the far-right, pro-Russian politician Călin Georgescu by 30 days, to November 27. It overturned an October 13 lower-court ruling that would have moved him to house arrest. Prosecutors say he and two others took €1.1 million from a businessman by promising to obtain a €15 million foreign credit line. Hundreds of supporters protested, and some entered the court building. George Simion of the far-right AUR had praised the lower-court ruling as proof that protesters “were being heard.” Georgescu topped the first round of the November 2024 presidential election, which was annulled over alleged Russian interference. He faces a separate indictment for inciting violence and promoting 1930s fascist leaders. Romania’s establishment parties are using the courts against the far right while carrying out austerity. Parliament has just approved pro-EU Luca Niculescu as prime minister after months of deadlock.

Middle East

Hebron lockdown displaces 5,000 as settlers beat journalists

Hebron, the largest city in the West Bank, has been under a “near-total lockdown” for six days. It was imposed after Palestinian gunmen killed two Israeli soldiers at a checkpoint last week. UNRWA says the incursions have displaced nearly 5,000 Palestinians and calls the “collective punishment” a violation of international law. Roland Friedrich, UNRWA’s West Bank director, warned of “alarming echoes” of the early-2025 “Iron Wall” operation in the north, which still leaves more than 34,000 refugees from Jenin, Tulkarem and Nur Shams displaced more than a year later. Palestinian officials accused Netanyahu of staging the operation to win settler votes.

  • Troops raided Fawwar refugee camp around midnight. In Tulkarem they detained four people, including a journalist and his two brothers. In Burin they turned homes into barracks. Schools in southern Nablus have switched to remote learning.
  • In Umm al-Khair (Masafer Yatta), armed settlers beat US journalist Jasper Nathaniel and Israeli-Russian activist Andrey X. Israeli forces then arrested both victims. Andrey X was concussed and was denied hospital care.
  • On October 16 in Abu Falah, settlers beat Washington Post photojournalist Heidi Levine, who was wearing a “PRESS” vest, and stole her passport and equipment.

Iraqi premier makes first post-Assad visit to Damascus

Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi met Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa at the People’s Palace in Damascus. It is the first visit by an Iraqi premier since Assad fell. Talks focused on exporting Iraqi oil through Baniyas, expanding trade and integrating agriculture. Since the war on Iran shut Hormuz, Iraq has begun shipping limited quantities of oil through Syria. In July the two countries signed agreements to revive the Haditha–Baniyas pipeline, with a consortium led by Chevron. Zaidi described the two peoples as “one people in two countries.” The pipeline would give US capital a strategic bypass route around the Gulf through a Syrian regime Washington now backs.

Taiz siege doubles prices

Taiz, Yemen’s third city with about 1 million people, has been cut off since early October, when Houthi forces took al-Turbah and severed the road to Aden. Fuel stations have been closed since October 5. Petrol (20 litres) now costs 120,000 riyals, up from 30,000. Cooking gas costs 40,000 riyals, up from 11,400. The price of tanker water has roughly tripled. On October 22 the WHO said 42 health facilities reported severe shortages of fuel, oxygen and medicine. A displaced father of four: “The whole day, we either eat rice or beans.” The war reignited in July, and the Saudi-led coalition has carried out hundreds of air raids.

Africa

Russia-Africa summit opens in Moscow

Putin and African Union chair Évariste Ndayishimiye are co-chairing the third Russia-Africa summit, held October 28–29. Attending are 48 delegations and 22 heads of state, including Egypt’s Sisi, Ethiopia’s Abiy, Kenya’s Ruto, Sudan’s Burhan and the juntas of Burkina Faso and Niger. Only 17 heads of state came in 2023. Russia-Africa trade exceeds $28 billion, up from $16.8 billion in 2019. Russian troops or missions now operate in at least nine African countries. Amnesty urged leaders to oppose the “deceptive and coercive” recruitment of Africans to fight in Ukraine, which has already caused a row with Kenya. The rising attendance reflects how the continent’s elites are hedging between imperialist and rival powers.

Khartoum food prices up nearly 60 percent

Figures from Sudan’s Central Bureau of Statistics for September show national prices up 28 percent year on year and food up nearly 37 percent, compared with about 21 percent in August. Food prices rose 17 percent in September alone. In Khartoum State, prices are up nearly 60 percent year on year. The army retook the capital from the RSF last year. Returning residents and wrecked infrastructure have pushed up prices for bread, oil and milk, with bread rising from 300 to 500 pounds a loaf in a week. The World Bank estimates the economy shrank 29 percent in 2023 and 14 percent in 2024. The IOM counts 8.9 million internally displaced people. A tea seller whose soldier husband has been missing for months spends about $4 a day on food and ends most days in debt.

Americas

UN General Assembly condemns Cuba embargo

The General Assembly voted 130–17 with 25 abstentions to condemn the US embargo of Cuba. Germany and Italy, which had voted yes in previous years, abstained. Cuba says Washington threatened countries with tariffs and visa bans. US Ambassador Mike Waltz boasted of “a tripling of countries that have shifted,” naming South Korea, and expelled Cuban diplomat Richard Tur de la Concepción for allegedly “assaulting an American diplomat” during Tuesday’s debate. Foreign Minister Bruno Rodríguez said the US was “once again isolated in its attempt to continue strangling the Cuban people.” The vote comes days after Secretary of State Rubio publicly urged Cubans to spy for the CIA (see WSWS coverage). According to the UN, infant mortality in Cuba has doubled since Trump’s January oil blockade.

Canada signs UAE trade pact

Mark Carney and Abu Dhabi Crown Prince Sheikh Khaled signed a Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement. It will remove or cut tariffs on more than 99 percent of Canadian exports to the UAE, and the two sides also signed preliminary energy and defence agreements. Two-way trade was C$3.5 billion last year, and the UAE pledged up to US$50 billion of investment in Canada in 2025. With talks with Washington collapsed under Trump’s tariffs, Carney has promised to double Canada’s non-US trade within a decade. A Canada-EU summit is under way, at which the EU has floated making Canada its first “associate member.”

Fed holds in split vote as war inflation forces central banks to tighten

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark rate at 3.75–4.00 percent on October 28, in its last meeting before the midterms. It raised rates last month for the first time since July 2023. The vote was 10–2: Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack dissented, both wanting another 25-basis-point hike. The statement said activity is “expanding at a strong pace” and that inflation “remains elevated.” Markets read it as a hawkish hold, and current forecasts point to a quarter-point hike at the December 8–9 meeting. September CPI rose to 3.7 percent from 3.4 percent in August, driven mainly by energy. September payrolls rose only 29,000, with 60,000 in downward revisions, and unemployment is 4.2 percent. Trump has demanded rates of “1% or less.”

On the same day the Bank of Canada raised its rate 25 basis points to 2.5 percent, its first hike in more than three years, even though core inflation is about 2 percent. Governor Tiff Macklem said the move was needed to stop “elevated energy prices” from spreading into broader inflation. He forecast inflation of 3.5 percent at the end of 2026 and growth of just 0.8 percent in Q4. The costs of the Iran war are being passed on to workers through higher rates while wages stagnate. This is the second rate shock of the war.

Anthropic study finds experts follow AI’s lead 86 percent of the time

In a study published Tuesday, Anthropic researchers Amaç Herdagdelen, Hailey Joren and Alex Tamkin gave 50 professional analysts a policy task for a fictional farming cooperative. Without telling them, each analyst’s AI assistant was set to favour one of two defensible options. The analysts chose the AI’s preferred option 86 percent of the time, where chance would give 50. Those who already had a view changed it 60 percent of the time when the AI disagreed. 77 percent used the AI’s arguments in their explanations, but only 15 percent credited it. The authors warn that this deference is “hard to identify, even for the experts themselves.” They say that AI’s built-in “priors” could shape consequential policy, including on AI safety itself. The study used one task and deliberately biased assistants.

The findings matter because frontier models are rapidly being deployed in government, military and corporate decision-making, and the companies that control them decide what priors the models carry. Anthropic is preparing an IPO valued at up to $2 trillion.
