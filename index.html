1. Macron vows “uncompromising” response as France’s cost-of-living revolt brings 2,400 arrests

The nationwide “yellow and youth” mobilization of Saturday, October 17, called jointly by student groups, a union and activists reviving the Yellow Vest movement, became the largest and most violent confrontation with the French state in years. Clashes continued in Paris on Sunday. In a televised address Sunday evening, President Emmanuel Macron blamed “insurrectionist elements from radical far-left groups.” He authorized prefects to work with mayors to ban rallies “if necessary,” and he refused Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez’s offer to resign. Nuñez told France Inter he was “entirely responsible” and admitted a “failure to anticipate.” He said police had “lost control” of the protests.

The protests are against fuel and living costs, overcrowded schools and the austerity of Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu’s government, which is cutting social spending while raising military spending. Lecornu and other officials blamed Jean-Luc Mélenchon’s France Unbowed (LFI) for instigating the unrest. Mélenchon accused Lecornu of “playing with fire.” The union federations have put off the next national strike day until November 5. The WSWS described “a mass mobilisation of youth and ‘Yellow Vests’” that “shakes the French government,” noting that more than 6,000 people, mostly minors, have been arrested since September 28.

Key facts: - Turnout: the Interior Ministry counted 550,000 nationwide; the CGT and organizers claimed 800,000. - Arrests: at least 2,400 over the weekend, with 350 more overnight into Monday. Paris reported 557 in custody, a “record.” - Police injuries: almost 1,000 police and gendarmes injured, seven seriously (Interior Ministry). In Marseille police counted more than 30,000 looters at the peak, deployed 800 officers and fired about 4,000 tear gas grenades. - Maiming: a 17-year-old in Saint-Étienne lost his right hand to a crowd-control grenade. - Damage: about 1,500 businesses damaged (Retail and Trade Federation). Limoges’s 19th-century town hall was burned; the prefect says the fire was set deliberately. - Macron’s address: “Tonight, we all feel anger and shame.” “How can French people do this to France?” He promised “full transparency” on the police response but did not declare a state of emergency or a curfew. - Realignments: Justice Minister Gérald Darmanin said the security services were failing to tackle the “ultra-left.” Marine Le Pen called the government “exhausted and powerless.” US Vice President JD Vance implied on X that immigration caused the unrest. - Political calendar: presidential elections are due in about six months. France 24’s “Spotlight” asked whether the riots are “a gift for Le Pen.”

2. Ukraine fires 1,015 drones at Russia as Trump suspends sanctions and attacks Zelenskyy

Overnight into Sunday, Ukraine carried out its largest drone attack of the war. Russia’s Defense Ministry said it downed 1,015 drones over 15 regions, Crimea and the Black Sea. In St. Petersburg the strikes hit the city’s oil terminal, the Ruchi oil depot, industrial warehouses in Kolpinsky district, an Ozon logistics hub and a VK data center in Leningrad region. At least one person was killed and five injured, and about 20,000 people lost power. The city’s main airport restricted operations. Russian strikes on Kyiv killed one woman and hit a metro station and a 25-storey apartment block. Further deaths were reported in Zaporizhzhia and Volyn, where two were killed and 12 injured. Zelenskyy said Russia launched nearly 2,700 attack drones, 1,700 glide bombs and 66 missiles over the past week.

Trump defended his October 17 suspension of congressionally mandated sanctions on Truth Social. He said Kyiv’s strikes on Russian refineries were “seriously hurting the world diesel markets” and that Zelenskyy “doesn’t want to end this war, and probably never did.” On NBC’s Meet the Press, Zelenskyy said the waiver “will hurt Ukraine.” He said he remains in “virtually non-stop communication” with envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner and that US–Ukrainian intelligence sharing has resumed after a pause. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, speaking in Central Asia, urged Zelenskyy to “focus” on ending the war. In today’s WSWS Perspective, Andre Damon calls the attack an escalation of “the US-NATO war against Russia,” made possible by NATO-supplied weapons and targeting data.

Key facts: - Russia warned European governments on September 30 that threats to its territorial integrity would meet “the entire arsenal of forces and capabilities at its disposal, including nuclear weapons.” On October 12, NATO’s top commander in Europe, General Alexus Grynkewich, threatened to “break” Russia’s “warfighting system.” - Trump’s announced October 11 “energy ceasefire” has collapsed. Zelenskyy says Russia attacked Ukrainian energy, oil and gas targets 76 times in the following week, and that he is “done” with it. - Rubio said Ukraine currently has no access to SpaceX’s military Starshield system and that its future access is “under discussion.” - On October 17, Russian strikes on Kharkiv, Kramatorsk, Volyn and Rivne killed at least eight and cut power to about one million people. Poland scrambled fighter jets. - Zelenskyy has asked EU leaders for an extra $79 billion to fund the war next year.

3. Hormuz attacks reach wartime weekly high as Pentagon readies “mother of all bombs” option

Talks to end the US–Israeli war on Iran, now in its eighth month, are stalled. On October 18 Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi restated Tehran’s seven-day proposal from September: “If they comply with our conditions, the Strait of Hormuz will reopen on the sixth day,” and talks would begin on the seventh. The conditions are an end to the US naval blockade, the lifting of oil sanctions and a ceasefire on all fronts. Tehran says it is still reviewing US “comments” on the plan. AP reports that Trump rejected an Iranian proposal late last month, though indirect contacts continue. Trump said last week that the US would not attack Iran before the November 3 midterms. CBS nonetheless reports that the White House and Pentagon have drawn up plans for a strike with the GBU-43 “mother of all bombs.” The White House did not deny the report.

Key facts: - Shipping war: UKMTO recorded at least 10 vessels attacked in or near the Strait in the past week, the highest weekly total since the war began on February 28, and 23 attacks since October 1, nearly double the average. Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said a supertanker hit a mine and caught fire on October 17. A tug off the UAE was hit by two projectiles on October 18. AP reported that one ship sank in the week to October 16. - Threats: New Defense Minister Mehrdad Akhlaghi warned that if Washington resumes attacks, “they themselves and the region will suffer enormous damage.” An army spokesman said the Strait would reopen if the US “leaves the region… If it does not leave, we will expel it.” - Iraq: Foreign Minister Fuad Hussein estimates $60 billion in lost revenue. Exports fell from about 3.5 million barrels per day before the war to 2.6 million in September, and foreign reserves have dropped by $20 billion. - Rerouting: Syria is preparing an overland oil route to bypass Hormuz. - US posture: US embassies across the region issued a security alert on October 18.

4. Saudi–Houthi war widens as GCC invokes collective defense and Egypt offers troops

The war on Iran has now produced open war between Saudi Arabia and Yemen’s Houthis. On October 18, Gulf Cooperation Council foreign ministers meeting in Manama declared that “an attack on any member affected all six,” invoking their joint defense pact. They demanded that the Houthis be designated a terrorist organization and that the UN arms embargo be tightened. Egyptian President Abdel Fattah al-Sisi said Egypt is ready to defend Saudi Arabia “if requested,” pending parliamentary approval. Iran denied involvement and offered to mediate. Yemen’s Saudi-backed foreign minister, Afrah Alzouba, rejected the offer and said Tehran “should withdraw its hand and its weapons from Yemen.”

Key facts: - Houthi strikes: at least 19 dead in more than a week of attacks on Saudi Arabia, 13 of them in a missile strike on Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport. A projectile injured six in a residential area of Najran. The Houthis have blockaded the Bab el-Mandeb to Saudi-flagged tankers. - Saudi strikes: Houthi media report 12 killed, including three children, on October 17, and at least nine killed and 16 wounded in northern Yemen on October 18. - Losses: two Saudi pilots died when a National Guard helicopter crashed in Najran. Riyadh blamed a “technical malfunction”; the Houthis claim they shot it down. - Humanitarian toll (IOM, WHO): more than 233,000 Yemenis displaced, 121,000 of them in Taiz. Food prices in besieged Taiz are up fivefold. Its 12 hospitals have four days of fuel left and no surgical supplies, amid cholera. - Riyadh: the Kingdom Centre tower told tenants to work remotely “until further notice.” - Frontlines: eight government-aligned soldiers were killed in Lahij on Saturday, and Houthis fired missiles toward Marib.

5. Alberta votes on starting the separation process

Albertans vote today on a 10-question ballot called by Premier Danielle Smith’s United Conservative Party (UCP) government. The independence question asks voters to choose between remaining in Canada and beginning the legal process toward a future “binding” separation referendum. More than 1.3 million people voted in advance polls, and results on the separation question are expected October 20. Polls suggest about four in five Albertans want to remain in Canada, though one report puts support for independence at a record 30 percent. Viewpoint Alberta data suggest about two-thirds of “yes” voters are casting a protest vote “to send Ottawa a message.” Prime Minister Mark Carney called that a “dangerous bluff” while conceding that Albertans’ frustrations “are real.”

Smith says she voted to remain, but she is seeking a strong mandate on the other nine questions as leverage against Ottawa. Five concern immigration, public services and proof of citizenship for voters, including restricting temporary residents’ access to healthcare and education. Four would expand provincial powers, including letting Alberta choose its own judges. The Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation and other First Nations have demanded a halt, citing treaty rights. Separatist organizer Cory Morgan says the movement will continue whatever the result. As Pierre Lajeunesse wrote on the WSWS, the campaign is driven by “a network of Christian nationalists, anti-vaccine activists, oil executives, and Republican operatives” tied to the UCP and the American far right.

Key facts: - A Globe and Mail investigation found that Alberta Prosperity Project (APP) representatives met US State Department officials on April 22, September 29 and December 16, 2025. The White House acknowledged the contacts but said no commitments were made. - APP co-founder Dennis Modry acknowledged proposals offering the US access to Alberta’s freshwater, oil royalties, minerals and land for a railway to Alaska. - US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has praised an “independent” Alberta as an economic partner. - In April, Elections Alberta won an injunction after the separatist Centurion Project improperly obtained the provincial voters list, exposing data on about 2.9 million Albertans.

6. Albania’s student revolt enters second week as Rama refuses to resign

Albania is seeing its largest protest wave since the early 1990s. It began on October 13 with a few hundred students outside the Ministry of Education in Tirana. Demonstrations reached a sixth consecutive day on Sunday and have spread to Kamza, Shkodra, Vlora, Lushnja, Elbasan, Durrës and Korçë. Students are protesting high tuition, unaffordable rents, poor university conditions and the lack of jobs; many young Albanians have emigrated. The movement now demands the resignation of Prime Minister Edi Rama, who has governed since 2013. Rama has ruled out resigning, blamed a “foreign hand” and the opposition, and asked the protesters: “Do they want this, or do they want the EU?” Analyst Mentor Nazarko told Al Jazeera: “His legitimacy has melted away.”

Key facts: - Injuries: the Health Ministry reported 65 injured on Sunday morning, including 37 police and 12 hospitalized, and 272 injured since Tuesday, 169 of them police. - Repression: police have used tear gas, water cannon and batons, and fired rubber bullets for the first time on Saturday. Per the WSWS, more than 167 mostly young demonstrators had been arrested and 388 people charged by Saturday. Some of those arrested are minors. - Escalation: on Wednesday protesters set fire to the Ministries of Education and Justice and broke into the Ministry of Finance. Secondary school students joined on Thursday, and parliament postponed its plenary session. - Origins: the protest wave began in May as the “Flamingo Revolution” against a roughly €1.4 billion luxury tourism project on Sazan island and the Zvërnec coast, whose main investor is Jared Kushner. - Opposition: protesters ejected MPs of Sali Berisha’s Democratic Party and chanted “Rama to prison, Berisha to prison.” The EU delegation has called only for a “proportionate” response.

Trump suspends Russia sanctions he signed into law weeks ago

On Friday, two days before the sanctions were due to take effect, Trump signed an executive order suspending them. They were part of a package he had signed into law the previous month. The law is named after Senator Lindsey Graham, who died in July after returning from Ukraine, and was co-drafted with Democrat Richard Blumenthal. It passed both chambers by large margins. Ebola reaches an eighth case in Kenya as the DRC’s toll passes 4,500

Kenya confirmed its eighth Ebola case on October 19, a 49-year-old man probably infected by the country’s first patient, who had travelled from the DRC and died in a Nairobi hospital on October 5. The outbreak is caused by the Bundibugyo strain. The existing Ervebo vaccine targets the Zaire strain, and there is “no vaccine or specific treatment” for Bundibugyo. The DRC has recorded at least 9,400 cases and more than 4,500 deaths since May, the deadliest outbreak in its history. Ituri province accounts for nearly three-quarters of confirmed cases, and the virus has now reached an eighth province, North Ubangi. Uganda, declared Ebola-free in August, has 20 confirmed cases (15 imported) and two deaths. WHO reports substantial transmission in North Kivu, which has banned entry from Ituri for a week.

An outbreak of this size spreading to Nairobi, a regional air hub, comes as the US has withdrawn from the WHO and gutted USAID. Western media have paid it little attention.

This week in history: October 19–25

  • 25 years ago (October 21–22, 2001): anthrax kills postal workers. Brentwood postal workers Joseph Curseen Jr. and Thomas Morris died after an anthrax letter addressed to Senator Tom Daschle passed through their Washington facility. Authorities knew by October 15 that the letter had also passed through Hamilton Township, New Jersey, but kept both facilities open for a week while politicians and staff were tested and treated immediately. The American Postal Workers Union told carriers not to wear gloves or masks because it was “not the visual image we wish to project.” The WSWS argued at the time that the weapons-grade anthrax pointed to the US military’s own germ-warfare program, not foreign terrorists.
  • 50 years ago (October 25, 1976): Arab League legitimizes Syria’s occupation of Lebanon. A Riyadh summit, ratified in Cairo, turned Syria’s June invasion into a 30,000-strong “Arab Deterrent Force,” almost entirely Syrian troops under Hafez al-Assad. Its purpose was to crush the PLO and Kamal Jumblatt’s Lebanese National Movement, which had nearly defeated the Phalangists. Arafat was forced to accept restrictions and give up heavy weapons. Damascus had assured Henry Kissinger that Israel would not be threatened. Jumblatt was assassinated in March 1977, reportedly by Syrian intelligence, and the war resumed.
  • 75 years ago (October 19, 1951): Egypt declares a state of emergency. The Wafd government of Mustafa el-Nahhas had abrogated the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty. British troops killed five protesters at Ismailia on October 16, and Egyptian workers walked out of British bases. Washington backed London and sought to draw Egypt into an anti-Soviet pact. The crisis led to the burning of Cairo in January 1952 and the Free Officers’ coup that July.
  • 100 years ago (October 25, 1926): Trotsky and Kamenev removed from the Politburo. A joint plenum of the Central Committee and Central Control Commission expelled the leaders of the United Opposition from the Politburo and removed Zinoviev as head of the Comintern. A week earlier the Opposition had made a tactical retreat in the face of organized heckling and intimidation, which Trotsky said replaced “the struggle of ideas” with administrative methods. It marked the bureaucracy’s abandonment of even the pretense of inner-party democracy.
