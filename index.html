1. Trump says Iran talks are “going fine” as Tehran offers a two-day Hormuz reopening plan

Speaking to reporters at his Florida residence on Saturday, Trump said negotiations with Iran were “going fine but you know they’re not very reliable.” He said he is “not in that much of a hurry,” because the US has already “knocked out their nuclear.” He added: “We still have to take care of, I would say one or two sites, but we can do that in 10 minutes. You probably could look at Pickaxe Mountain.” That is a suspected underground complex, and Tehran denies any nuclear work takes place there. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Trump had not decided on new strikes and that Washington remained open to a diplomatic solution.

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told Al Jazeera that Iran and Qatari mediators had reached a new proposal that would cut the time needed to fully reopen the Strait of Hormuz to two days. Last month’s version offered one week, conditional on the US lifting its blockade of Iranian ports, releasing frozen assets and waiving oil sanctions, and Washington rejected it. Araghchi called recent US moves toward “broader dialogue” a “positive step.” He said Iran is not planning to meet US officials, and that whether talks are direct or indirect will be decided “once Washington agrees to Iran’s conditions.” Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has meanwhile ordered the armed forces to answer any new attack without “geographic limits” and to update their target lists.

Key facts: - Strike timing: Washington has reportedly told Gulf allies to prepare for strikes as early as November 4. The White House says it does “not comment on sensitive military plans that may or may not exist” (Times of Israel). - Mediation: Qatari mediators held more than 12 hours of talks in Tehran before Iranian negotiators travelled to Doha. A US official denied a report of a “preliminary breakthrough.” - Tehran’s line: A senior Iranian official said there will be no nuclear talks and no reopening of Hormuz until the US accepts Iran’s demands. - Blockade: CENTCOM says the US blockade has now redirected 144 vessels, up from 138 two weeks ago. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent posted that the US has “taken out” 1.5 billion barrels of Iranian oil, without giving a timeframe, and that Gulf “transit levels are back to pre-conflict levels minus Iranian barrels.” - Qeshm incident: Iranian state media said a drone struck fishing boats near Qeshm Island, wounding two. Two fishermen reported missing were later found. Iran blamed the US, and CENTCOM did not respond. - Regional alarm: Egyptian Foreign Minister Badr Abdelatty said he is “extremely worried” about escalation.

As Kevin Reed noted on the WSWS, the war began on February 28, the day after Trump said publicly that he had made no final decision on using force. Trump’s talk of no “hurry” carries the same weight.

2. Third weekend of revolt: 320,000 march in France as German youth protests end in riots

On Saturday, youth-led protests over living costs, education and militarism took place in France, Germany, Italy and Portugal. The French government counted about 320,000 marchers at roughly 150 demonstrations, down from 410,000 on October 24 and more than 500,000 on October 17. About 70,000 police and gendarmes were deployed. In Paris, the main march of about 70,000 was moved out of the centre onto the Boulevards des Maréchaux after a last-minute court fight. Students led it hand in hand, with CGT stewards at the front. Clashes broke out at Porte de Charenton at dusk, and police answered bottles and fireworks with stun grenades and tear gas. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez called an attack in which a riot officer was hit by a Molotov cocktail an “attempted murder.” The BBC reported more than 500 arrests after clashes in Toulouse, Lyon and Marseille.

In Germany, the youth campaign Gen Z regelt (“Gen Z’s got this”) rallied in more than 30 cities against school underfunding, living costs, spending cuts and the return of military conscription. Evening clashes followed. In Frankfurt, rioters smashed shop windows, including at a gun shop, looted stores and threw Molotov cocktails at emergency services. Hesse Interior Minister Roman Poseck blamed “around 100 clearly identified left-wing extremists.” He added: “We all have those images from France in our minds… France must not be held up as a model in this regard.” In Portugal, police with dogs stormed the rebuilt housing camp in Lisbon’s Rossio Square at 4:30 a.m. on Sunday, a day after thousands marched against the government’s rent bill.

Key facts: - Germany: Authorities counted up to 18,000 protesters in Frankfurt, Munich and Berlin, and German media reported tens of thousands nationwide. Berlin police reported 27 officers injured and up to 56 people detained. Hamburg police arrested 120 people after barricades were set alight, according to the broadcaster NDR. - The movement’s demands: “We are against compulsory military service and we are in favour of a fairer distribution of the burden,” said Gen Z regelt spokesperson Lina Friedrich. She described the aim as fighting “for peace and freedom, rather than for war and coercion.” - France’s controls: Place de la République and Les Halles are closed from 11 p.m. to 6 a.m., and unaccompanied minors face a 9 p.m. curfew around the Champs-Élysées. A Paris court suspended an earlier curfew on minors on Wednesday as a “serious and illegal infringement” of freedom of movement. - A student’s conditions: Corentin Cabreux, 21, told AP that his state-run student residence has “damp stains all along the bottom of my walls… cockroaches everywhere, rats.” - Portugal: Protesters had pitched more than 50 tents. Police also cleared a camp in Porto. The housing group Casa para Viver said 60 people were injured in last week’s clearances. The Bank of Portugal estimates the national housing shortage at 300,000 homes, about the size of Lisbon’s entire housing stock. - Italy: There were clashes between police and demonstrators in Rome and Turin.

Every government involved is giving the movement the same law-and-order treatment, presenting the riots as the work of “extremists” while extending curfews and police powers. The common target of the demands is the redirection of social spending into rearmament.

3. Judge bars Justice Department threats against election workers as DOJ moves to segregate Alaska ballots

Late Friday, US District Judge Trina Thompson in the Northern District of California issued a temporary restraining order. It bars the Justice Department from threatening or coercing election workers to stop processing ballots, and from demanding that local officials segregate or withhold ballots the federal government suspects were cast by noncitizens. The suit was brought by labor unions and civil rights groups. It came the day after the DOJ sent letters to election officials in all 50 states threatening to prosecute and jail anyone who failed to preserve ballot records. Thompson wrote that the department’s “intimidation, threats, and attempts at coercion force election workers daily to choose between acceding to defendants’ unlawful demands or adhering to their duties.”

In Alaska, the DOJ asked a judge on Friday to order state officials to “preserve and segregate all ballots ranking Daniel J. Sullivan,” the retired teacher who shares a name with the Republican incumbent, to investigate possible voter “confusion.” US Attorney Michael Heyman confirmed a criminal investigation and said reporting on the Peltola campaign had brought a “significant surge of law enforcement assets to Alaska.” An FBI agent visited a boat dealer in Petersburg who had donated to Dan J. Sullivan to ask whether he had been misled. The dealer said he had not: “Everybody knows who the incumbent is.” The DOJ also filed proposed questions it wants to compel the Peltola campaign and the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee to answer, as part of its civil suit seeking to block certification if Sullivan loses. Trump says he told the department to file the case.

Key facts: - Flagged California ballots: Two UCLA data scientists found that more than 40% of the ballots flagged by the administration carry names that do not appear on the state’s voter rolls at all (AP). - Peltola: “I am not interested in tricking voters,” she said Thursday. The DSCC denies any involvement. - What is at stake: The ballot-segregation demand would let the administration hold specific ballots in reserve for a post-election challenge in a Senate race expected to help decide control of the chamber.

Patrick Martin noted on the WSWS that the Times report driving the Alaska campaign rests largely on Peltola’s former campaign manager, who has since retracted her comments. He also pointed out that cross-party “spoiler” manoeuvres are routine and not illegal.

4. Starving Oleshky residents evacuated under the first local truce of the Ukraine war

Evacuees from the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky in Kherson region crossed into Ukrainian-held territory on Saturday, at the Belarusian border in Chernihiv region. They travelled under a six-day local ceasefire mediated by US First Lady Melania Trump with help from Pope Leo XIV. It is the longest pause in fighting anywhere on the front since February 2022. Residents described months of famine after fighting and drones cut supply routes. Some said food deliveries stopped in May. “We chopped grass and squash… just to hold on,” Svitlana Yermakova told Reuters. An elderly man in a wheelchair said he had lost 20kg in a year. Zelenskyy thanked the first lady and said “further steps toward de-escalation… are also needed.”

Elsewhere the air war intensified. The Institute for the Study of War counted 134 Russian drones overnight on October 30–31 and 168 drones and loitering munitions during the day on October 31. Kyiv had electricity shortages on Saturday after strikes on energy infrastructure, according to the power company DTEK. Ukraine struck a defence facility in Rostov region and two refineries in Ufa. Trilateral US–Russia–Ukraine talks are planned for Abu Dhabi. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said on October 30 that preparing for them “does not mean the parties will cease fighting.”

Key facts: - The route out: Rather than crossing the Dnipro, evacuees had to take a long detour through occupied territory and Belarus to reach Ukrainian-held land. - Not everyone left: Some Oleshky residents chose to stay behind (Al Jazeera). - The truce is local: It covers only the evacuation zone, while Russian strikes continue elsewhere on the front.

5. India’s opposition rallies in Delhi against the purge of 130 million voters

On Sunday, tens of thousands gathered at Ramlila Maidan in central Delhi for a rally led by Rahul Gandhi and the Congress party. They are demanding the resignation of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar and electoral reform. The trigger is the Special Intensive Revision of the electoral rolls, which has removed 130 million names from draft voter lists. The Election Commission says it is clearing out voters who have died or moved. The opposition calls it “vote chori” (vote theft) to benefit Modi’s BJP. Thousands of police and paramilitaries ringed the grounds. The Gen Z-led group Cockroach Janta Party pledged support.

Key facts: - Investigation: In September, the Indian Express reported that Kumar took key decisions on the revision without the agreement of the other two election commissioners, who objected on more than a dozen occasions. - Mock impeachment: On Saturday, opposition leaders held a symbolic impeachment at Congress president Mallikarjun Kharge’s residence, chaired by former Lok Sabha Speaker Meira Kumar. Kharge accused the CEC of “treason against the nation and the Constitution.” People struck off the rolls attended. - Modi: He said “some people” want to create chaos so that he can be defamed, and accused the opposition of “tearing apart the dignity and boundaries of the constitution.” - Background: Last month Gandhi led tens of thousands from Connaught Place to the Supreme Court. A party official called that march “just the trailer.”

The removal of 130 million names, more than the entire electorate of most countries, is the largest disenfranchisement exercise under way anywhere. The Congress party, which ran the Indian state for most of its history, is leading the protest in order to keep it within parliamentary channels.

6. Tel Aviv rally demands a Jewish–Arab coalition as settlers attack olive harvesters

Thousands rallied in Tel Aviv’s Habima Square on Saturday, urging opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot to deal with the Arab-led parties to remove Netanyahu. Counter-protesters chanted that Israel “should not have a government with so-called terrorists.” Tuesday’s election gave Netanyahu’s bloc 53 seats and Eisenkot’s bloc 48 in the 120-seat Knesset. The three Arab-led parties won 19, enough to give an Eisenkot-led coalition 67. Eisenkot, the former army chief, has so far ruled out such a coalition.

Key facts: - Arab vote: Nearly 840,000 people voted for Ra’am and the Joint List, a 64% increase on 2022, according to the Central Election Committee. - Incitement: Netanyahu accuses the Palestinian-Israeli parties of “stealing” the election with Qatari funding, which Qatar calls disinformation. Right-wing circles have called the record Arab turnout “electoral terrorism” and compared it to October 7 (Al Jazeera). - West Bank: According to WAFA, settlers attacked Palestinians east of Duma, leaving one man with a head wound, and Israeli forces reportedly blocked an ambulance from reaching him. Dozens of settlers forced olive pickers at gunpoint to abandon their harvest in Marah Rabah, south of Bethlehem. The IDF said troops were sent to disperse a crowd. - Gaza: Two girls, reportedly Habiba Zannoun and Aysal Abu Shaiban, died in an overnight fire in a displacement tent in al-Mawasi, Khan Younis. Palestinian media linked the fire to conditions in the camp. - Lebanon: Lebanese media reported an Israeli strike in Kfar Tebnit. The IDF called it a “controlled detonation” during “routine operations” to demolish infrastructure. - Gaza’s medics: Al Jazeera profiles hospital staff forced out of work by trauma. Anaesthesiologist Manar Fayyad, 29, of Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital, quit after his brother was brought into his operating room dead from an air strike.

Whatever coalition emerges, Eisenkot commanded the 2006 assault on Lebanon. A change of government would replace Netanyahu but would not change the occupation or the wars.

7. FBI blames North Korea for the record Binance crypto heist

On Saturday the FBI attributed the theft of “more than $6 billion USD in virtual assets” from Binance, the world’s largest crypto exchange, on or about October 29, to TraderTraitor. The group is believed to be part of North Korea’s Lazarus Group, the outfit accused of the 2014 Sony Pictures hack. It is the largest theft in history. Binance says it has replaced 90% of the stolen tokens, and co-CEO Richard Teng says users “will not bear the financial impact.” Withdrawals began resuming in phases on Sunday.

Key facts: - How it was done: Teng described a “complex attack” using a compromised laptop and malware designed to delete evidence of its activity. - Laundering: The FBI says the hackers are converting stolen assets into Ethereum and will launder them further through decentralised finance and instant-swap services. It urged the private sector to block transactions linked to the addresses involved. - Cover: Binance set aside about $6.34 billion. The money came partly from its $1 billion Secure Asset Fund for Users, partly from its futures insurance fund and partly from its corporate treasury. - Market reaction: When the hack was announced, Bitcoin fell nearly 5%, Ethereum nearly 6% and Binance’s own token BNB nearly 10% (Guardian). - Context: Analysts say crypto theft is a central revenue source for North Korea’s sanctioned weapons programmes. The attack also adds to concern that advancing AI models could undermine cryptographic and IT security.

A single breach removed more than $6 billion from a largely unregulated exchange that sits at the centre of a market with trillions of dollars in nominal value.

Starmer, out of office, says Europe must take charge of its own defence

In his first major speech since leaving Downing Street in July, Keir Starmer told the British Irish Chamber of Commerce in Dublin that “a stronger Europe is vital and the UK must be at the heart of it.” He said Europe “has twice been caught asleep”: first by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, and second “when President Trump made clear, once and for all, that the US will no longer bankroll our European security.” He added: “This is about responding to a permanent change of affairs, not an aberration of the Trump presidency. Almost 140 arrested at Palestine Action protest outside the US embassy

The Metropolitan Police arrested 139 people at a Defend Our Juries protest outside the US embassy in Battersea on Saturday. Participants held signs reading “I oppose genocide, I support Palestine Action.” It was the largest mass action against the group’s proscription since Andy Burnham became prime minister, and a parallel vigil took place at the US consulate in Edinburgh. Commander Adam Slonecki said those attending were “essentially meeting to purposefully commit a crime.” Organisers estimated 400 participants.

  • Defend Our Juries says more than 4,000 people have been arrested for supporting Palestine Action since the July 2025 ban, the large majority of all UK terrorism arrests in that period.
  • “We supply weapons that are being used on Palestinians,” said the Rev. Bill White, 72. Sue Morhall, 79, said she has been arrested “five or six” times.

Spain’s transport minister blames “human error” for the Toledo rail crash

At a press conference on Friday, Transport Minister Óscar Puente set out what he called the “objective facts” of the Toledo high-speed collision. He rejected claims of underinvestment and defended the LZB signalling system, which he said “has already failed 170 times so far this year.” He called that “normal” and around the EU average. He said a controller cleared a stalled train to restart at up to 160km/h while a second train was stuck on the line ahead, even though the coming collision was visible on control-centre screens in Madrid: “They could see what was happening.” He refused to resign. The People’s Party is calling for his resignation, and Ipsos’s Paco Camas notes that this is the second fatal crash this year, after January’s derailment at Adamuz.

  • The government’s case reduces a system that fails about every other day to the mistakes of individual workers. The Sánchez government faces an election on November 29.

UN demands an inquiry after a staff member dies in Houthi detention

The UN said on Saturday that Mohammad el-Shami, a staff member of the UN human rights office (OHCHR) in Yemen, died in Houthi custody more than a month ago and that his family was only recently informed. High Commissioner Volker Türk demanded an “urgent, independent and thorough investigation,” and Secretary-General António Guterres said he was “appalled.” El-Shami was one of more than 60 UN and NGO staff detained in June 2024 as part of an alleged “American-Israeli spy network.”

  • He is the second UN staffer to die in Houthi detention, after a WFP employee in February 2025. Seventy-two UN and NGO staff have been detained in total, including seven more OHCHR personnel who are still held.

Thai lawyer’s cumulative lèse-majesté sentences pass 34 years

On Friday a Bangkok court sentenced human rights lawyer Arnon Nampa, 42, to two years and eight months for a 2020 speech, delivered in a Harry Potter costume, calling for the monarchy to be held accountable. It is one of 14 cases against him under Article 112, and his cumulative sentence now exceeds 34 years, with two verdicts still pending. “If the country was more open, we would be able to talk about these issues freely,” he said.

  • Since the 2020 student protests, 294 people, including 20 minors, have been prosecuted under the law, with sentences of up to 50 years. Most are denied bail through trials that last years.

El Niño emergencies spread across Latin America and the Pacific

Bolivian President Rodrigo Paz declared a nationwide state of emergency on Saturday, warning of “very severe impacts in terms of drought and floods.” Drought now affects 78,000 families in six of nine departments and has killed more than 75,000 cattle. In neighbouring countries, Santiago de Chile recorded its wettest October ever, and wildfires in Peru killed at least 12 people last month. In Papua New Guinea, authorities have declared the El Niño drought a “national crisis” as rivers dry up and village farms fail.

  • The WMO puts the probability that El Niño persists through February 2027 at nearly 100%. Scientists project more than 450,000 excess deaths worldwide, concentrated in the poorest countries.

AI selloff erases about $2 trillion from the Nasdaq-100 in October

Friday’s tech rout capped a week in which the Nasdaq lost about 2%, the S&P 500 0.58% and the Dow 0.85%. It was the third straight weekly decline for both the S&P and the Nasdaq. Reuters calculations cited by the Guardian put the Nasdaq-100’s October losses at more than $2 trillion, slightly more than SpaceX’s entire market value, with nearly $1 trillion of that in the final week. Investors are questioning whether the hyperscalers’ AI capital spending can produce returns. Amazon disclosed a trailing free-cash-flow outflow of about $42 billion and guided to about $220 billion in capex, even as AWS grew 39% year on year. Meta reported negative free cash flow for the first time since 2012.

Stress is now showing in the debt-financed “neocloud” data-centre firms that lease AI computing power to OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta. CoreWeave, which has accumulated $35.1 billion in debt secured on customer contracts and chips, has lost a quarter of its value in a month. Nebius, IREN and Applied Digital have fallen as far or further, and their bond yields are climbing. Gartner predicts that 80% of neocloud firms will “cease independent operations” by 2030. Treasury Secretary Bessent called them “a small component of the overall ecosystem.”

Chevron earned $9.9 billion on revenue up 36% to $67.8 billion, and now books one month of its Venezuelan production. Both stocks briefly touched record highs, and Exxon is up 64% over 12 months. AAA puts average gasoline at $4.20 a gallon, about $1.20 higher than a year ago, and diesel at $6.32, up 71%. At a Georgia rally on October 24, Trump said higher gas prices had been a blessing for Americans.

Yerevan nature summit agrees a course correction but no money

Delegates from nearly 200 countries meeting in Yerevan, Armenia, agreed the core of a new action plan early on Saturday, hours after the talks were due to end. The 2022 Global Biodiversity Framework set 24 targets, including protecting 30% of land and sea by 2030, but the UN says the world is on track to meet just one. Countries acknowledged they are falling behind, but the main obstacle remains finance. Governments pledged $30 billion a year by 2030 and $170 billion from private sources, and most of that has not materialised. “We are still seeing an appalling loss, threatening the collapse of ecosystems globally,” said Craig Bennett of The Wildlife Trusts. Countries have placed 252 million hectares of degraded land under protection.

Ebola: US travel attestation begins Tuesday as Kenya records a second death

Congo’s health ministry reports more than 10,300 confirmed cases, with a case fatality rate of 48.6% and eight of 26 provinces affected. The outbreak is driven by the Bundibugyo species, for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment, and its epicentre is in conflict-torn Ituri. The UN says it is “taking lives at a much faster rate” than earlier outbreaks. Kenya has reported a second death. From November 3, international travellers to the US must attest that they have not recently been in Congo.

Arts and culture

Patrick Stewart, 1940–2026

Patrick Stewart died on Wednesday at his home in Los Angeles, aged 86, after what his spokesperson called “a brief and poignant journey with Alzheimer’s disease and Lewy body dementia.” Tributes continued through the weekend. Stewart grew up in working-class Mirfield, West Yorkshire, and made his name at the Royal Shakespeare Company before he was cast at 47 as Captain Jean-Luc Picard in Star Trek: The Next Generation (1987–94). He went on to four feature films, Star Trek: Picard (2020–23) and Professor Charles Xavier in the X-Men films. Ian McKellen called him “the most respected and beloved actor of his generation on either side of the Atlantic.” He wrote that Stewart was “defined by being born and bred in Yorkshire… Yorkshiremen have no truck with half-truths or avoidance of big issues.” Ben Kingsley recalled watching him rehearse Grumio on his first day at the RSC.

Stewart’s significance went beyond his voice and presence. Picard was a humane, reasoning figure in a series that imagined a future without money or want, in which diplomacy came before force. That vision is far from Hollywood’s current output of franchise cynicism and militarised superheroes. The Guardian’s appreciation argues that Stewart “could make anyone feel hopeful for humanity.” He also spoke publicly about the domestic violence he witnessed as a child and campaigned against it for many years.
