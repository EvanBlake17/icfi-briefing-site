1. US orders all citizens out of Iran, warning hostilities could escalate fast

On Wednesday the US Virtual Embassy in Iran issued a security alert: “Do not travel to Iran for any reason. United States citizens should leave Iran immediately.” It warned that Iran, the Houthis and other Tehran-aligned groups may target US citizens, companies and institutions across the region. It told Americans to prepare for flight cancellations, airspace closures and sudden changes at land borders. Washington has had no diplomatic relations with Tehran since 1980, and Switzerland acts as its protecting power.

The alert came days after Iran struck US assets in Bahrain and Kuwait. Bahrain’s Interior Ministry said Saturday’s attacks wounded two people and set fire to a civilian industrial facility. Euronews headlines from the past 72 hours report that Iran vowed to use “every means” against a US blockade of Hormuz and ordered the strait closed, saying the closure would “deepen day by day.” Qatar, which is trying to revive peace talks, said it “will not accept” attacks, amid a diplomatic row that began when Iran protested a Qatari-German joint statement. Only the headlines were available, not the articles.

Key facts: - Duration: The war began on February 28 (Ofgem/Euronews). Since then, tanker traffic through Hormuz has collapsed and the Dutch TTF gas benchmark has risen about 190 percent, from roughly €32 to €92 per MWh. - Cost: According to figures in the WSWS’s Iowa coverage, the Pentagon told Congress the war cost $47.1 billion through October 16, with munitions accounting for more than 60 percent. It has requested $67 billion in supplemental funding. - Political lineup: On November 11, newly elected Democratic veterans attacked Hegseth, not the war itself. Rep. Chrissy Houlahan said his leadership had “subjected all of us to the consequences of this unlawful war.” As Patrick Martin notes on the WSWS, these Democrats are demanding the war be run better, not ended.

2. Washington proposes Section 301 tariffs of up to 40 percent on 14 economies

The US Trade Representative (USTR) ran an eight-month Section 301 investigation into “excess capacity” in 16 trading partners. It found the practices of 14 of them “unreasonable” and burdensome to US commerce. The 14 are China, the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland and Thailand. Findings on Mexico and Norway are still pending. The new duties would partly replace the revenue lost when the Supreme Court ruled in February that Trump’s emergency-powers tariffs were unlawful. The administration cites a 2025 US goods trade deficit of $1.24 trillion.

The proposed EU tariff is 15 percent on top of most-favoured-nation duties, with an option to raise it to 20 percent if talks and existing commitments prove “unsatisfactory.” Goods already covered by Section 232 tariffs are exempt: cars and parts, steel, aluminium, patented medicines and semiconductors. Many Chinese goods would face up to 20 percent, minus existing forced-labour 301 duties. If talks with Beijing fail, the rate on all Chinese goods could reach 40 percent. A separate Section 301 action against Brazil proposes a 10 percent tariff on finished aircraft, which targets Embraer, along with higher out-of-quota sugar duties.

Key facts: - Timeline: Comments are due January 5 and hearings begin January 25. No effective date has been set. The US-China truce expires January 10, and Trump plans to host Xi at the G20 summit in Florida next month. - EU: Spokesperson Olof Gill: “We strongly reject the premise that Europe is a source of structural overcapacity. To the contrary, Europe is among those most exposed to overcapacity.” - China: Foreign ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun: “Tariff wars and trade wars are not in anyone’s interest.” - Trade war and war production: Writing on the WSWS, Jerry White cites a Wall Street Journal report that officials are in talks with GM, Ford and Magna about supplying motors to drone makers. The Pentagon estimates the drone market could reach $20–30 billion a year. As White puts it, the tariffs and the industrial mobilization are “a shared drive to confront China.”

3. AI rout deepens as the Nasdaq swings 4.6 percent and Anthropic briefly falls below $1 trillion

The sell-off that began Tuesday turned into a near-crash during Wednesday’s session. At its low the Nasdaq was down 4.6 percent, the S&P 500 3.2 percent and the Dow more than 1,100 points, before a late recovery. The Nasdaq closed down 0.62 percent at 23,585.80, and the S&P 500 down 0.25 percent at 7,182.78. Most S&P 500 stocks rose, but the AI leaders fell. Nvidia lost another 3.91 percent to $149.30, its second straight decline. That has erased about two-fifths of Monday’s gain after Jensen Huang was reinstated. Anthropic, the developer of Claude, fell intraday to $73.85, taking its valuation below $1 trillion. It closed at $82.79, just under its $83 IPO price.

Japan’s Nikkei 225 lost 2.78 percent to 57,011.62 on losses in SoftBank, Advantest and Tokyo Electron. The Shanghai Composite dropped 2.03 percent, its largest one-day fall in three months, while the ChiNext fell 4.19 percent and the STAR 50 fell 5.12 percent. On Thursday, the Nikkei, KOSPI, Taiex and Hang Seng each recovered more than 1 percent. Kioxia and SoftBank posted double-digit gains.

4. EU leaders open budget summit as a €45 billion Ukraine loan collides with spending cuts

EU heads of government meet in Brussels today for two days of talks, aiming to set up a final deal in December on the 2028–2034 multiannual budget, worth about €2 trillion. European Council President António Costa’s compromise cuts €204 billion from the Commission’s original proposal and includes a digital services tax, which France has long sought. The Ukraine question sits on top of the budget fight. Russia’s attacks have left a €70 billion gap in Kyiv’s 2027 budget. The Commission proposes a €45 billion loan backed by joint EU debt, split into €20 billion for the budget and €25 billion for the military, on top of an existing €90 billion credit line. The UK, Norway, Canada and Japan are expected to cover the rest.

On Wednesday, MEPs condemned a proposed 10 percent cut to the budget and warned Costa that Parliament would not approve a deal without more money. The “frugal” states (Germany, Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands and Sweden) want deeper cuts to agriculture and cohesion funds so the money can go to defence, climate and technology. The 17 “Friends of Cohesion” from Southern and Eastern Europe say Costa’s cuts already go too far.

Key facts: - Merz threatens to veto the budget unless all 27 states agree on Ukraine support. Slovakia’s Fico calls the linkage a “textbook case of blackmail.” Slovakia, the Czech Republic, Hungary and Bulgaria do not want to join the loan. - France calls the compromise “highly disappointing.” Marine Le Pen, frontrunner for the French presidency, has vowed to cap France’s contribution at €5 billion a year and overturn the deal. - Hungarian PM Péter Magyar said the talks were “playing out a bazaar scene reminiscent of Cairo, Tunis, or Marrakesh.” - Public opinion: A poll finds 53 percent of Germans oppose continued military aid to Kyiv. - Class content: The core dispute is how far to cut farm and regional development spending to pay for rearmament and the war in Ukraine.

5. Army detonates its entire lead azide stockpile in Iowa and declares success before any soil test

At 9 a.m. Central Time on Wednesday, the Army blew up the entire US stockpile of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown: 68 barrels holding nearly 10,000 pounds (about five tonnes) of degraded primary explosive. Two simultaneous blasts sent twin white plumes above the treeline, and the wind carried the dust southwest. Officials said barriers, ballistic gels and foams were used to contain it. An hour before the blast, FEMA sent everyone within 20 miles an alert reading “EVACUATION IS NO LONGER SAFE OR ADVISED!”

Within minutes, Trump posted that “the Army’s mitigation efforts have far exceeded expectations.” About an hour after the blast, acting Army Secretary Adam Telle called it a “HUGE success,” and the EPA said its real-time monitors showed “ZERO detection of airborne lead.” The first official briefing came two hours after the blast. It lasted five minutes and took no questions. No soil sample had been analyzed at that point.

Key facts: - Deposition forecast: The Army’s final forecast has lead settling above the EPA floor-dust standard across about 148 square miles, and at 20 times that level across about 27 square miles. The airborne plume could reach nearly 100 miles into northern Missouri. The EPA permit requires soil and wipe sampling only in the smaller zone, and results will take three to five weeks. - “Hose it off”: Army spokesperson Justine Barati told residents worried about lead on their property they could simply hose it off. University of Iowa professor emeritus Peter Thorne said hosing “will indeed move (lead) to the soil or into surface water.” Col. Aaron Teller acknowledged that in an unmitigated 1977 detonation in Colorado, about a quarter of the lead went airborne. - The road not taken: The Army’s contractor, MuniRem Environmental, says a joint panel of Army specialists and EPA representatives recommended chemical neutralization in March. On May 8, then-Army Secretary Dan Driscoll chose detonation instead. - The war motive: The Army’s own Environmental Assessment ranked disposal options by how quickly the plant could “safely resume its essential medium and large-caliber munitions production mission.” The plant is the Army’s main producer of 155 mm shells, and a $635 million expansion is under construction. - Regulatory backdrop: Last year the Trump administration tripled the soil-lead level that triggers urgent removal at Superfund sites, to 600 parts per million. The Iowa plant is itself a Superfund site. - WSWS demands (Benjamin Mateus): Publication of all raw monitoring data, sampling across the full deposition zone, free blood-lead testing now for every downwind child and pregnant woman, and full compensation for farmers.

6. Kim Jong Un tours uranium plants and orders rapid deployment of battlefield nuclear weapons

KCNA reported on Thursday that Kim Jong Un spent Wednesday touring two nuclear-material production plants, one newly opened and one being expanded. He also visited a base that produces nuclear-powered equipment and a nuclear research institute. The locations were not disclosed. Photos showed rows of what appear to be centrifuges for weapons-grade uranium, and halls displaying what are believed to be warheads. Kim called North Korea’s nuclear status and its expansion “in quality and quantity” an “irreversible reality.” He urged rapid deployment of low-yield battlefield nuclear weapons, citing danger on the border with South Korea.

Kim justified the expansion by accusing Washington of encouraging allies to acquire nuclear-powered submarines and pledging to share US nuclear weapons with them. That is a clear reference to AUKUS and to South Korea’s submarine program. Trump raised resuming talks during last week’s APEC meeting in China but got no reply. He told the New York Post it was “not really the right time.”

Key facts: - IAEA: Director General Rafael Grossi says North Korea is expanding its original Yongbyon enrichment facility, which he called a “serious concern.” - Seoul: The Defense Ministry says it takes the battlefield-weapons remarks “seriously.” The Joint Chiefs declined to analyze the facilities. - Kim’s terms: Kim says he could return to talks only if Washington drops its “hostile policy” and accepts North Korea as a nuclear state. He has moved closer to Moscow and Beijing since talks collapsed in 2019.

Europe

Russia hits Ukraine’s gas and power plants as Kyiv strikes three refineries and sinks a corvette

Overnight into Wednesday, Russia launched 112 Shahed, Gerbera and decoy drones, and Ukraine’s air force says it downed 88. At least seven people were killed across six regions, including two in Sumy region aged 76 and 69, and a 17-year-old in Odesa region. A strike on a minibus in Kharkiv region killed one and injured seven. A Naftogaz gas production facility in Poltava region caught fire and shut down. Acting Naftogaz head Serhiy Fedorenko said Russia is deliberately targeting gas production “to deprive Ukrainians of heat in winter.” DTEK said one of its thermal plants stopped generating after its third large-scale strike this month.

Ukraine hit Rosneft’s Syzran and Kuibyshev refineries in Samara region, which have a combined capacity of about 17 million tonnes a year. It also hit the Novoshakhtinsk refinery in Rostov region, which suspended operations after a six-hour drone and missile attack. Kyiv says it struck Savasleyka airbase in Nizhny Novgorod region, the launch base for Kinzhal-carrying MiG-31s, damaging three MiG-31s and two Su-34s. Zelensky confirmed that the Russian corvette Merkury was sunk in the Libyan port of Benghazi. On Tuesday, Putin threatened retaliation and complained that Kyiv had ignored Trump’s call to stop hitting refineries.

Magyar and Zelensky open a “new chapter” in Uzhhorod

On Tuesday, Hungarian Prime Minister Péter Magyar met Zelensky under tight security in Transcarpathia, home to Ukraine’s Hungarian minority. Both declared that “a new chapter had begun” after 16 years of hostility under Orbán. Hungary brought 1 billion forints in aid, including patient monitors, medicines and generators for the winter. On Wednesday, the European People’s Party (EPP) joined the ECR, Patriots for Europe and Europe of Sovereign Nations to pass “Omnibus VIII,” the Commission’s plan to speed up environmental impact assessments. UK energy price cap jumps 26 percent on the Iran war

Ofgem will raise the price cap by 26 percent in January. The typical household bill rises by about £443 to £2,166 a year, which hits the roughly 20 million households on standard variable tariffs immediately. Ofgem’s Neil Kenward said “conflict in the Middle East continues to drive energy costs higher.” British electricity will cost about €48.40 per 100 kWh, roughly 70 percent above the EU average of €28.30 and well above Ireland, the dearest EU state at €38.70. UK inflation reached a 12-month high of 3.6 percent in October, and this rise will push it higher. Burnham’s October budget gave a one-off £100 payment, which the rise more than cancels. The UK wants to join the EU internal electricity market, but Germany, Belgium, the Netherlands and two other states want London to pay into cohesion funds first. Energy minister Martin McCluskey says no sum has been agreed.

Unannounced strike by Paris Eurostar staff halts Channel trains

Paris-based Eurostar staff, including train drivers and managers, walked out on Wednesday without advance notice. Eurostar says it was only told overnight. At least six trains were cancelled, one of them from Brussels, and five more were delayed. One Gare du Nord passenger was first told the delay was due to “an unexpected number of travellers,” then later received an email blaming “unannounced strike action by some of our staff.” Eurostar warned of knock-on disruption on other routes and said “discussions with unions are ongoing.” No union or grievance has been publicly identified. The bypassing of the usual notice period suggests the action may have started outside official union channels.

Spain heads to Sunday’s snap election with real wages below their 2009 peak

Spain’s economy is forecast to grow 2.6 percent this year, against 1.2 percent for the eurozone. But the average real wage in 2025 was €35,121, about 1 percent below its 2009 peak of €35,533, according to OECD data. Between 2015 and 2025, food prices rose 45 percent, real house prices 43 percent (against a 25 percent EU average) and rents 43 percent. The Bank of Spain estimates a shortage of 750,000 homes. One renter in four spends more than 40 percent of income on housing. About two-thirds of 18- to 34-year-olds live with their parents, and 47.3 percent of 26- to 34-year-olds in that group say they cannot afford to leave. “Housing has gone, well, bananas,” said Ángel Talavera of Oxford Economics. Sánchez is campaigning on the growth figures, while Feijóo’s Popular Party is promising income-tax cuts.

Experts blame a bad weld for Spain’s Adamuz rail disaster

On January 18, an Iryo high-speed train derailed at Adamuz, Córdoba, and collided with a Renfe Alvia, killing 46 people. Three court-appointed engineers dated their preliminary report November 20. It names a poorly executed weld and ground subsidence as the likely causes. The weld joined a 1989 rail to a 2023 rail during upgrade works on the Madrid–Seville line in 2025. A 6.4 mm track irregularity was detected in October 2025, and the experts found no evidence of the closer monitoring it called for. They also found inconsistencies in the welding records. Ten months after the crash, key laboratory tests are still incomplete. The state lab CEDEX says it lacks the means to run some of them.

Diesel tanker sinks off Ibiza

The tanker Med Emre, carrying about 7,000 tonnes of diesel, sank 25 to 30 nautical miles north of Ibiza at about 1:20 a.m. Thursday after a breach in its hull. Seventeen crew were rescued, and one later died at Can Misses Hospital. Spain’s National Maritime Pollution Plan is active at level 2. The extent of any spill is not yet known.

Abrego Garcia leaves for Spain rather than face deportation to Liberia

Kilmar Abrego Garcia arrived in Madrid on Wednesday morning with his wife, Jennifer Vasquez Sura, after 20 months of litigation. He is the Maryland sheet-metal apprentice whom the administration illegally deported to El Salvador’s CECOT prison in March 2025. He boarded at Dulles on Tuesday night only after federal authorities removed his ankle GPS monitor under court order. Judge Paula Xinis’s November 20 order, unsealed Wednesday, barred officials from asking “any air carrier, common carrier, or person to detain, divert, or delay” him. It also directed that his departure be recorded as execution of his removal order. For over a year he had offered to go to Costa Rica, which agreed to protect him. The administration insisted on Liberia instead. “The government did not decide where my story continues,” Abrego Garcia said from Spain. His attorney, Simon Sandoval-Moshenberg, called the departure “temporary.” He said the family expects “the next administration” to bring him home, though a return requires a waiver.

Nordic study of 3.6 million children finds ADHD drugs in pregnancy safe for development

A registry study in The BMJ followed more than 3.6 million children born in Sweden, Norway and Denmark between 2000 and 2021. About 7,600 were exposed to ADHD medication in the womb, nearly 2,400 of them in the second or third trimester. Prenatal exposure to amphetamines, methylphenidate or atomoxetine was not linked to higher rates of ADHD, autism, intellectual disability, epilepsy or learning disorders. A crude association disappeared once the authors adjusted for maternal education, smoking and maternal ADHD. The authors call the results “largely reassuring” for patients deciding whether to stop treatment. Limitations include missing data on the severity of the mother’s ADHD and the use of filled prescriptions as a proxy for doses taken. The study is a strong example of what national health registries make possible, a capacity the US lacks and is now actively dismantling.
