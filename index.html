1. Cost-of-living revolt spreads across Europe as France arrests 1,200

Saturday’s national day of action in France was the largest mobilization of the autumn. Students, trade unions and the revived Yellow Vest movement marched together against the cost of living, decaying schools and public services. The Interior Ministry counted more than 550,000 people, the CGT 500,000 and organizers about 800,000. Most marches were peaceful, but fighting, looting and arson broke out at their edges in a dozen cities. A fire set by masked protesters largely destroyed the town hall in Limoges, which is more than 140 years old. Town halls in Marseille and Lille were broken into, and Lyon’s tourist office was burned. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said 1,200 people were arrested and 150 police officers were injured: “It’s a miracle the toll wasn’t higher.” Macron promised that those responsible would be “prosecuted and punished.”

Similar protests, called separately, took place across Europe on the same day. In Lisbon, tens of thousands answered a call by the CGTP union federation for higher wages and pensions and against rising housing and fuel costs. Portuguese house prices have more than doubled since 2017. Minister Carlos Abreu Amorim said the government “will not yield to shouts from the street.” In Milan, a march against war and the cost of living ended in clashes in the Piazza del Duomo, and about 50 people, many of them minors, were detained. Belgian police used tear gas and water cannon in Brussels and Liège after weeks of student protests against university fees. Alberto Alemanno of HEC Paris told Al Jazeera that mobilizations in Greece, Germany and Austria are “in early stages” and expected to grow this week.

Key facts: - Police violence: A 17-year-old in Saint-Étienne lost a hand to a police stun grenade, according to France 24. Riot police fired tear gas at crowds gathering in Paris’s Place de la République before the authorized march began. Demonstrator Jules Petrichor, 47, said police started gassing protesters “for no clear reason,” which “worsened things.” - Looting: Shops were looted in Paris, Marseille, Nice, Nantes, Bordeaux, Limoges, Lille, Toulouse and Strasbourg, including a Hermès boutique in Marseille and an Apple store in Lille. Paris Mayor Emmanuel Grégoire said whole streets “have been devastated.” - Grievances: Protesters linked the cost of living to the Middle East war and inflation. Other targets were school conditions, inequality and “the wealth of the ultra-rich.” Chants of “Macron, resign” were widespread. - Political responses: Marine Le Pen said the movement had been “hijacked by the far left.” Mélenchon said the day was peaceful overall and accused TV news of exaggerating the violence. Vance posted video of the burning Limoges town hall and wrote that “Western liberals imported the third world into France.” Meloni blamed “hooligans who exploit demonstrations to sow chaos.” - Media: A Euronews crew was attacked by masked people while filming looters in Paris, and an Al Jazeera reporter was struck on the head in Rennes.

The ruling class in every European capital is answering a social movement driven by war-fueled inflation and austerity with the language of law and order. In Vance’s case, the answer is open racism.

2. Settlers beat a French mayor and attack European diplomats in the West Bank

On Saturday, a day after settlers beat Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine, masked settlers attacked a convoy of European diplomats in Taybeh, a Christian village northeast of Ramallah. The convoy included senior French and Belgian representatives based in Jerusalem and Polish diplomats. Bernard Baudoux, 78, mayor of Aulnoye-Aymeries in northern France, had come to sign a twinning agreement and join the olive harvest. The attackers shouted insults about France, kicked him and hit him with iron bars. Local pastor Bashar Fawadleh said another person needed stitches for a head wound and a woman in her 70s was also beaten. “The whole world needs to know what’s happening,” Baudoux said, adding that he believed the Israeli government tolerates such attacks.

Netanyahu called the coverage a “smear” campaign against settlers. He said the diplomats should have coordinated with the army and pointed out that Baudoux is a communist who supports the Palestinian cause. Ynet reports that no arrests have been made and that police accuse the diplomats of not cooperating. Gadi Eisenkot, Netanyahu’s main rival in the October 27 election, called the violence “Jewish terrorism, under the auspices of the Israeli government.”

Key facts: - Scale: The Israeli monitoring group Yesh Din has recorded at least 170 settler pogroms against Palestinians since September 27. - Washington: Secretary of State Rubio said the US was “very disturbed” and had asked Israel to “crack down.” He expects a response through the Jerusalem embassy by today. Ambassador Mike Huckabee called the attack on Levine “a despicable act of terror carried out by masked cowards,” though he did not name the attackers as settlers. The State Department condemned “criminal violence by any party.” - Europe: French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot called the attack “appalling” and said “they must urgently halt this criminal violence.” Belgium called for an end to “impunity for violent settlers,” and Ynet reports that it is now pressing for EU sanctions. - Levine: Her daughter told AP that Levine had surgery on a nearly severed finger and also has a broken rib and several broken vertebrae. Levine called Netanyahu’s suggestion that settlers had been trying to help her up “such a lie and the biggest insult.”

Washington’s sudden concern follows a $1 billion Apache gunship sale to Israel this week and comes 10 days before an Israeli election. It is aimed at the international damage from the attacks, not at the occupation that produces them.

3. Two more tankers hit mines as Egypt offers to defend Saudi Arabia

Iran’s Revolutionary Guards said on Saturday that two more tankers, each carrying about 2 million barrels of crude, caught fire after hitting naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz. IRNA said the ships had switched off their positioning and radar systems and taken a route Tehran had not authorized. According to the IRGC, one was trying to cross “with US support” and the second was left immobilized. IRGC naval commander Rear Admiral Ali Azmaei warned that ships that disable their navigation systems will be treated as “hostile.” Separately, Al Jazeera reports fires on three tankers in the strait and the Sea of Oman. Britain’s maritime agency says a tugboat was hit by two projectiles, and gunmen fired on an LNG tanker off Yemen. Iran’s army says two more tankers caught fire on Thursday and Friday in its “prohibited zone” in the Gulf of Oman.

The war is drawing in more states. Egyptian President Sisi said on Saturday that he would “carry out any measure to protect” Saudi Arabia if asked, and that it was “inconceivable” for Cairo to stand by. “We are speaking only about defence,” he added. Egypt’s parliament meets in emergency session today at his request. Trump told reporters that “Israel’s been very helpful to Saudi Arabia” and said Saudi-Israeli normalization may now be possible “maybe more than ever.” He also told Newsmax that “I wouldn’t use nuclear” against Iran and that he expects a deal to end the war after the November 3 midterms.

Key facts: - Blockade: CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper said the US naval blockade has stopped about $25 billion in Iranian oil exports and helped freeze about $350 million in Iranian cryptocurrency. US forces escorted 400 merchant ships through the strait last week. Cooper says more than 20 civilian sailors have been killed in Iranian attacks since February. He also said Iran-aligned militias are withholding fighters’ pay to send money to the IRGC, a sign of “greater desperation.” - Yemen front: The Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed four drones over Riyadh, the Eastern Province and the south, and that two of its soldiers were killed in a helicopter crash in Najran. Coalition airstrikes are trying to break the Houthi siege of Taiz. A missile strike near the presidential palace in Aden killed at least five people on Thursday. The Houthis say 124 people have died in their territory since fighting resumed, including three children in Dhamar. - Iran’s role: The Wall Street Journal reports that Iran is providing the Houthis with intelligence, training and targeting help. Former IDF Iran analyst Danny Citrinowicz called it “strategic cooperation,” not a client relationship. Tehran denies any role and offers to mediate. - Civilian life: Several embassies have told their citizens to avoid Saudi airports. Riyadh has extended remote schooling in the capital and Diriyah, and Britain now advises against all but essential travel to Saudi Arabia.

4. Rubio calls the Russia sanctions waiver a 60-day pause as Republicans break ranks

Secretary of State Rubio said on Saturday that Trump’s waiver of the Graham Act sanctions and tariffs on Russia is a 60-day pause. He said the president will use that time to “analyze” the global economic impact of measures that hit several countries besides Russia. Rubio pointed to the Iran war and the Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia as drivers of global energy prices, saying the conflicts “are starting to crash into each other.” He argued that Putin would have sold the diesel covered by the US-Russian deal on the world market anyway: “the only question was how much the US would get.” Late Saturday, Trump called the waiver “a very good decision” and “good for the world.”

Key facts: - Republican dissent: Senator Thom Tillis, who leaves office in January, called the waiver “beyond irresponsible.” He said that if Trump wants lower energy prices he should solve the Iran problem “not by doing a solid for Vladimir Putin.” Senator Ted Cruz called it “a mistake.” - Kyiv: Zelensky said the waiver “rewards” Putin and that he fears more sanctions will be lifted. - India: India, one of the largest buyers of Russian oil, had warned that the Graham Act tariffs of up to 100 percent would damage relations. Rubio arrives in Mumbai today, skipping New Delhi (see International). - Front line: Video posted overnight showed multiple explosions in St Petersburg during what appeared to be a Ukrainian drone swarm. Romanian F-16s shot down two drones over the Black Sea, and President Nicușor Dan called for more NATO oversight there. Russian missiles started factory fires in Odesa and Zaporizhzhia.

Washington is trading sanctions relief for diesel supplies to contain an energy shock caused by its own war on Iran, while the European powers push to continue the war in Ukraine. The deepening rift shows that inter-imperialist conflict is sharpening.

5. Kenya’s Ebola cases rise to seven as Congo’s toll passes 4,400

Kenya confirmed four new cases of Bundibugyo virus disease on Saturday, bringing its total to seven, including the first patient, who died. The day before, Kenya had confirmed its third case. The government says the risk to the public remains low, and Africa CDC has said it backs Nairobi’s response. Kenya’s first case was imported from Congo, so the jump to seven suggests local transmission chains are now established in East Africa’s main transport hub.

Key facts: - Congo: The US CDC counts 9,235 cases and 4,444 deaths since the outbreak was detected in May. Save the Children says about 961 children have died, roughly seven a day. At least 10 students have died, and 25 schoolchildren in Ituri have confirmed cases. - Spread: The outbreak has reached eight provinces: Ituri, North Kivu, South Kivu, Tshopo, Haut-Uele, Bas-Uele, South Ubangi and North Ubangi. Officials told Reuters about North Ubangi, but Kinshasa has not yet announced it. - Vaccines: No vaccine or treatment is approved for the Bundibugyo strain. A phase 1 trial of an Oxford candidate, which uses the same platform as the Oxford/AstraZeneca COVID vaccine, gave its first dose in Masaka, Uganda, last week and aims to enroll 110 people by November. German researchers found that Merck’s Ervebo, licensed against the Zaire strain, produces some cross-reactive T-cell response, but whether it protects is unknown.

The epidemic is spreading through a war zone. On the same day, former President Joseph Kabila joined an armed rebellion based in Goma (see International).

Kabila joins an armed rebellion against Tshisekedi in eastern Congo

Former President Joseph Kabila now leads a new political-military coalition, the Front pour la Refondation de la Nation (FRN), announced in Goma. It replaces the AFC-M23 alliance, which has held Goma since January 2025. The FRN accuses President Félix Tshisekedi of tyranny and calls for resistance “by all means,” including force. A Congolese military court sentenced Kabila to death in absentia in 2025 for treason and war crimes linked to M23.

  • Government spokesman Patrick Muyaya said Kabila has “nothing to offer but chaos.” Former Assembly Speaker Vital Kamerhe told Kabila he “cannot erase” his own record by blaming his successor.

Tunisia’s largest protest against Saied in years

Thousands marched in Tunis on Saturday against President Kais Saied, who has ruled by decree since 2021. On October 6, police stopped a “Dignity March” of unemployed graduates walking about 300 km from Menzel Bouzaiane at Borj Cedria, short of the capital.

  • Unemployment (INS, Q2 2026): 14.9 percent overall, 26.6 percent for graduates, 35.6 percent for women graduates and 35.4 percent for 15- to 24-year-olds.
  • Growth: 2.7 percent in 2025 and 2.4 percent in the first half of 2026. The World Bank projects 2.3 percent for 2026.

Omar’s lawyers call the raid on her home unlawful as Republicans call for her deportation

On Friday, Rep. Ilhan Omar’s lawyers, Amy Jeffress and Joshua Matz, wrote to federal prosecutors demanding the return of the property seized in the October 12 raid on her Washington home. “The political motivations behind this pre-election investigation and search are clear as day,” they wrote. “This search was neither justified nor lawful.” They say agents gave no advance copy of the warrant and did not contact the House Office of General Counsel, contrary to a magistrate judge’s instructions meant to protect a sitting member’s legislative materials. Justice Department threatens California election workers with prosecution

One in nine infected health workers has persistent long COVID symptoms

A study in the American Journal of Industrial Medicine followed 1,496 health workers in 17 states who had symptomatic COVID-19 between August 2022 and May 2024. It found that 11.3 percent reported at least one moderate or severe symptom at both three and six months, most often fatigue. At six months alone, 27.5 percent reported moderate or severe symptoms. The odds were three times higher for women than for men (aOR 3.05), 3.5 times higher for those 65 and over compared with 18- to 29-year-olds, and 1.8 times higher after an infection in the previous year. Physicians, nurses and therapists had lower odds than support staff. The authors call an association with recent vaccination “unexpected” and likely due to bias. Arts and culture

Deva, the composer who brought gaana into Tamil cinema, dies at 75

Deva (Devanesan Chokkalingam), who scored more than 400 films in Tamil, Kannada, Telugu and Malayalam over nearly four decades, died of a heart attack in Chennai on Saturday. His main achievement was bringing gaana, the folk song of Chennai’s working-class neighborhoods, into mainstream film music, often singing his own songs. That music had long been looked down on as the music of the city’s laboring poor. He began composing songs for the Doordarshan farm program Vayalum Vaazhvum and devotional music. His full film debut was Mattukkara Mannaru (1986). His scores for Rajinikanth’s Annamalai (1992) and Baasha (1995), whose “Baasha, Baasha” theme is among the best-known pieces in Tamil film, and for Kamal Haasan’s Panchathanthiram (2002) made him one of the most popular composers of the 1990s. “Tamil popular music history cannot be written without Deva,” said MK Stalin. Lyricist Vairamuthu said: “It feels as though I have lost one of my own fingers.”
