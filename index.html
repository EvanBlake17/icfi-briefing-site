1. Washington tightens AI chip controls as Nvidia reports without Huang

On Monday the Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security announced a rule restricting remote access to overseas data centers that run advanced US AI chips. It is the most sweeping export-control move since Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia CEO on November 12. According to Kyodo, the rule is intended to “undermine the ability of companies headquartered in countries of concern” to use US semiconductor technology to train AI models. Data centers must now run “robust checks” on whether customers are directly or indirectly owned or controlled by people in China, Iran, North Korea or Russia. Exports and re-exports of advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now need a license because of a “higher-than-normal risk of diversion.” Separately, the New York Times reports the administration has announced an investigation into Megaspeed, the Singapore-based data center firm at the center of the scandal. The Times calls this a response to criticism that Commerce had moved too slowly. Nvidia has warned chip buyers of legal risks tied to Chinese access to AI, CNBC reports.

The announcement came hours before Nvidia’s third-quarter earnings, due after the bell Tuesday. They are its first since Huang’s exit. Interim CEO Colette Kress, who remains CFO, had guided to $108 billion in revenue and a 74% gross margin. A CNBC source says Nvidia’s board is not considering bringing Huang back. Trump said Monday he “would certainly not mind” Huang’s return, cast doubt on the spy allegations, and said he “might” raise the matter with Xi. In the WSWS Perspective, Andre Damon argues that “a sex scandal and an unproven spy charge” are being used to force a shift toward full economic war on China. He says the Democrats are leading the charge, attacking Trump as insufficiently belligerent.

Key facts: - Treasury Secretary Bessent said on Friday that Treasury and other agencies had wanted Megaspeed on the Entity List for months, “but Commerce has the pen.” (Kyodo) - Singapore and Washington agreed to continue talks over Megaspeed. Indonesian police raided its Indonesian unit and reportedly found server racks but no Nvidia GPUs. One official said the chips likely went to China, “possibly by private jet.” (Reuters; The Next Web) - The Times reports Huang’s daughter Madison, a close ally, remains in a senior role at Nvidia. - Bill Ackman has outlined how an activist-investor campaign to reinstate Huang could work. (Business Insider)

2. Ukraine brings down a span of the Crimean Bridge as Russian missiles hit Kyiv’s Dnipro crossings

Ukraine struck the Kerch (Crimean) Bridge for the second time in two days. Moscow described the earlier attack as the largest drone strike of the war. Photos and video published Monday night show one of the bridge’s arches and part of the road deck collapsed into the sea, and rail and road traffic is suspended. Late Monday, Russian missiles struck the North and South bridges over the Dnipro in Kyiv, according to Mayor Vitali Klitschko, and caused water and power cuts in the capital. Bloomberg reports that both sides are “seeking to ratchet up the war’s costs” ahead of expected talks, as Washington tries to fold the war into this week’s Shenzhen summit.

On the diplomatic track, Zelensky said Ukraine held new online talks on Monday with US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner and with European representatives. Kushner reported progress toward a deal. A senior Russian official told NHK that Moscow has “not yet agreed” to include Europe in the next round. EU governments accused Russia of dragging out talks while it bombs power plants before winter. The Kyiv Post reports European leaders are preparing a parallel summit in Berlin.

Key facts: - The Crimean Bridge links southern Russia to the annexed peninsula and is Russia’s main overland supply route to Crimea. The Russian-backed governor confirmed the second strike. (Reuters via US News) - The Kyiv bridge strikes follow what Moscow called Ukraine’s largest-ever drone attack on Russia, with Moscow Oblast among the main targets. (Yahoo News) - Trump said he plans to raise the Ukraine war at his meeting with Xi and Putin.

3. Iran bars nuclear inspectors until the war is “permanently ended” as three US carriers gather

Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said on Sunday that IAEA inspectors will not be allowed into Iran’s bombed nuclear sites until the war with the US and Israel has “permanently ended” and Washington meets Iran’s conditions. The statement came days after the IAEA urged Tehran to move toward resuming inspections, and on what The American Conservative counts as day 262 of the war. Also on Monday, the USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier strike group reached the US Central Command area, carrying thousands of additional troops. Three American carriers are now in the region, only the second time that has happened since the 2003 invasion of Iraq. Reports describe Trump as oscillating between threatening new strikes and calling the conflict over.

At sea, the UKMTO reported another tanker attack in the Strait of Hormuz that set the vessel’s engine room on fire. On Monday Oman’s foreign minister met Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi in Muscat on securing maritime traffic and reviving diplomatic channels, “as attacks on tankers and the US naval blockade continue” (Moneycontrol). Iran warned it would respond to any US attack “without waiting for Washington to strike first.”

Key facts: - Pakistan’s Foreign Minister Ishaq Dar told the Economic Cooperation Organization meeting in Islamabad that Pakistan has “not given up hope” on US-Iran mediation and will press on with regional backing. (Firstpost; TRT) - The Mirror calls the US naval deployment the largest in the region since the Second Gulf War. - Gharibabadi framed the inspection terms as a message to Trump. Iran ties any access to an end of hostilities, not merely a ceasefire. (Straits Times; AOL)

5. French public sector strikes as housing protests spread across southern Europe

French unions called a nationwide public sector strike for Tuesday, November 17, to win pay rises. Youth organizations and Yellow Vest activists are joining the mobilization “despite government efforts to ease tensions,” according to wire reports. Bloomberg says the day tests the momentum of a movement that has “brought repeated disruption in recent weeks.”

Housing is a major front on the Iberian Peninsula. Thousands demonstrated across Portugal on Saturday under the banner “Casa para Viver!” (“A home to live in!”), including more than 500 in Faro. Protesters have set up a camp in Lisbon’s Restauradores Square and plan to stay until Saturday and keep protesting until the state budget vote. Housing marches were also held or planned in Santiago de Compostela, Las Palmas and Bilbao. In Prague, Prime Minister Andrej Babiš vowed on Sunday to press ahead with abolishing public broadcasting fees. Police had confirmed the protest against the plan as the largest since the Czech Republic’s founding in 1993 (Radio Prague).

Key facts: - teleSUR counts hundreds of thousands of students and workers in demonstrations across Western Europe since September 2026.

6. Bangladesh garment strike spreads to the highways as police fire tear gas

The garment workers’ wage revolt in Bangladesh, the world’s second-largest apparel exporter, escalated on Tuesday morning. The Business Standard reports that workers blocked the Dhaka-Mymensingh highway at Chandana Chowrasta and the Bhogra bypass in Gazipur, bringing traffic to a standstill. Around 11 a.m. they also blocked the Dhaka-Aricha highway at Fulbaria in Savar. At about 11:50 a.m. police fired tear gas shells and sound grenades to clear the Savar blockade. Clashes and chases were still going on in Hemayetpur at the time of reporting. Workers demand a minimum monthly wage of Tk30,000. The current Tk12,500 (about $240) minimum was set in November 2023.

The Logical Indian counts about 121 factories shut, and owners are threatening indefinite closures without pay. The WSWS gives a higher count: 147 factories employing about 300,000 workers closed across Savar, Ashulia and Gazipur by the weekend. It also reports that the Minimum Wage Board meeting scheduled for November 16 was postponed, and that employers have made no counterproposal.

Key facts: - Two workers have died. Toufiq Sheikh, 21, was attacked on November 2. Mohammad Selim, 23, died after 12 days in hospital from injuries sustained in police attacks. (WSWS) - At least 57 arrests had been reported by November 2, and IndustriALL says at least ten union leaders have been arrested. (WSWS) - Garments make up 85% of Bangladesh’s exports, worth about $38 billion, and employ about 4 million workers. (WSWS) - Employers say the Tk30,000 demand has “no logical or mathematical basis.” The BBC aired interviews with a factory owner and the garment workers’ union on Monday.

Eisenkot bloc moves to seize the Knesset speakership

Nearly three weeks after Israel’s deadlocked October 27 election, the bloc led by former IDF chief of staff Gadi Eisenkot has not formed a coalition. It is nonetheless moving to take control of the Knesset on Tuesday, November 17, with a vote to replace Speaker Amir Ohana. Yashar’s Matan Kahana is expected to win with 67 votes, including 19 Arab members (Anadolu). Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich offered to back Kahana if Arab MKs were excluded from the deputy speaker posts. Kahana rejected the offer as “unstatesmanlike.” Ahmad Tibi of Ta’al has been named temporary deputy speaker. The incoming bloc plans to repeal Netanyahu’s judicial overhaul legislation, with MK Kariv leading the effort.

  • One widely circulated analysis concludes that a government without Netanyahu “may be close, but a government without his politics is far less certain.”

Israel strikes Lebanon’s Beqaa Valley and abducts two brothers in Shebaa

RSF commander defects as the Sudanese army advances in Kordofan

Senior Rapid Support Forces commander al-Rasoul has defected to the Sudanese Armed Forces as the army advances in Kordofan, the New Arab reports. Milburn review targets young people’s disability benefits

Alan Milburn’s review of “Young People and Work,” commissioned by Prime Minister Andy Burnham’s Labour government, is released Tuesday morning. It calls for a “complete system reset” to get 440,000 young people into work, out of nearly a million who are not in education, employment or training. The Guardian reports guaranteed jobs and expanded employment support alongside benefit cuts. Under-25s would be barred from the health element of Universal Credit and from Personal Independence Payment and moved onto a lower “youth allowance.” Milburn has acknowledged that “some people will get less financial support.” One report says the plan would save just £200 million a year after a decade. The Scotsman estimates up to 20,000 young Scots could lose health benefits. A parallel disability benefits review under Sir Stephen Timms is due next month.

AI selloff deepens into a Nasdaq correction

Wall Street fell for a third straight session on Monday, led by chip and AI stocks. The Nasdaq Composite closed in correction territory. Nvidia fell about 4.1% to a 52-week low, and Intel and Micron each lost more than 7%. Banks were also weak. Asian shares followed Wall Street lower on Tuesday. Reuters attributes the fall to tech weakness after Washington “stepped up efforts to curb China’s access to advanced AI chips” (see Top stories). The week brings two tests of the AI investment boom: Nvidia’s earnings after Tuesday’s close, and Anthropic’s IPO. Anthropic is to price 825 million shares at $70–78 on Wednesday and begin trading on Nasdaq as ANT on Thursday. Its amended filing lists $900.9 billion in compute commitments.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said Monday that Fed staff are “gaming out” contingencies for AI’s effect on the economy. He used the term “superintelligence” and warned that the technology could also “disappoint.” He gave no new rate guidance, but markets are pricing further tightening.

Mamdani disbands the NYPD’s Strategic Response Group

On Monday Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Police Commissioner Jessica Tisch announced that the Strategic Response Group will be disbanded. The SRG is the NYPD’s counterterrorism and crowd-control unit, notorious for its violence at protests. Three new teams will replace it, along with a civilian Community Dialogue and De-escalation Division in the Mayor’s Office of Community Safety. Mamdani endorsed a proposed law to make the change permanent. The New York Post objects that this could “further handcuff cops.” The WSWS argues the reorganization moves the SRG’s functions into other units and leaves the police apparatus and Tisch in place.

Funerals began Monday for the construction workers among them, and the FDNY held a vigil Sunday night. Brooklyn officials are demanding accountability. Duffy says FAA ground stop cost “billions” as router maker disputes blame

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy says Friday’s nationwide ground stop cost “billions” and was “no hack.” He vowed to modernize the aging air traffic system and explained why the FAA is “stuck” with L3Harris, the contractor that runs its telecommunications network. Hewlett Packard Enterprise, which made the Arkansas router at the center of the outage, says the device “worked as configured.” The FAA and FBI are examining it. Fast Company reports that auditors, a union and the FAA’s own staff had warned for months about the rushed overhaul and the contractor. The FAA says its new SMART AI traffic tool was not the cause, but lawmakers are scrutinizing its rollout.

Republicans race the lame-duck clock

The Post report shows the federal health apparatus not merely failing to fight the epidemic but keeping clinical warnings from physicians.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Kerch and Kyiv bridges fall as Washington and Moscow bargain over Ukraine in Shenzhen

Ukraine has brought down part of the Crimean Bridge, and Russia has hit Kyiv’s main Dnipro crossings and cut water and power in the capital. All of this is happening days before the first-ever US-China-Russia summit, where Kushner claims “progress” and Moscow shuts Europe out of the talks. An article can expose how both sides are escalating to strengthen their bargaining positions over the carve-up of Ukraine, while Ukrainian and Russian workers face a winter without power.

Recommended reading Bloomberg Ukraine attacks Crimean Bridge again as Russia strikes Kyiv

2. Washington extends the chip war to Southeast Asia and Brazil

Monday’s Commerce rule turns data centers worldwide into instruments of US export enforcement and requires licenses for chip sales to five countries. It is the concrete policy outcome of the campaign the Perspective analyzes. A follow-up could examine the rule’s effect on Malaysia’s and Singapore’s data-center industries, Brazil’s inclusion, and how the AI arms race is drawing in third countries.

Recommended reading Kyodo/Mainichi US restricts remote access to data centers using advanced AI chips

3. Iran shuts out the IAEA as the US assembles a three-carrier armada

Tehran ties inspections to a permanent end of the war, at the moment the largest US naval force since 2003 gathers in the Gulf. Pakistan and Oman are trying to mediate, and Trump alternates between threats and claims that the war is over. Coverage should show how the “non-proliferation” agenda at Shenzhen serves as a pretext for renewed bombing after the midterms.

Recommended reading CBS News Iran says no U.N. inspections of damaged nuclear sites until war is “permanently ended”

4. The CDC kept quiet on measles in pregnancy

Federal scientists asked for a national alert on measles in pregnancy, and the Kennedy-led CDC refused. The data surfaced only through a journal, as congenital cases mount and the US formally loses elimination status. The story exposes the deliberate suppression of public health information, not merely negligence, and fits the WSWS’s ongoing exposure of the Trump-Kennedy assault on vaccination.

Recommended reading Washington Post CDC declined to alert risk of measles in pregnancy. A journal released the data.

5. Milburn review: Burnham’s Labour targets disabled young people

The review is released today. It would push under-25s off PIP and the Universal Credit health element onto a lower youth allowance, for savings of only £200 million a year after a decade. That suggests the aim is disciplinary and political, not fiscal. A news article on the published proposals would build on the WSWS analysis of the CSJ report and the drive to fund rearmament through welfare cuts.

Recommended reading Guardian Andy Burnham’s government wants to get young people into work. Will cutting their benefits help?

6. Israel after Netanyahu: the Eisenkot bloc and the continuity of genocide

The Knesset speaker vote marks the practical end of Netanyahu’s control of parliament. But Eisenkot, a former chief of staff, is the architect of the “Dahiya doctrine” of disproportionate force against civilian areas. Meanwhile the Gaza “ceasefire” has passed 6,000 violations, and Israel struck the Beqaa and abducted Lebanese civilians. A perspective could warn against illusions that a change of government means a change of policy.

Recommended reading MSN Is Israel’s Eisenkot bloc less right wing than Netanyahu?

7. CSU faculty and Canadian teachers move toward strikes as education austerity spreads

California State University faculty are voting on a strike, 45,000 Ontario Catholic teachers voted 98% for a strike mandate, and Nova Scotia support workers rejected their contract. These struggles run alongside teacher strikes across Latin America and the Victorian schools crisis. A report could connect them as a common international fight against austerity and propose rank-and-file committees.

Recommended reading Mercury News California State University faculty are voting on whether to strike

A Harvard-led study puts US hyperscale data centers at 1.8% of national electricity and 25.7 million tons of CO₂ a year. The figures land the same week Anthropic’s filing shows $900 billion in compute commitments and COP31 launches a voluntary “AI pledge.” The science article could link the AI capital boom, energy demand and the bankruptcy of voluntary climate diplomacy.
