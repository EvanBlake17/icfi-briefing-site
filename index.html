1. US land blockade of Iran takes effect as Rubio calls war unpopular but necessary

President Trump announced Sunday that a US land blockade of Iran is now in effect. The war is now eight months old. Trump told CNN that renewed US strikes were “highly unlikely” to be needed if talks yield “a very strong agreement.” He also said three aircraft carriers are in the region, “fully locked and loaded,” and boasted that US forces could knock down 38 Iranian bridges “in literally one hour.” He meets Qatar’s prime minister and foreign minister at the White House today. According to AP, Tehran is reviewing Qatari proposals to narrow the gap.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio, speaking in Florida, quoted Trump’s own description of the war: “doing it isn’t necessarily popular, but necessary.” He said “I wouldn’t say we’re at the verge of a breakthrough” and called Iran’s demands “outrageous and ridiculous.” Trump said he expects to raise “denuclearisation” and the Iran war with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin at next week’s APEC summit in China. France 24 reports that Washington has not ruled out striking the suspected nuclear site known as Pickaxe Mountain, which Tehran denies is a nuclear facility.

Key facts: - Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi wrote that “American voters have made their choice” and that Tehran’s “2-day proposal stands.” Under that proposal, Iran would reopen Hormuz to commercial shipping within 48 hours if the US ends the war “on all fronts.” He warned Iran would “rain down hell” if Washington resumes attacks. - IRGC Brigadier-General Ali Belali said Iran’s response “will be devastating and severe.” The Foreign Ministry said it would “not go easy on American targets.” - Iranian state media reported that an oil tanker caught fire after hitting a naval mine east of Hormuz. Iran’s navy said the ship had switched off its identification systems and entered the restricted zone. - Al Jazeera’s live coverage reported explosions on Qeshm island, which were blamed on the US. Iranian authorities said separate blasts in Khuzestan were not caused by an attack. - Araghchi blamed the US midterms for the delay in talks. He said mediators would need time before resuming shuttle visits.

2. Trump and Blanche threaten prosecutions as Jeffries backs Hegseth impeachment

The first weekend of Sunday shows since the November 3 midterm rout, in which Democrats took both houses of Congress, turned into an open confrontation. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next Speaker, said “I’m a hard yes on the Hegseth resolution.” The impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is led by outgoing Republican Rep. Thomas Massie, a critic of the Iran war. Jeffries accuses Hegseth of engineering that war. Rep. Robert Garcia, who is seeking to chair House Oversight, named Stephen Miller as a priority target. Trump made Miller White House chief of staff last week, a post that needs no Senate confirmation. Garcia also wants to question Elon Musk over DOGE’s federal job cuts and its access to personal data.

The administration answered with threats. On CNN, Attorney General Todd Blanche said Democratic lawmakers could face investigation over “threatening” rhetoric: “If that’s what this new incoming leadership wants to do, we are ready.” On Fox News Sunday he said the Justice Department would represent any official facing impeachment, including Hegseth (“If you work in this administration, you have a lawyer”). Asked whether Democrats could be prosecuted, he said, “It depends on what the crime is and what they’ve done.” Trump posted on Truth Social that Democrats planning to summon his allies were “SLEAZEBAGS” who “should go to jail,” and called their plans “totally illegal.”

Leading Democrats on the same programs stressed cooperation. As Patrick Martin wrote on the WSWS, Bernie Sanders told Meet the Press and CNN that Democrats should not focus mainly on investigating Trump. He called Trump “erratic” rather than fascist and “a good politician,” and suggested Trump might back a living wage and universal health care. Elizabeth Warren told Face the Nation she looked forward to bipartisan work on credit card interest rates and on tighter chip export curbs against China.

Key facts: - The new Congress is sworn in January 3. Republicans control both chambers until then. - The House can impeach by simple majority. Removal requires two-thirds of the Senate. - Martin reports that Sanders would not commit to opposing Iran war funding before the December 11 funding deadline. Sanders also warned that 15 million people lose health coverage on January 1, 2027, under the 2025 “One Big Beautiful Bill.” - France 24 reports Trump is vowing a political “comeback” as the parties clash over the prosecution threats.

3. Abu Dhabi talks set for Tuesday as Trump says Ukraine will have a different shape

US-mediated trilateral talks between Russia and Ukraine take place in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday and Wednesday (November 10–11). French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said Europe “will be represented” and that France will attend, but he did not name its delegate. G7 foreign ministers meet in Paris for two days from today. Rubio will attend both days, unlike in March. The European powers plan to press Washington for a “fair settlement” and to hold off further US military action against Iran. France 24 reports European concern that Kyiv could be pushed into unfavorable terms. Trump has predicted a “very big” deal with Russia covering “all of Europe,” and envoy Jared Kushner described US work on a broader European security framework.

In a 60 Minutes interview aired Sunday, Trump said Ukraine would likely “end up with a little bit different shape” after the war and that “the only reason it’s going to end is because of me.” He defended his waiver on Russian oil sanctions. He also attacked Biden-era aid, telling his interviewer, “You haven’t gotten anything yet.” He denied responsibility for the October speed caps on Ukraine’s Starshield satellite service.

Key facts from the front: - Ukrainian drones reportedly struck the Kirishi (Kinef) refinery overnight. It is Russia’s second-largest, about 800 km north of Ukraine. They also hit a bridge over the Mius River on the highway linking Rostov to occupied Ukraine. - The Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant lost its sole external power line and ran on diesel generators for about 10 hours before the IAEA confirmed power was restored. - Russian strikes on the power grid continue. Three people in Kyiv Oblast died of carbon monoxide poisoning from a generator run indoors. - Ukraine reported 725 Russian strikes on 53 settlements in Zaporizhzhia Oblast in one day. - Moscow police detained more than 200 people. Some detainees reported pressure to sign military contracts, according to a Russian Telegram channel cited by the Kyiv Independent. - Zelensky promised more personnel changes at the SBU after senior officers were dismissed over ties to a criminal gang.

As Clara Weiss reported on the WSWS, protests against an Azov-run detention center near Kyiv show “boiling tensions” over forced mobilization as the war nears its fifth year (details below).

4. Far-right gunman kills six in Krakow as Poland braces for Independence March

On Saturday, a 24-year-old gunman identified as Michał O. opened fire from his car on Krakow’s Main Market Square. He killed six people, including two foreign nationals (one of them British), and wounded about 15–16, among them two police officers. He wore camouflage, a helmet and a protective vest and used a legally licensed AR-15-style rifle. Police later found his parents and sister shot dead at the family home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, bringing the toll to nine. France 24 reports police announced Monday that the gunman had died in hospital. It is Poland’s deadliest shooting since the end of Stalinist rule in 1989.

Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński said the suspect likely held “very radical views” and was fascinated by “far-right ideologies.” Prosecutors opened a terrorism investigation on Sunday, and the president declared two days of national mourning. The shooting comes before Wednesday’s Independence March, which draws hundreds of thousands of nationalists, Catholic conservatives and fascist groups. President Karol Nawrocki will attend. Prime Minister Donald Tusk called rumors of a ban “disinformation”: “No one intends to ban the Independence March.”

Key facts: - A Constitutional Tribunal session is set for Tuesday. Riot police blocked access to the court, and a newly appointed judge took over in an interim role, in effect suspending the incumbent president of the court, who is close to Nawrocki. - The opposition Law and Justice party (PiS) cancelled protests planned for Monday and Tuesday but may resume them later in the week. - Observers fear marchers could leave the agreed route and head for the Tribunal. - Writing in Rzeczpospolita, Bogusław Chrabota said radicalized discourse “gives rise to Polish Breiviks.”

5. Eisenkot gets mandate to form Israeli government and opens door to Netanyahu

At a ceremony in Jerusalem on Sunday, President Isaac Herzog formally tasked opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot with forming a government after the inconclusive October 27 election. Al Jazeera says he has six weeks. Eisenkot was military chief of staff from 2015 to 2019 and sat as an observer in Netanyahu’s war cabinet for the first eight months of the Gaza genocide. He said he was open to including Netanyahu in his coalition and suggested far-right Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich would be welcome. Herzog said the US midterms had created a “new political reality” and uncertainty for Israel. Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister.

Key facts: - Seats: Eisenkot’s four-party alliance has 48, including 21 for his Yashar party. Netanyahu’s right-wing and religious bloc has 53. The Arab-led parties have a record 19 (Joint List 11, Ra’am 8). A majority is 61. - Eisenkot has ruled out bringing in the Arab-led parties, which endorsed him and would give his camp 67 seats. Winning over the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism and Shas would give him exactly 61. - If Eisenkot fails, Netanyahu is next in line. If both fail, new elections follow and Netanyahu stays caretaker. Al Jazeera’s Nour Odeh reports many of Netanyahu’s critics believe that is his aim. - Eisenkot: “I cannot conceive of any reason why they would not support a government under my leadership.”

As Jean Shaoul wrote on the WSWS, the contest is “a factional struggle” within the ruling class over how to conduct the war. Both camps back the Gaza onslaught, its extension into the West Bank, and pressure on Iran.

6. New Zealand election ends with both major parties at historic lows

Saturday’s general election left no clear winner and a caretaker government that will stay in place for weeks. In preliminary results, Labour took 29.36 percent and National 29.21 percent, down from 38 percent in 2023. Both are historic lows. National won 42 seats and Labour 36. The governing National–ACT–NZ First coalition holds 67 of 131 seats, but RNZ notes that about 399,000 special votes, counted by November 27, could erase that majority. Turnout was about 74.6 percent of enrolled voters, the second-lowest in the country’s history.

Key facts: - Greens 13.3 percent (their best result ever), ACT 10.9 percent, NZ First 9.78 percent. The Opportunity Party (TOP) took 5.96 percent and seven seats, and its leader Qiulae Wong is being called a possible kingmaker. - An overhang has enlarged parliament, which RNZ estimates will cost about $5 million in the first year. There are 35 new MPs. - National lost several senior MPs, including Chris Bishop, who lost Hutt South to Labour. - Luxon: “It is too close to call.” Hipkins said the next parliament will “require all of us to compromise, to work together and to find a common ground.” He also attacked the vote-splitting strategy, saying democracy is not “a plaything for billionaires.” - RNZ’s own analysis describes “a story of disenchantment and fracturing.”

Middle East

Houthis claim missile and drone strike on Riyadh financial district

Yemen’s Houthis claimed four operations on Sunday using ballistic and cruise missiles and drones. They listed as targets Riyadh’s King Abdullah Financial District (KAFD), Riyadh airport, Aramco storage tanks and Abqaiq. Eyewitnesses told AFP that windows were blown out across several floors of the Aramco tower. The Saudi energy ministry called it an “attempted attack” causing minor shrapnel damage and no casualties. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Malki said two ballistic missiles were intercepted over Riyadh.

  • The 2022 truce collapsed in July 2026 after the Saudi-backed government struck Sanaa airport. The Houthis then declared a maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia and seized Yemen’s entire Red Sea coast.
  • The UN counts more than 1,600 killed and over 300,000 displaced since fighting resumed.
  • KAFD houses the Public Investment Fund, the stock exchange and Goldman Sachs. Analyst Andreas Krieg called the strike an assault on the credibility of Vision 2030.
  • Britain began flying RAF jets on “defensive” missions over Saudi cities in October. Yemeni officials say thousands of Syrian troops are deploying to Saudi Arabia and Yemen, which Damascus denies “at this time.”

Drone strike on Khor Mor gasfield blacks out most of Iraqi Kurdistan

A drone struck the Khor Mor gasfield in Sulaymaniyah province on Sunday, starting a fire and halting production. The Kurdistan Regional Government’s electricity ministry said 2,500–2,800 MW dropped off the grid, and gas supply to power plants in Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, Bazian, Chamchamal and Duhok was suspended. More than 70 percent of the region was left without power. Hospitals and water plants are running on hydroelectric power and alternative fuels. Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi reported no casualties and set up a joint investigative committee. Nobody has claimed the attack, the second on the facility in a year. It is unclear whether it is connected to the US-Israeli war on Iran.

Europe

More than 200,000 march in Amsterdam demanding sanctions on Israel

More than 200,000 people marched through Amsterdam on Sunday in the fourth “Red Line” protest, by the count of co-organizer Amnesty Netherlands. They demanded an end to arms sales, tighter trade restrictions, and severed economic and military ties with Israel under the slogan “No Money for Genocide.” Prime Minister Rob Jetten, who marched in last year’s protest, stayed away. He said his government is applying “greater political and diplomatic pressure” on Israel. Protester Mike van Dongen said Dutch authorities “don’t dare” take serious steps.

  • Al Jazeera puts the death toll from Israel’s war since October 2023 at at least 74,000 Palestinians, including 21,000 children. Israeli attacks have continued almost daily since the October 10, 2025 “ceasefire.”
  • The Dutch minority government, in office since February, banned imports from settlements, which sparked a diplomatic row with Israel.
  • The same day, Hamas blamed Israeli policy for a deadly sandbank collapse in Gaza, and Israel released Palestinian journalist Abed Abou Shhadeh. He said his detention was meant to intimidate Arab society.

French PM Lecornu meets unions and employers as protests continue

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu meets union and employer representatives today on wages and purchasing power. The government is already citing the national debt as a limit on what it can concede. Merz to nominate Ilse Aigner for German presidency

Chancellor Friedrich Merz told a CDU leadership dinner on Sunday that he will nominate Ilse Aigner, 61, as federal president. A joint CDU–CSU meeting today is expected to confirm the choice. Aigner is a CSU politician, former agriculture minister under Merkel, and speaker of the Bavarian parliament since 2018. Under German practice the coalition partner SPD must agree, and it has often backed Aigner. The Federal Convention votes January 30. Frank-Walter Steinmeier (SPD) reaches his two-term limit in March 2027. The CDU’s months of delay had strained relations with the CSU.

Africa

Egypt train collision near Cairo kills seven and injures more than 125

On Sunday a Giza-to-Fayoum train ran into the back of a stationary Cairo-to-Aswan train in Giza’s al-Ayyat district. Six carriages overturned and some were thrown onto a country road. The Health Ministry reported at least seven dead and more than 125 injured. Villagers and railway workers dug passengers out before more than 70 ambulances arrived. President el-Sisi ordered an urgent investigation to hold anyone “negligent” accountable.

  • In 2002, a fire on a train near al-Ayyat killed more than 370 people, Egypt’s worst rail disaster. Other deadly crashes followed in 2009, 2021 (11 dead) and August 2025.
  • Egypt’s 110 million people depend heavily on rail. The regime has poured billions into highways and showcase high-speed rail while much of the ordinary network remains outdated.

Migrant boat sinks off Senegal killing at least 34

A pirogue capsized off the Loumpoul dunes in Kebemer department, between Dakar and Saint-Louis, killing at least 34. Only one survivor has been found, and an “indeterminate number” remain missing. President Bassirou Diomaye Faye pledged to pursue the organizers of the crossings. The day before, authorities intercepted 142 people, including 23 women and four children, on a Spain-bound boat.

  • The Canary island of El Hierro declared an emergency Saturday after more than 3,300 arrivals on about 30 boats.
  • Patrols by Senegal, Mauritania and Morocco, backed by the EU, are pushing departures south toward Gambia, Guinea-Bissau and Guinea, which lengthens an already lethal crossing.

Latin America

Pope Leo criticizes Milei austerity on Argentina visit

Pope Leo XIV, three weeks after lung cancer surgery, arrived in Buenos Aires on Sunday. It is the first papal visit to Argentina in nearly four decades. Speaking in front of President Javier Milei, he said “the economy must be at the service of the people, rather than people being at the service of the economy.” He also spoke of workers who “despite hard work, nonetheless struggle to meet their household’s needs,” and of pensioners and the disabled. France 24 reads the latter as a reference to Milei’s cuts to disability assistance.

  • The poverty rate reached 32.3 percent in September. Milei’s cuts have targeted public hospitals, universities and pensions, and critics say he has restricted the right to protest.
  • Leo’s 12-day tour includes a prison visit and three open-air masses. The Church expects seven million attendees.
  • Only 57.7 percent of Argentines now identify as Catholic, down from about 90 percent in the 1960s.

Nvidia board investigates Huang over relationship with alleged Chinese spy

On Sunday Nvidia said a committee of independent directors, working with outside counsel, will investigate CEO Jensen Huang. Huang has confirmed an intimate relationship with a woman accused of working for Chinese intelligence. In an email to staff he wrote, “I was unfaithful to my wife.” The board said it has “no reason to believe” national security or trade secrets were compromised and that Huang remains in charge. It added: “The Board prioritizes U.S. national and economic security.”

  • The New York Times reported on October 29 that Alice Huang (Huang Le), who is not related to the CEO, sought ties to him to obtain information on Nvidia technology. Her employer Megaspeed bought about $4.8 billion in Nvidia equipment. China’s embassy denies the allegations.

  • Trump called Huang “an honourable guy.” He reportedly bought up to $1.6 million in Nvidia stock and sold about $1 million in August, and his administration takes a 15 percent cut of certain Nvidia chip sales to China.

The affair is turning into another front in the US–China technology war, with Democrats positioning themselves as more hawkish on China than the administration.

Vance blames Iran war for Republican rout as Trump rejects regret

The return of measles in the US and the bipartisan dismantling of public health — Benjamin Mateus

The CDC figures are reported in the Science section above. Mateus argues that measles has returned because of deliberate policy. He traces the collapse to the abandonment of measures against COVID-19, begun under Trump’s first term and continued under Biden, which let routine childhood care break down. Kindergarten two-dose MMR coverage fell from 95.2 percent in 2019–20 to 92.4 percent in 2025–26, and exemptions reached a record 4.2 percent, below the roughly 95 percent needed to stop transmission. There were no measles deaths from 2016 to 2024 and three in 2025. This year’s deaths are concentrated in Pennsylvania (six), with one each in New York and Wisconsin.

He rejects blaming the Amish and other Plain communities, which have always been largely unvaccinated. What has changed is the collapse of immunity around them. Kennedy called the outbreaks routine and suggested some Pennsylvania deaths may have been fabricated. Mateus calls the MAHA movement’s “medical freedom” rhetoric the spearhead of a “fascist” assault on public health and the cover for society abandoning responsibility for it.

The global data he cites: under-five measles cases rose 40 percent worldwide from 2019 to 2023 (Nature Medicine). Bangladesh has had more than 1,000 deaths since March after cutting UNICEF vaccine procurement. The Americas have recorded 57,666 cases this year, with 53 deaths in Guatemala and 23 in Mexico. A Lancet study estimates vaccination has averted 154 million deaths since 1974, 93.7 million of them from measles vaccination. Mateus contrasts the present with the Soviet fight against typhus, quoting Lenin’s 1919 slogan, “Either the lice will defeat socialism, or socialism will defeat the lice!” His conclusion is that public health cannot be defended by the parties that dismantled it, and that the working class must organize independently to do so.
