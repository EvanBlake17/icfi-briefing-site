1. COP31 summit in Antalya collapses without an agreement

The UN climate summit in Antalya, Türkiye, ended on Sunday, November 22, without adopting its main political text. Delegates rejected a fourth, 12-page draft 44 hours after the scheduled close, which the WSWS notes makes it the longest UN climate conference on record. It is the first COP since Copenhagen in 2009 to end without an overarching agreement. Any deal requires near-unanimous consensus (AP), and the split was over fossil fuels. Saudi Arabia, Russia and India opposed language on moving away from coal, oil and gas, citing economic risks. The EU, Britain, Norway, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Vanuatu and Belize rejected a final text that left them out. The US sent no delegation for the second year running. While the talks were underway, Trump, Xi and Putin jointly pledged in Shenzhen to increase petroleum supply.

As Bryan Dyne argued on the WSWS, governments bound to the fossil fuel corporations and their financiers cannot carry out what the crisis requires: “the planned, international reorganization of energy production.”

Key facts: - Draft history (WSWS): The first draft named fossil fuels six times and called for a phase-out. The second removed every reference. The final draft mentioned only the 2023 UAE Consensus, without naming coal, oil or gas. - Ireland’s climate minister Darragh O’Brien, speaking for the EU, said the draft “fails to meet the moment.” He called it “greenwashing” and said leaving fossil fuels unnamed was “unconscionable” (NBC News). - COP31 president Murat Kurum: “There is nothing to do. We did all that we could.” - Chief negotiator Chris Bowen cited “unbridgeable red lines.” He insisted the existing agreement to reduce coal, oil and gas use remains in force and that the transition “is happening at speed and it’s irreversible.” - Temperature: The final draft acknowledged that the Paris 1.5°C target will be missed. Current national plans put the world on track for just over 2.5°C. - Next steps: Key decisions may be pushed to next year’s COP in Ethiopia.

2. Democrats reach a 258-seat House majority, then rule out using it against the Iran war

On Sunday the AP called North Carolina for Democrat Chuck Hubbard, who unseated Republican Rep. Virginia Foxx in a district the Cook Political Report had rated lean Republican. Foxx had won by nearly 19 points in 2024. The result gives Democrats 258 House seats. That is their most this century, above their 2008 peak, and their largest majority from a national election since 1992. Republicans hold 173, with races still outstanding (New York Times), even after mid-decade redistricting drawn in their favor. Former Speaker Kevin McCarthy told the Times the margin lets incoming Speaker Hakeem Jeffries release members to vote against bills without endangering them: “It gives real power.”

In the same days, Democratic leaders publicly ruled out using that power to stop the war on Iran. The government runs on a continuing resolution that expires on December 11. Republicans lack the 60 Senate votes needed to pass a spending bill, so Democrats decide whether one passes. As Andre Damon reported on the WSWS, 19 days after winning both houses, party leaders have ruled out using the December 11 deadline to stop or limit the war and are instead offering to work with Trump. Trump has said he will veto whatever the new Congress sends until it accepts his terms, and he has posted a photo of a “VETO” hat.

Key facts: - Schumer, November 4: “We are going to end the war,” citing the power of the purse. - Rep. Ansari, a day after calling for an immediate end to the war (November 18): “Nobody wants a government shutdown. There’s no question of that.” - Sen. Van Hollen had joined three senators on November 12 in demanding spending-bill language to restrict war funds. He now favors a floor amendment instead and says he is “never in favor of shutting down the government.” - Senate war-powers vote (November 19): It failed 48–51. Three Republicans voted yes and Democrat John Fetterman voted no. GOP Sen. Thom Tillis reversed his earlier vote, saying, “This one doesn’t go to the president, so it’s just a statement from Congress” (CBS). - “Trumpcare”: Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed posted a universal-coverage plan branded “Trumpcare,” with gold mock insurance cards. Trump reshared it without comment on Saturday. On Sunday El-Sayed told Meet the Press that “plans are in the works” for him to meet Trump or Vance on healthcare. During the campaign Trump had called El-Sayed a “jihadist.” - Jeffries has named cost of living, healthcare and immigration as areas to “work together with the president.”

3. Denmark and Poland press the EU to restrict Russian citizens after an alleged assassination plot

Denmark will ask the EU this week for tougher measures against Russia, possibly including restrictions on Russian citizens entering and moving freely within the bloc, and Poland says it will back the request. The move follows Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen’s announcement on Friday that Danish intelligence (PET) had foiled a plot “planned and initiated by Russian intelligence services.” The target was Albert Vasiliev, a rapper who performs as Kyivstoner and holds Ukrainian and US citizenship. He was to be killed at a concert north of Copenhagen on September 25. On Sunday, Polish authorities said Moscow’s services had also allegedly targeted Vasiliev in Poland last summer. The Russian embassy called the accusations unsubstantiated.

The proposal would extend the conflict from sanctions on the Russian state to the collective restriction of Russian nationals, and it is unfolding alongside a confrontation in Polish airspace.

Key facts: - Charges: Two men have been charged with attempted murder and with assisting a foreign intelligence service. Denmark has expelled Russia’s defense attaché. - Von der Leyen: Russia’s threats are “becoming more real” and are reaching EU territory. - Polish airspace: Defense Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz said one of the three objects that entered Polish airspace during the “Coalition of the Willing” exercises was a Kh-101 cruise missile. Hours after Trump called the incident a “mistake,” Prime Minister Donald Tusk said, “This doesn’t look like an accident.” Officials call it an “intentional provocation,” and Warsaw is asking NATO for more protection on the eastern flank. - NATO: Secretary General Mark Rutte, who traveled to Warsaw on Saturday, called the incursion a “grave violation.” - Elsewhere on the front (November 21–22): A Russian drone hit a five-story building in Kharkiv region, wounding 11 including two children. Ukrainian drones wounded three people in Russia’s Bryansk and Belgorod regions.

4. Burnham signs first Brexit “reset” agreements in Brussels

At the second EU–UK summit since Brexit, held in Brussels on November 20, Prime Minister Andy Burnham signed a set of agreements that von der Leyen called “a turning point.” Burnham said, “This is a good day for Britain and this is a good day for Europe,” and described the deals as an opening step in a broader debate that could include rejoining. Senior Labour figures are pushing for a rejoin pledge or a referendum. Earlier in the week Burnham said “the Brexit we were given has not worked.” The AP notes that restoring ties will take years.

Chris Marsden argued on the WSWS that the deal is not a reversal of Brexit but “a reorientation of the British ruling class to rearming Europe” amid a deepening rift with Washington over trade and Ukraine. He wrote that “Burnham’s turn to Europe is a decision to pursue British imperialism’s predatory interests against Russia.”

Key facts: - Food: A common food-safety area from March 2028. Britain aligns with EU food rules and border checks on animal and plant products end. - Carbon: The UK emissions-trading scheme links to the EU’s, exempting British steel and other exports from the EU carbon border tax. - Youth mobility: Up to 100,000 people aged 18–31 from each side may live and work across the Channel, starting by 2030 at the latest. Marsden puts the stay at up to three years, while the Washington Post reported two-year visas. - Conditions: The package depends on a tuition-fee deal for EU students within six months. The food agreement includes a compensation penalty aimed at a future Reform UK or Conservative government that might withdraw. - Scale: The Treasury estimates the gain at 0.5% of GDP. Marsden cites estimates that Brexit cost 4–8%. - Washington’s response: US tariffs on British goods have reportedly risen from 0.8% to 7.2%, and the US has criticized the UK’s alignment with EU rules. - Polls: The 2016 vote was 52–48 to leave. Recent polls cited by the WSJ show about 62% now favor rejoining. - Mark Leonard (ECFR): “The British political class is catching up with the rest of the country.”

5. Settler arson near Ramallah deepens Israel’s post-election crisis

Overnight attacks on Palestinian villages near Ramallah on Saturday left vehicles burned and anti-Arab graffiti on walls. Reuters journalists in Kufr Malek, east of Ramallah, saw torched cars and the word “Revenge” sprayed on walls. Israeli settlers are suspected. UN faces its first-ever vacancy at the top as Security Council deadlocks

Secretary-General António Guterres leaves office on December 31, and the Security Council has not chosen a successor after eight inconclusive secret ballots (Reuters, Al Jazeera, November 19). This has never happened before. The five veto-holding permanent members remain deadlocked. Costa Rica’s Rebeca Grynspan is the frontrunner, and the UAE’s Lana Nusseibeh is also in the field. Turkey added Feridun Sinirlioğlu at the “11th hour.” The Charter does not say what happens if the post is vacant. The options, extending Guterres’s term or naming an acting chief, both face political obstacles.

  • Security Council Report’s Shamala Kandiah Thompson: “What is worrying is how late in the year this is.”
  • Open University’s Georgina Holmes: There is a “struggle over who will exercise political influence within the UN.”
  • Context: The deadlock coincides with the Shenzhen declaration’s call for “UN reform” and with waning US engagement in the institution.

NIH reviews link glyphosate to blood cancers in people and tumors in rodents

Two National Institutes of Health reviews, posted ahead of peer review and reported by the Washington Post on November 22, link glyphosate, the active ingredient in Roundup, to blood cancers in people and to tumors in mice and rats. The findings put federal researchers at odds with the EPA’s long-standing position that glyphosate is “unlikely to cause cancer.” The EPA is reexamining the chemical with public input, and the EPA and HHS say they will add the NIH data to that review. Bayer, which owns Roundup maker Monsanto, calls the research preliminary and stands by the product’s safety record.

  • Health advocates: The NIH work supports years of court claims linking glyphosate to non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
  • Political conflict: HHS Secretary Kennedy and the MAHA movement have long called for tighter glyphosate rules. That puts them at odds with Trump allies in agribusiness (Politico), after the administration had backed Bayer at the Supreme Court and promoted domestic glyphosate production.
  • Timing: The findings came out one day after NPR reported on Bayer’s settlement with thousands of Roundup claimants.

BepiColombo enters orbit around Mercury

The European Space Agency and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency’s BepiColombo mission, a pair of linked spacecraft, entered orbit around Mercury on Saturday after an eight-year cruise. The two craft will separate next month and orbit at different altitudes. They will study Mercury’s dense interior, its magnetic field, its craters and volcanic plains, and ridges up to three miles high. Surface temperatures above 800°F put heavy strain on the electronics. The mission is planned to last at least one Earth year, about four Mercury years. It is the first visit to Mercury in more than a decade.

Arts and culture

Sunrise on the Reaping and the market for dystopia

The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping opened with an estimated $120 million in North America and $225 million worldwide over the November 20–22 weekend, on a budget of about $150 million. It is Lionsgate’s sixth film in the franchise and the year’s fifth domestic opening above $100 million. The prequel, adapted from Suzanne Collins’s 2025 novel, is set 25 years before the original story. Its predecessor, The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes (2023), took only $361.8 million worldwide, about half what each of the first four films earned. Total ticket sales were still about 10% below the same weekend last year, partly because some potential moviegoers were playing Grand Theft Auto VI, which Rockstar says took a record $3.79 billion on its first day.

Collins’s series is built on a real social image: an oligarchic capital that turns the children of impoverished districts into televised spectacle and turns rebellion into branding. That it opened in a week of war, a market crash and mass youth strikes against conscription shows how directly it speaks to the public mood. It also shows how readily the film industry sells that alienation back as a franchise commodity. The question for any review is whether the film deepens Collins’s critique of spectacle and state violence, or settles for the series’ habitual turn to individual heroism.

Medieval Nubia’s “Great Church of Jesus” reaches floor level at Old Dongola

A Polish-Sudanese mission led by Artur Obłuski has reached the original floor of the monumental cathedral at Old Dongola on the Nile’s east bank, first identified in 2021. The team descended a staircase whose walls stand about 36 feet high. The building measured roughly 154 by 187 feet, “larger than the famous White House,” Obłuski said, and was probably the principal church of the kingdom of Makuria, one of three Christian Nile kingdoms that emerged after the 6th-century missions. He compares its regional influence to Justinian’s Hagia Sophia. The excavation is going on while Sudan is being torn apart by civil war, with its heritage and population both under assault. It is a reminder of an African civilization that colonial historiography long ignored.

WSWS coverage — last 24 hours

Perspective published: “Europe-wide strike wave: Prepare a European general strike!” by Johannes Stern — read in full on wsws.org. (The URL is dated /2026/11/23/ and the piece is labeled Perspective, but the page displays 22 November. The Perspectives index page could not be loaded to confirm.)

Stern treats the national strikes and youth protests as one continental movement with “the same underlying causes: falling real wages, skyrocketing living costs” and rearmament. Figures cited (mostly from unions and organizers): - France: More than 500,000 in the national day of action. - Italy: Up to 1.5 million marched against Meloni’s budget, per the CGIL, which has called five one-day general strikes against these budgets. - Germany: About 87,000 youth marched against war and austerity. 151,000 metal and electrical workers walked out. - Belgium: The government wants about €10 billion in cuts and revenue by 2029. The FGTB, CSC and Synova unions answered with a €21 billion “alternative budget” framed around competitiveness. - Greece: ADEDY held a single one-day strike while Athens funds a roughly €25 billion rearmament program.

His central argument is that the unions are integrated into militarization. He cites IG Metall’s support for converting plants to munitions, the CGT’s demand for state control of defense production, and the CGIL’s call for budget amendments. The union leaderships “defend capitalism and fundamentally support the policies of war and austerity.” He cites the 2015 Syriza betrayal and notes that a school-strike spokesperson at the November 20 Berlin rally endorsed an “international general strike” after IYSSE interventions. His conclusions: “The struggles must be developed independently and across national borders into a European general strike,” which “must not be another symbolic protest aimed at putting pressure on capitalist governments” but must raise the question of power. That means rank-and-file committees, expropriation of the banks and corporations, and a United Socialist States of Europe in place of the “imperialist European Union,” uniting workers in Russia and Ukraine, under the leadership of the SEPs and the ICFI.

COP31 summit in Türkiye collapses: Capitalism blocks any solution to climate crisis — Bryan Dyne

Beyond the chronology in top story 1, Dyne’s unique contribution is the arithmetic of priorities: - Military vs. climate: European military spending rose 14% in 2025 to $864 billion (SIPRI). Developing countries need about $305 billion a year for climate-resilient infrastructure. - Profits vs. aid: Guterres has noted that the nine largest oil companies made $95 billion in profit in one quarter. The Loss and Damage Fund has received about $600 million. - Warming: UNEP’s latest report puts warming at 2.6°C.

Dyne argues the COP process has failed since 1995, and that Kyoto and Paris were steps toward this breakdown, not departures from it. He describes the EU’s climate posture as driven by geopolitical rivalry with Russia and energy security rather than environmental concern, which helps explain Brussels’s “ambition” when its own rearmament is factored in. His programmatic conclusion: the expropriation of the energy, construction and banking monopolies under democratic working-class control, as part of an internationalist socialist program. His core argument is that capitalism cannot address climate change because it rests on competing nation-states and private control of energy.

Democrats back off demand to bar Iran war funding in spending bill, offer to work with Trump — Andre Damon

The material not used in top story 2 is Damon’s argument about the limits of the shutdown tactic itself. Troops stay on duty during a funding lapse, and the Pentagon can draw on unspent 2025 reconciliation funds, so even a shutdown would not end the war. Damon cites the ruling-class voices shaping the Democrats’ retreat: - Washington Post editorial: “Congress should not shut down the government to stop him.” - Thomas Friedman (November 17): Democrats should investigate the war rather than defund it. - Hillary Clinton (November 19): The war is depleting the Patriot interceptor stocks needed in Ukraine.

They rule out impeachment and mass mobilization and seek cooperation with Trump in the interests of the oligarchy. Their concern is the war’s effect on American imperialism’s position, not its victims. Only an independent mass movement of the working class can stop the war.

International Court of Justice finds Myanmar guilty of genocide — Peter Symonds

Symonds’s account of the November 19 ruling adds several findings. Myanmar also failed to prevent and punish genocide, failed to enact the required legislation, and breached a 2020 order to preserve evidence. Fewer than 600,000 Rohingya remain in Myanmar, while more than a million live in deteriorating camps. The court ordered investigation, prosecution and restitution, but not restoration of citizenship. Symonds attacks Aung San Suu Kyi and the NLD for shielding the military and dismisses her Western image as a “democracy icon.” He rebuts the Wall Street Journal, which complained that the ruling’s standard is “designed to facilitate bad-faith allegations of ‘genocide’ against Israel.” Symonds notes that the State Department itself declared Myanmar’s actions genocide in 2022. The court “has no power to enforce any of its orders,” and President Min Aung Hlaing will not be arrested. But the precedent of inferring intent as “the only reasonable inference” from the pattern of conduct threatens to expose Washington and its allies. That is why Trump’s attacks on the ICC and the Shenzhen declaration’s demand for immunity matter.

Reject the US Steel contract! Build rank-and-file committees — Samuel Davidson

The USW tentative agreement with US Steel covers 11,200 workers at 13 facilities in six states. The previous contract expired September 1. It offers raises of 5, 4, 4 and 5% over four years, with inflation running at about 4%, plus a $4,000 ratification bonus. The lowest rate rises only from $28.53 to $29.96. Premium-free healthcare is kept, but deductibles rise to $300/$600 and ER copays to $125. Workers get summaries with their ballots and must go to local offices to read the full text. Davidson contrasts these terms with the company’s position: - Profits: Q3 adjusted EBITDA of $740 million, up 84%. - CEO pay: David Burritt took about $108.8 million cashing out stock after Nippon Steel’s takeover. - Investment: A nearly $3 billion hot strip mill at Edgar Thomson. - Safety: Five deaths at US Steel and Cleveland-Cliffs in about 15 months. All seven Chemical Safety Board recommendations from the 2025 Clairton explosion remain open. - Jobs: US Steel’s North American workforce fell from about 100,000 in 1980 to just over 14,000.

The USW’s economic nationalism ties workers to the company’s and Washington’s trade-war aims. “The USW no longer functions as an organization for the defense of workers’ interests.” Burritt’s own verdict: “Our best days are ahead of us.” The article calls for rejection, full text distribution, and committees linking US Steel and Cleveland-Cliffs workers with National Steel Car, Stelco and the locked-out refinery workers.

Harvard graduate strike enters fifth week, after UAW sends faculty back without a contract — Mike Ingram

More than 4,000 HGSU-UAW members are in the fifth week of their second strike this year and have been without a contract for 17 months. The UAW ended the one-week strike of about 2,600 Harvard Academic Workers on an offer of 7% plus a 1.5% bonus, then 3.5% a year, terms HAW’s own negotiators called inadequate. The graduate workers’ demands are pay matched to Boston living costs, protection for noncitizen workers against ICE, an independent harassment process, and reinstatement of workers cut from the bargaining unit. The UAW pays $550 a week from the second week, out of a strike fund of nearly $1 billion. Harvard’s endowment is about $68 billion. At MIT, graduate workers ratified 3.5%/3.25%/3.25% against the 6% they sought, and facilities workers who voted over 98% to strike remain without a contract. Ingram’s point is that “the employer has faced its workers one group at a time” and “every pause in the struggle has been used to wear them down.” He calls for a mass membership meeting, full contract text before any vote, strike pay from day one, and joint action with Boston-area university and hospital workers.

Charlestown High School students walk out to oppose Boston school closures — Andrew Timon

Charlestown students marched to the Bunker Hill Monument in their second walkout this month. Mayor Michelle Wu’s Boston Public Schools wants to close Charlestown High, Greater Egleston High (76 students) and the East Boston Early Education Center, cutting the district from 104 schools toward 95 by 2030. The School Committee votes December 2, and closures would take effect in June 2028. Over 500 teachers will be laid off before 2027–28. The closures affect 776 students and 193 educators. Nearly one in nine BPS students (more than 5,400) were homeless in 2024–25, and English learners are 33.6% of enrollment. Charter enrollment has risen almost sixfold since 2000. Closed buildings go to developers: the former William Barton Rogers School was sold for $100 and its “income-restricted” units rent for up to $2,682. BTU President Eric Berg asked only for mergers and more time, which Timon describes as accepting the closure framework. Teacher strikes are illegal in Massachusetts, yet educators in 12 districts have struck in seven years. The article proposes rank-and-file committees of teachers, students and parents preparing for possible strike action next September, when the BTU contract runs out in 2027.

Results of Alberta’s October 19 referendum express absence of working-class alternative to the far-right UCP — Penny Smith

This week in history: November 23–29

  • 25 years ago (2001), German Greens back the Afghanistan war: At their Rostock conference on November 24–25, more than two-thirds of about 700 delegates endorsed the Bundestag decision to send up to 3,900 soldiers to the US-led war. Joschka Fischer was then foreign minister. Schröder had tied the deployment to a confidence vote and won 336 votes against the 334 needed. The token dissent of left Greens Ströbele and Buntenbach changed nothing. The WSWS tied the war to control of Central Asian and Caspian energy. It was a decisive step in the Greens’ transformation into a party of German militarism, a line that runs directly to their support for the current war against Russia.
  • 50 years ago (1976), the Çaldıran–Muradiye earthquake: A magnitude 7.3 quake on November 24 in Van province, near the Iranian and Soviet borders, killed an estimated 4,000–5,000 people and destroyed more than 80% of buildings over 2,000 km². About 250,000 survivors were left in freezing conditions. The Third Army, stationed nearby, initially supplied 400 tents and 470 blankets. The WSWS called the death toll a social crime of shoddy construction and state neglect.
  • 75 years ago (1951), Shishakli’s coup in Syria: On November 29, Colonel Adib Shishakli overthrew Prime Minister Maarouf al-Dawalibi’s government after Dawalibi refused to make Fawzi Selu defense minister. President Hashim al-Atassi resigned and Selu was installed as a figurehead. Parliament was dissolved and parties suppressed. It was Syria’s fourth coup since 1946, part of the US drive, backed by the CIA, to displace Britain and France, secure the TAPLINE oil route and suppress the Communist Party. Shishakli formally took the presidency in 1953 and was overthrown in 1954.
  • 100 years ago (1926), Zinoviev removed from the Comintern: On November 23, the Seventh Enlarged Plenum of the ECCI removed Grigory Zinoviev, Comintern chairman since 1919, after his bloc with Trotsky in the United Opposition. Bukharin was elevated. The plenum endorsed “socialism in one country” against permanent revolution, a turning point in converting the Comintern from the general staff of world revolution into an instrument of Soviet foreign policy. Zinoviev was expelled from the party in 1927, capitulated, and was shot after the first Moscow Trial in 1936.

Jacobin / DSA

  • “Los Angeles Socialists Won Big in the Blue Electoral Wave” (Chris Kutalik, November 22). A roll call of DSA-LA wins:
    • Mayor: Nithya Raman defeated incumbent Karen Bass.
    • City Attorney: Marissa Roy won with about 68%.
    • Congress: Angela Gonzales-Torres beat Democratic incumbent Jimmy Gomez with more than 62%.
    • Local: DSA now holds a majority on Burbank’s council, and won council seats in West Hollywood and seats on school boards.
    Kutalik is a former DSA communications director and former Labor Notes editor. He presents these wins as proof of a “democratic road to socialism” that combines elections with movement organizing. The key political fact goes unstated: the article never says whether these candidates ran in Democratic primaries or under the Democratic label, even as it places them inside the “blue wave” that gave Democrats Congress. It also notes “tension” between Mayor Raman and the chapter over BDS. That is an early sign of the Mamdani pattern documented by the WSWS’s New York interviews, in which DSA officials govern capitalist cities and the organization manages the fallout. The perspective here is the conquest of offices in the capitalist state by a faction of the Democratic Party, not the independent mobilization of the working class.
  • “What the ’60s Left Gave Us.” The opening credits the New Left with helping to end the Vietnam War and changing American culture “for the better.” It is a nostalgic, national retrospective that reads the period through the cultural gains of the middle class rather than the role of the working class, the mass strikes of 1967–74, or the Stalinist and Maoist politics that disoriented the movement. (Only the opening could be read.)

Assessment: On a weekend of record Democratic gains and immediate Democratic capitulation on war funding, Jacobin is celebrating the DSA’s growing presence in the Democratic coalition. It has nothing to say about the retreat on Iran.

Left Voice

  • “Shenzhen Summit: The Stakes of the Meeting Between Xi, Putin, and Trump Amid the Return of Class Struggle” (André Barbieri and Julien Anchaing, November 22, reprinted from CRP Asia). The authors reject the “new Yalta” analogy and argue the summit “primarily [confirms] the accelerating decline of the United States.” They say “class struggle is emerging as a more decisive factor than geopolitics,” citing France, Spain, Italy, Bangladeshi textile strikes and wildcats in China. That is correct as far as it goes. But China is described only as “the rising capitalist superpower” and Russia and China as “equally reactionary,” while the term “imperialist” is reserved for the US and Europe. Treating Beijing and Moscow as rivals in a “multipolar” contest rather than analyzing them on the basis of the world economy blurs the task of uniting workers across all three countries against all three governments. The article does not mention the Fourth International or a revolutionary party.
  • “Neither What Fascism Is Nor How to Fight It” (Nathaniel Flakin, November 22), a review of Naomi Klein and Astra Taylor’s new book. Flakin correctly faults its ideological, classless definition of fascism. He rejects its program of “multiracial social democracy” and its New Deal model as schemes to save capitalism. He quotes Horkheimer: “Whoever is not willing to talk about capitalism should also keep quiet about fascism.” He also notes that the book praises Mamdani for working with Gov. Hochul. But calling Trump and Bolsonaro unstable Bonapartist governments rather than fascist ones also serves to minimize the danger. And Flakin’s alternative, working-class self-organization, leaves out the question of building the party.

Assessment: Left Voice’s analysis is sharper than Jacobin’s, but it is centrist. It offers revolutionary phrases on class struggle and fascism without a clear stand on Chinese and Russian capitalism and without the question of revolutionary leadership.
