1. Iran strikes US bases in Bahrain as tanker convoy prepares to run the blockade

On Saturday night Iran launched two waves of drone and ballistic missile attacks on four US military sites in Bahrain, which hosts the headquarters of the US Fifth Fleet. Iran’s semi-official Fars agency named Sheikh Isa air base and Juffair as targets and called the strikes a response to continued US incursions into Iranian airspace. The Revolutionary Guards (IRGC) claimed to have destroyed two Patriot batteries and a radar system. Neither Bahrain nor the US military has confirmed this. Bahrain told residents to shelter, said its air defenses intercepted “several” attacks, and called them a “heinous Iranian aggression.” Its Interior Ministry reported a fire at a civilian industrial compound and no injuries. Soon afterward, explosions were reported in Sirik in southern Iran. Euronews notes that US forces have struck Iranian military sites regularly in recent weeks.

The attacks came as Iran prepares to break the US naval blockade of its ports. More than 14 Iranian tankers are reportedly assembled off Kharg Island. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi called the blockade “an act of war” and said Washington had been told the convoy would soon sail for China. Tehran warned of a “fierce military response,” including widespread strikes on Gulf energy infrastructure, if US forces engage the ships. The Institute for the Study of War (ISW) set out the choice facing Washington: let the blockade collapse, or go back to open war at a high political cost and with strained ties to Gulf allies. In Vice President JD Vance’s words after the November 3 midterms, quoted by Andre Damon on the WSWS, “the reason we lost is the war.”

Key facts: - Shipping: At least three tankers were attacked in or near the Strait of Hormuz on Saturday. UKMTO reported two hit by projectiles while leaving the Gulf. Iran’s navy said a third struck a mine east of the strait and caught fire. Iranian state media described the military’s overnight operations to stop vessels as its most “intense and varied” yet. - CENTCOM denied an Iranian claim that a surface-to-air missile damaged a US F-16 on Friday evening. - Army spokesman Brig. Gen. Mohammad Akraminia warned that any attack would bring a “decisive and immediate response” that would not be “limited to one geographic area.” State TV showed ground troops in coastal defensive positions for the first time. - Diplomacy: Qatar is mediating, and a Qatari delegation held about two hours of talks in Tehran on Friday. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said reopening Hormuz would be “not difficult” if the US ended the “completely illegal” blockade, lifted sanctions and acknowledged “past mistakes.” - Trump, speaking to Fox News: “I intend to reach a permanent deal with Iran that prevents them from obtaining nuclear weapons.” He added that he was never interested in regime change. - President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran faces a “silent economic war,” harder than a missile war, and that it can withstand it.

2. Russia answers the Kerch Bridge strike with mass attacks on Kyiv and Kryvyi Rih

Russia carried out the “painful” response Putin promised after Ukraine’s strikes on the Kerch Bridge. Putin made that pledge after Thursday’s Shenzhen summit with Trump and Xi. Zelensky says Russia fired more than 400 attack drones and over 110 missiles overnight from Thursday into Friday, calling it “one of the largest attacks” of the war. Euronews puts the Kyiv death toll at six to seven across its reports, and key bridges over the Dnipro were damaged. A Dutch embassy building in Kyiv was damaged by nearby debris on November 19.

On Saturday a ballistic missile hit Kryvyi Rih, Zelensky’s hometown. It killed a seven-year-old boy, Tykhon, and a 20-year-old woman who worked as an emergency medic. About 60 people were injured, six of them children. City administration head Oleksandr Vilkul said the strike started four fires and damaged about 25 apartment blocks, a hospital, schools, an industrial site and businesses. Zelensky said the attack was launched “in a highly demonstrative manner.” He described Putin’s attempt to “redraw” the world with Xi and Trump as a failure.

Key facts: - Other Russian targets (per Zelensky): Kharkiv, Donetsk, Dnipro, Rivne, Chernihiv, Mykolaiv and Zaporizhzhia regions. A strike on Krolevets railway station in Sumy region injured three. - Ukrainian strikes (General Staff): an ammunition depot near Pervomaiske in occupied Crimea, a bridge in Luhansk, and two command posts in Zaporizhzhia and Donetsk regions. Ukraine also hit the Ilsky and Afipsky refineries in Krasnodar Krai. - Civilian toll: The UN recorded 372 Ukrainian civilians killed and 1,981 injured in October. That is up 95% on October 2025 (per ISW). - Attrition: Ukraine’s General Staff claims Russian losses averaged about 2,269 a day in November, above Russia’s recruitment rate. ISW treats Russian recruitment figures as inflated.

3. Drone strike on Russian ship in Gulf of Finland caps a week of NATO-Russia incidents

On Saturday morning drones struck the Russian cargo ship Sparta II in international waters west of Suursaari island in the Gulf of Finland, and a fire broke out on board. The US sanctioned the vessel in May 2022, and Ukraine has also sanctioned it. Leningrad Oblast Governor Aleksandr Drozdenko said two drones hit the ship while it was sailing to Kaliningrad. It later drifted into Finnish territorial waters south of Loviisa. The Finnish Border Guard took charge of rescue and pollution control. The crew had not abandoned ship and no environmental damage was detected. Nobody has claimed the attack. Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo nonetheless said it showed the war “spilling over into our neighboring regions,” and added that “Ukraine has the right to fight back against the aggressor.” That afternoon a Russian helicopter was suspected of violating Finnish airspace off Loviisa. Defense Minister Antti Häkkänen suggested the flight “may be related to rescue or monitoring operations.”

On Friday President Macron warned of a “new level” of Russian aggression. “Over the past 24 hours, several of our European partners and allies have once again faced serious incidents,” he wrote, and said Moscow was “taking an irresponsible gamble that can only further isolate it.” The incidents he and others cited: - Poland: Polish authorities said two Russian missiles and a jet-powered drone crashed on Polish territory during “Koalicja 26.2.” This allied “Coalition of the Willing” exercise was taking place at the Nowa Dęba training ground. - Estonia: All five road crossings with Russia close on December 1. Tallinn cites a “GRU-ordered” sabotage attempt last week against drone maker Threod Systems. Residents have been rushing to cross before the deadline. - Denmark: The Security and Intelligence Service (PET) accused Russian intelligence of a September assassination attempt on a Ukrainian American citizen north of Copenhagen. - Moldova: Moldova closed its airspace after an attack drone was detected heading toward Chișinău. President Maia Sandu said “several Russian drones” entered on Tuesday evening. - Kaliningrad: ISW relays Polish press claims that a Russian false-flag plot involving Kaliningrad was foiled.

The incidents follow Berlin’s “Coalition of the Willing” summit. Each one is being used to justify the rearmament drive that the European youth strikes opposed on November 20.

4. Sixth Saturday of protest in France as Lisbon and Spain march over living costs

The day after the European youth strike and Italy’s general strike, workers marched again on Saturday in France, Portugal and Spain over wages, housing and public services.

France: This was the sixth straight Saturday of national demonstrations over living costs, student rights and police violence. It fell on the eighth anniversary of the Yellow Vest movement. The Interior Ministry counted 82,500 marchers nationwide, 21,500 of them in Paris. Organizers claimed 150,000, with 45,000 in Paris. Both counts were lower than the week before, when authorities reported 106,000 and organizers 200,000. The state deployed about 70,000 police and security personnel. In Lyon, demonstrations were banned across a large area, and police fired tear gas at unauthorized marches heading for the city center (AFP). In Marseille police counted 4,600 marchers, and one group broke off into an unauthorized march.

Portugal: Thousands marched through Lisbon in heavy rain, called by the CGTP-IN union federation and arriving on special trains from Porto and buses from Aveiro and Faro. They demanded higher wages and pensions, affordable housing and lower prices, and opposed the government’s 2027 budget. House prices have more than doubled since 2017, and Eurostat puts Portuguese average earnings 37% below the EU average. A CGTP-IN campaign video said: “They talk about the economy, but they don’t know what it’s like to stretch every penny to pay the rent and electricity bills.” A rally is set for Tuesday, November 24, outside the Assembly of the Republic during the final budget vote.

Spain: In Barcelona, police counted 3,000 marchers and organizers 5,000, demanding housing and better-funded public education. In Madrid, a march organized with the Marea de Residencias demanded truth, justice and compensation for the 7,291 elderly people who died in the region’s care homes in the first Covid wave of 2020. Marchers also demanded full public ownership of care homes. They denounced protocols under Isabel Díaz Ayuso’s regional government that restricted hospital transfers for residents. The regional government defends its handling of the crisis.

As the WSWS reporters in Italy put it, the task is to turn these separate national days of action into “a European general strike” that poses the question of power, against union leaderships that keep them divided and limited to one day.

5. COP31 runs into overtime after draft deletes every reference to fossil fuels

The COP31 climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, ran past their scheduled close into Saturday. The trigger was a second draft text, released Thursday, that removed every mention of fossil fuels and other energy sources. An earlier draft circulated during the week had called for a faster phase-out. On Friday France, Panama and several other states circulated a letter demanding that any deal include “a clear transition away from fossil fuels.” The EU said the revised text risked “turning this climate COP into a fossil summit.” A final text was expected Saturday. Nothing published by the time this briefing was compiled confirmed a deal.

Key facts: - Murat Kurum, COP31 president and Turkey’s environment minister: “We are close to the finish line, but we are not there yet.” - Chris Bowen, Australia’s climate minister, who is leading the negotiations, said he would keep working “for as long as it takes.” - Juan Carlos Navarro, Panama’s environment minister, threatened to walk out and blamed a “petroleum mafia.” - OPEC Secretary General Haitham al-Ghais met Russian climate envoy Ruslan Edelgeriev on the sidelines. Both are among the strongest opponents of fossil fuel language. - Context: Nearly 200 countries are negotiating as the Iran war and the Hormuz blockade drive a scramble for fossil supply. The US House is about to take up a Senate permitting bill that would fast-track gas plants for data centers (see United States).

6. UN killer-robot talks end without binding rules

A week of talks at the UN in Geneva on lethal autonomous weapons ended past midnight with a compromise announced at 4:30 a.m. Saturday. Countries have two more years to propose regulations, which may be either legally binding or voluntary. AI-enabled weapons that make life-and-death decisions will therefore remain unregulated until at least late 2028. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), backed by the UN secretary-general and Pope Leo, had called for binding rules within a year. After the deal the ICRC said it “regretted” the outcome. One delegate called it “just kicking the can down the road,” and campaigners called it a “nothing burger.”

Key facts: - The US proposed five years of talks on nonbinding, optional guidelines. On the first day Russia delayed proceedings by objecting to the agenda. The US, Russia and Israel had all signaled resistance to binding rules. - The UN reports drones caused 80% of civilian deaths in Sudan this year. Semi-autonomous drones are killing civilians in Ukraine, Sudan and Gaza. - Georgia Hinds, ICRC legal adviser, called algorithmic killing a “moral red line” and said “a machine cannot be held accountable for a violation of the law.” - Anna Hehir of the Future of Life Institute warned that, unlike nuclear weapons, autonomous weapons are easy to buy or build, including for non-state and criminal groups. - Context: The WSWS reported this week, drawing on Anthropic’s own prospectus, that the company’s Claude model has been used to select more than 1,000 targets in the war on Iran.

EU’s Costa proposes 204 billion euro budget cut and a digital levy

European Council President António Costa issued a new draft of the EU’s 2028–2034 Multiannual Financial Framework ahead of a special summit in Brussels on Thursday. The draft cuts a further €49 billion to satisfy the “frugal” states (Germany, the Netherlands, Sweden and others), who now call themselves “modernisers.” That brings the total to €1.78 trillion, down from the Commission’s nearly €2 trillion, a cut of €204 billion or 10%. The frugals had demanded “several hundred billion.” Merz said there would be no agreement without unanimous financial support for Ukraine, which drew backlash from several member states. An Élysée source said the plan “does not constitute a basis for an agreement.” Elections in France, Italy and Poland could delay a deal.

Key facts: - Cuts by heading: cohesion, agriculture and fisheries lose €54 billion, the first time cohesion funds have been cut in a compromise proposal. Competitiveness and defense lose €95.5 billion (16%), “Global Europe” external aid loses €45 billion (21%), and administration is cut to €108 billion. - New revenue, €64 billion a year: a 3% levy on online advertising, intermediation and user-data revenue for firms with more than €50 million in EU turnover and €750 million globally. It replaces the planned corporate resource levy. Parliament estimates the levy at up to €25 billion a year and the Commission at €5 billion. Costa’s version is closer to the Commission’s figure. Most of the firms it covers are American, which raises the risk of retaliation from Washington. - Other own resources: 30% of Emissions Trading System revenue, phased in through 2034 and strongly opposed by Poland. Also a minimum 15% tobacco tax, higher plastic and e-waste levies, 100% of carbon border levy revenue, and 5G/6G spectrum sales. - Joint debt: NextGenerationEU repayments for 2028–2034 are cut by €10 billion. Repayment still runs to 2058. - A 17-member “Friends of Cohesion” bloc from southern and eastern Europe is resisting cuts to regional funds for poorer areas.

The draft spares rearmament financed at the national level and shifts EU cuts onto aid to the poorest regions and onto external development spending.

Mexico captures “El Chapo Isidro” in Sinaloa raid that leaves 11 dead

On Friday night Mexican National Guard and Army special units captured Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, “El Chapo Isidro,” in Guasave, Sinaloa. He is on the FBI’s Ten Most Wanted list, with a $5 million US reward, and heads the Guasave Cartel, an offshoot of the Beltrán Leyva organization. Troops came under fire from gunmen using firearms and explosives. The Defense Cabinet said seven suspected cartel gunmen and four soldiers were killed. Eight people were arrested and flown to Mexico City on an Air Force plane. Gun battles continued into Saturday morning. Burning trucks were used as roadblocks, gunfire broke up a baseball game in Guasave, and residents were told to stay indoors.

  • US role: Mexico City says intelligence from the Trump administration contributed but gave no detail. The outlet N+ reported joint work between Mexico’s Defense Ministry intelligence and US agencies.
  • Context: The arrest follows the Army’s capture of a top Jalisco New Generation cartel boss in April and the death earlier this year of Jalisco leader “El Mencho.”
  • Euronews notes that removing a leader “does not necessarily mean the dismantling of the organisation” and risks a succession war in Sinaloa.

Saudi-Houthi war escalates as Yemen’s children pay the price

Saudi Arabia said on Friday that the Houthis had fired more missiles and drones at the kingdom, days after Riyadh launched a major air campaign across Houthi-held Yemen (AP). UNICEF reports that at least 64 children have been killed and 64 injured since hostilities intensified on September 3. More than 330,000 people have been displaced, and over 800,000 in Taiz are cut off from aid. The war is an extension of the US-Iran confrontation, and it is drawing the Gulf monarchies directly into the fighting. Separately, AP reported that Iraq reached a compromise with an Iran-backed militia it had accused of oil smuggling.

Weekend reckoning after Wall Street’s worst week since 2020

Markets are closed for the weekend. The AP’s final tallies confirm the scale of Friday’s rout and settle the discrepancies between sources in yesterday’s figures. The Nasdaq lost 10% on the week, its worst since the March 2020 lockdowns, and is now negative for 2026. The S&P 500 fell seven sessions in a row, its longest losing streak since the pandemic began, and is now more than 10% below its October 6 record. The previous correction was in March 2025, during the tariff war. Chip stocks led the collapse. Nvidia fell 13.8%, its ninth loss in ten sessions and more than 25% since the Jensen Huang scandal broke. AMD fell 13.3%, Micron 13.9%, Broadcom 12.1% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index 10.7%.

NPR’s business desk set out how the losses could spread. Nvidia owns stakes in AI firms such as Anthropic, lends them money, and sells them its chips. Heavily indebted data-center builders such as CoreWeave, in which Nvidia is a major investor, raise the risk that one failure triggers “something close to a financial crisis.” AI investment accounted for much of the economy’s 3.4% annualized growth last quarter, and AI stocks are heavily represented in pensions and 401(k)s. The Fed raised rates in September for the first time in over three years, which raises the cost of the debt-financed data-center buildout. The sell-off is spreading to workers’ retirement savings even as the companies at its center use AI to justify job cuts.

Trump orders federal contractors to clear responses to Congress with the White House

An executive order issued late Friday directs all federal contractors and subcontractors to consult the administration about congressional inquiries. They must withhold communications the White House considers privileged and coordinate “appropriate responses” with the White House and agencies. Firms that do not comply face contract termination and suspension from future federal work. The order covers tens of thousands of businesses. A White House fact sheet presents it as “protecting executive privileges from congressional overreach” by lawmakers who harass contractors.

  • Target: Democrats take control of Congress in January and have named federal contracting, especially Pentagon money flowing to firms tied to Trump’s family, as a top investigative priority.
  • Earlier step: On Monday, White House counsel Will Scharf told agencies to get approval from his office and the Office of Legislative Affairs before agreeing to appear before Congress. In a letter to Rep. Robert Garcia, Scharf suggested some congressional investigations may amount to “blatant criminality.”
  • Garcia, the top Democrat on House Oversight, called the order a “blatant attack on Congress’ constitutional right to conduct oversight.” This week he sent document-preservation letters to Trump family members and senior officials.

The order uses the federal government’s purchasing power to discipline private companies and claims executive privilege over the communications of private firms. Democratic leaders have already ruled out impeachment, and Andre Damon reports on the WSWS that they have also sided with the White House against Rep. Thomas Massie’s impeachment drive against War Secretary Hegseth.

Senate passes data-center permitting bill 62–33

On Thursday the Senate passed the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (S. 5653) by 62–33, with 14 Democrats and one independent joining most Republicans. Two Democrats and two Republicans wrote it. The bill speeds approval of data centers and power plants. Supporters pitch it as relief from electricity prices, which are up more than 6% in a year and 39% in five years, both faster than overall inflation. It now goes to the House. Trump called it a “BIG WIN” for “energy dominance.”

  • Sen. Shelley Moore Capito (R-WV): a “major step” on affordability. Majority Leader John Thune: it will save households “hundreds of dollars a year.”
  • Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI), co-author: says data centers will “pay for the power they use.”
  • Opponents: Sen. Jeff Merkley (D-OR) called it “a bad deal” that unleashes “a flood of natural gas for data centers” while renewable transmission stays subject to Trump’s obstruction. Sens. Cantwell and Padilla warned about the Clean Water Act, sacred sites and tribal sovereignty. The Sierra Club and National Wildlife Federation oppose it.

The vote came just after the midterms, in which opposition to data centers crossed party lines. A substantial bloc of Democrats nonetheless joined the Republicans in passing it.

Bayer wins final approval of $7.25 billion Roundup settlement

On Friday, Missouri circuit judge Timothy Boyer gave final approval to Bayer’s nationwide settlement with people who say the weedkiller Roundup caused their non-Hodgkin lymphoma. It is worth up to $7.25 billion over several years. Boyer called it “among the largest in the history of mass tort personal-injury class action settlements.” Individual awards run from $10,000 to $165,000, depending on occupational exposure, age at diagnosis and other factors.

  • Bayer, which owns Monsanto, previously paid about $10–11 billion to settle more than 100,000 claims. It still denies that glyphosate causes cancer. The WHO’s cancer agency classified it as “probably carcinogenic to humans” more than a decade ago.
  • Earlier this year the Supreme Court ruled that Bayer could be shielded from state-court failure-to-warn suits. The Trump administration sided with Bayer in that case and issued an executive order promoting domestic glyphosate production. The EPA says it found no cancer risk of concern and is reviewing the evidence.
  • CEO Bill Anderson called the deal a “decisive milestone” that will “mitigate the risks” of litigation. Glyphosate is no longer in Roundup sold for home use, but agricultural workers remain the most exposed.

Federal jury convicts man of starting the Palisades Fire

On Friday a federal jury in Los Angeles convicted Jonathan Rinderknecht, 30, of arson and destruction of property by fire. The charges cover the January 1, 2025 Lachman Fire, which smoldered in root systems and flared up on January 7 as the Palisades Fire. That fire killed 12 people and destroyed whole neighborhoods in Los Angeles and Malibu. His first trial in June ended with a jury deadlocked 10–2 for acquittal. Sentencing is set for March 17, 2027. Each count carries 5 to 20 years.

  • In the retrial prosecutors dropped the theme of his resentment of “the rich and powerful,” which they had earlier presented as “societal revenge.” They relied instead on cellphone and camera data and on his ChatGPT query “Are you at fault if a fire is lit because of your cigarettes?”
  • The defense argued the scene was compromised and pointed to neighbors who reported a bang and teenagers running from the trailhead. It is considering an appeal.
  • US Attorney Bill Essayli accused the first jury of acting improperly.

The verdict assigns individual guilt for a disaster whose scale was shaped by drought, hurricane-force winds and a water system that failed firefighters.

Democratic gains bring the popular-vote compact within reach of 270

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact now covers 18 Democratic-led states and DC, with 222 electoral votes. It takes effect at 270. Next year Arizona, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Nevada and Wisconsin will all be under full Democratic control, with 61 electoral votes between them, and any four would take the compact past the threshold. Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro and Nevada’s incoming governor say they are open to joining. Arizona Gov. Katie Hobbs, a past supporter, has gone quiet. Pew finds 63% of Americans support a national popular vote. Opponents argue the compact needs congressional consent under the Constitution’s compact clause. Swing-state Democrats may also be reluctant to give up their leverage before 2028.

Storms on both coasts threaten Thanksgiving travel

Measles deaths reach 11 by state counts as PAHO declares the virus endemic again

The Pennsylvania Department of Health announced the state’s eighth measles death of 2026, an unvaccinated resident of rural Clinton County. The announcement came a day after the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) formally declared measles endemic in the US again, 26 years after it was eliminated. PAHO found that the same lineage has circulated in the US, Mexico and Bolivia for at least 12 months. State health departments report 11 deaths this year: eight in Pennsylvania, two in Wisconsin (including a child in Grant County this month) and one in New York. The CDC’s Friday update still lists six. The agency adds deaths only after its own review and is still drafting a case definition with state epidemiologists.

Key data: - National (CDC, as of November 19): up 426 cases in one week, across 47 jurisdictions and 43 outbreaks, with about 96% of cases outbreak-associated. 646 people have been hospitalized. - Pennsylvania: 1,589 cases, more than a quarter of the national total, in 46 of 67 counties, with 315 hospitalizations. The eight deaths were in Lancaster (3), Jefferson (2), Clinton, Mifflin and Montour counties. The outbreak has spread well beyond Lancaster’s Amish settlements. - Vaccination: Pennsylvania kindergarten MMR coverage fell from 95.5% (2020–21) to 92.7% (2025–26), while exemptions rose from 2.7% to 6%. - Official response: CDC Director Erica Schwartz says the loss of elimination status will not change the federal response. HHS Secretary Kennedy blames “worldwide COVID-era disruptions.” Trump’s August executive order calls for splitting MMR into three shots.

Anthropic researcher claims AI-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis

On Friday, Anthropic technical staff member Levent Alpöge said that he and Claude had found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. Formulated in 1859, the conjecture concerns the distribution of prime numbers. It is one of the Clay Institute’s seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a $1 million reward. Alpöge posted a four-page explanation. The proof itself is about 17 million lines of Lean, a language for machine-checked proofs, and he says more than 250,000 pages came out of his work with the AI. He acknowledged that he has not yet fully understood it, but argued that a proof that satisfies the computer very likely satisfies people too. Rutgers mathematician Alex Kontorovich said Claude had followed the code and judged the proof true, and called the moment an “out-of-body experience.” Clay Institute president Martin Bridson called the announcement “of historic significance” but said real understanding “will be long and difficult.”

The claim came the day after Anthropic’s stock market debut. According to AP, Alpöge had messaged CEO Dario Amodei about his “ability to announce” as the company prepared its listing. In September OpenAI claimed its technology was key to a breakthrough on the Navier–Stokes equations. If the proof holds, it is a historic achievement of machine-assisted reasoning. But a proof that no human has yet understood raises its own questions about what counts as mathematical knowledge. And a claim released in the middle of a $93 billion share sale raises questions about how science is being used to sell stock.

Grand Theft Auto VI and the commodity form of satire

Grand Theft Auto VI, Rockstar’s first new installment since 2013, dominated cultural coverage this week. NPR critic Jason Sheehan calls it a “masterpiece.” Sensor Tower puts pre-sales above $1.5 billion, and Newzoo projects $3–5 billion in first-week sales, which could make it the largest single entertainment launch in history. A work about being priced out of a decent life is now the most profitable entertainment commodity ever made. Its record launch comes in the same week Wall Street lost trillions on the AI bet.

LA Opera revives Bernstein’s Candide

LA Opera opened Leonard Bernstein’s Candide at the Dorothy Chandler Pavilion on Saturday. Scott Bakula and Patti LuPone lead the cast, and the run continues through December 13. Voltaire’s 1759 satire targeted the Leibnizian doctrine that this is “the best of all possible worlds,” a doctrine used to justify war, the Inquisition and slavery. Bernstein’s 1956 operetta used a libretto by Lillian Hellman, who had defied the House Un-American Activities Committee, and it was conceived as an attack on McCarthyite conformity. A satire of official optimism amid catastrophe is well timed. How much of that political edge this star-driven production keeps is the question for any review.
