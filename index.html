1. Trump threatens to destroy the rest of Iran as mines set five tankers ablaze in Hormuz

Trump said the US may have to “destroy” the rest of Iran. He claimed rebuilding the country would take 25 years and said people would “know soon enough” whether he acts. That day Iranian forces reported at least five oil tankers and a tugboat on fire in the Strait of Hormuz. On Wednesday the New York Times, citing unnamed Pentagon officials, reported that thousands of US air and naval personnel, on more than a dozen ships and at regional and US bases, were ready to resume major combat on Trump’s order. Iran has reportedly sent a nuclear proposal to Washington through Qatari mediators, and the US is reviewing it. Tehran says it will not reopen the strait or negotiate until its seven conditions are met.

All this follows a US strike early Tuesday on what Trump called an anti-aircraft facility. Iranian media placed it on Qeshm Island, beside the strait. Trump called it a “minor attack” that “wasn’t war.” Iran called Washington’s reply to its plan for reopening the strait “unconvincing.”

Key facts: - Thursday’s ship fires: The IRGC said a large tanker in an “illegal passageway” south of the strait hit a mine and burned. It said a supertanker leaving the eastern strait caught fire, and so did a tugboat sent to fight that blaze. The Iranian army separately reported three more tankers hit by naval mines east of the strait (Al Jazeera, citing AFP, AP and Reuters). - Off Qatar: UKMTO said a tanker was hit by two projectiles about 50 nautical miles north of Madinat ash-Shamal at 23:20 GMT on Wednesday. No group has claimed it. - Undercounting: UKMTO said last month it can no longer count attacks accurately because most ships now refuse to report them. - Strike plans: Before the midterms, US officials said Central Command had prepared plans for a “short and intense” campaign that could begin as early as Wednesday, with three US carriers in the region. Trump said he is “not looking to … kill lots of people” but “if we have to, we’ll have to.” - Iranian threats: Tehran says states that help a US attack are “legitimate targets.” Officials have named European countries and Gulf undersea cables as possible targets. - A doubting analyst: John Mearsheimer asked: “If the 40-day bombing campaign didn’t work, how is the three-day bombing campaign going to work?” - Iran’s claims: The IRGC says it shot down its 56th US MQ-9 drone over the strait.

The WSWS Perspective warns that the war “remains live.” It says the Democrats, now in control of Congress, “have not called for an end to hostilities.”

2. Wall Street loses $3.2 trillion in a day as the AI trade breaks

US stocks lost about $3.2 trillion in market value on Thursday, one of the biggest one-day falls this century. The S&P 500 fell 4.36 percent to 7,353.73, its worst day since the April 2025 “reciprocal” tariff shock. The Nasdaq Composite fell 5.52 percent and is now in a correction, more than 10 percent below last month’s record. The Nasdaq 100 fell 6.36 percent, its worst day since 2020, and the Dow lost 1,833 points. On Wednesday the market had surged on the midterm results. Thursday’s turn came as investors reassessed what the Democratic gains mean for the AI data-center buildout. Several incoming Democratic governors had campaigned on restricting data centers because of rising electricity and water costs.

Nick Beams writes on the WSWS that the political trigger was “the catalyst, not the root cause.” The root cause, he argues, is a speculative boom resting on debt and on profits that have not yet appeared.

Key facts: - Chips: The PHLX Semiconductor Index fell 12.25 percent. Nvidia fell 9.26 percent, losing more than $400 billion in value. 3. Democratic victors line up behind bipartisanship as Trump concedes the war cost him

With counting nearly finished, Democrats hold about 242 House seats and about 54 Senate seats to the Republicans’ 44, with Alaska and Mississippi uncalled. Trump told Fox News, “I think the Democrats did a good job. Key facts: - Breakthroughs: Hamilton beat incumbent Roger Marshall to become the first Democratic senator from Kansas in 94 years. Talarico beat Ken Paxton. Abdul El-Sayed beat Mike Rogers in Michigan to become the first Muslim elected to the Senate, after Trump called him a “jihadist.” In Maine, Troy Jackson unseated Susan Collins. - Governors: Keisha Lance Bottoms (Georgia) beat Rick Jackson, who spent more than $200 million of his own money. 5. France’s one-day strike draws up to 1.6 million as arrests since September pass 12,400

Unions held more than 260 protests across France on Thursday against rising prices and the €54 billion in cuts in Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu’s budget. The Interior Ministry counted 575,000 marchers nationwide, 65,000 of them in Paris. The CGT counted 1.6 million, 350,000 of them in Paris, across 193 cities. The government deployed 70,000 police and gendarmes. Police arrested 448 people on the day, bringing arrests since the movement began in September to more than 12,400.

Key facts: - Gaps between counts: In Marseille police counted 14,000 and the CGT 150,000. In Toulouse the figures were 21,800 and 80,000, and in Lyon 30,500 and 55,000. - Schools: The SNES-FSU union said 52 percent of middle and high school staff struck. The Education Ministry said 13.5 percent in secondary schools and 19.1 percent in primary. - Transport and power: Nine in ten TGVs ran. The Lyon metro stopped and Bordeaux transit was largely paralysed. Nuclear output fell by 2.4 gigawatts, and France imported electricity for several hours. - Repression: Police used water cannon and armoured vehicles in Rennes. A teacher suffered a head injury when police broke up a school blockade in Aubervilliers. - Who stayed out: The unions did not call out refineries, trucking or airlines, sectors that had struck militantly in recent years. - Next steps: Lecornu has convened a “social conference” with unions and employers on Monday. Strikes follow in Spain (November 11), Italy (November 20) and Greece (November 25).

WSWS reporters write that the action “remained largely within the public sector” despite being billed as a general strike. They argue that a real general strike must be prepared by committees of action independent of the union bureaucracies.

6. Turkey ratifies the Mecca defence pact, binding a NATO army to the Saudi monarchy

Turkey’s parliament ratified the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan on Thursday, by 279 votes to 119, with 398 of its 592 members voting. Turkey has NATO’s second-largest army. Its Article 2 promises any member under attack immediate help with “the full range of mutual assistance measures,” mirroring NATO’s Article 5, and members cannot withdraw for 10 years. Ratification came as Saudi Arabia faces escalating Houthi attacks. On Thursday Riyadh said it intercepted a drone near Medina.

Key facts: - Article 1: Signatories will use “all necessary means” against threats to one another’s territory. - Other articles: The 16-article pact also covers defence-industry cooperation, joint training, logistics and intelligence sharing. New members may join with the existing members’ approval, and Syria has expressed interest. - Turkish deployment: Experts say sending troops to Saudi Arabia would need further legislation. Defence Minister Yaşar Güler said in October that Turkey does not foresee “entering into any conflict with the Houthis.” - NATO question: Officials dismiss concerns about clashing NATO and Mecca obligations. Deputy Foreign Minister Levent Gümrükçü calls the pact complementary to NATO, and Trump praised it as a “big, bold and important first step.” - The Houthi war: Recent Houthi strikes have included deadly attacks on Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport.

Ulaş Sevinç of the Sosyalist Eşitlik Partisi had called for unconditional opposition before the vote, arguing that the pact ties Turkish workers to the Saudi regime, which has opened its bases to the US war on Iran.

7. Newsom commutes all 548 California death sentences

On Thursday Governor Gavin Newsom announced he will commute the sentences of everyone on California’s death row, 548 people, to life without parole. The Death Penalty Information Center calls it the largest mass clemency in US history. California has the country’s largest death row, so the move cuts the national total by nearly 30 percent. In an open letter Newsom wrote that “state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California’s values.”

Key facts: - Cost: California has spent about $6 billion on the death penalty since 1978 and carried out 13 executions. It has executed no one in 20 years. - Court review: About two-thirds of the commutations go to the California Supreme Court before they become final, because state law requires review for people with multiple felony convictions. - Exonerations: At least 203 people sentenced to death in the US have been exonerated since 1973 (DPIC). - Federal policy: In April the Trump administration pledged to expand federal executions, including by firing squad. - Newsom’s record: He leaves office in January and is a likely 2028 presidential candidate. He vetoed a 2022 bill limiting solitary confinement and a 2019 bill limiting state prisons’ cooperation with ICE.

Life without parole still means death in prison for all 548. The measure changes the form of that punishment rather than ending it, and it comes from a governor positioning himself for 2028.

Justice Department sues Penn State over tuition for undocumented students

On Thursday the Justice Department sued Pennsylvania State University over its policy of offering in-state tuition to undocumented students who attended and graduated from a Pennsylvania high school for at least four years. The suit asks a court to bar the policy and any future policy giving undocumented students “preferential treatment” over citizens. It relies on the 1996 welfare “reform” law (the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act). “There are no exceptions,” the complaint states.

  • The campaign: This is the department’s 27th such suit. Courts have permanently blocked similar policies in Texas, Kentucky, Oklahoma, Nebraska, Illinois and Kansas.
  • Who is affected: The students, mostly brought to the US as children, have grown up and been educated in the state.
  • Penn State: The university says it is “reviewing” the complaint.

Guinea-Bissau ends a US-funded trial that would have delayed hepatitis B shots for infants

Guinea-Bissau has halted a US-funded hepatitis B vaccine trial on newborns. A researcher on the project confirmed it will not proceed without government approval and called its end a “significant setback” for African science. About 14,000 infants were to be randomised to the vaccine at birth or six weeks later and followed for five years. The CDC, under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., provided $1.6 million. The WHO called the trial “unethical,” and critics compared it to Tuskegee.

  • Disease burden: About one-fifth of Guinea-Bissau’s 2.2 million people have hepatitis B. Infants are at highest risk of chronic infection, which leads to liver damage and cancer.
  • The researchers: The Danish-led Bandim Health Project designed the trial. It studies the “non-specific” effects of vaccines and has been accused in Denmark of withholding unfavourable results, including null results from a 14-year-old DTP trial.
  • The design: The trial was open-label, so researchers knew who was vaccinated, and it exploited a dose shortage that permits delayed vaccination until 2028.
  • The end: Police raided Bandim facilities on October 19 and seized vaccine batches. The transitional government, in power since a November coup, says it never approved the trial.

The trial fits the US health secretary’s campaign to prove vaccine harm. Its subjects were to be babies in one of the world’s poorest countries.

Waiting for Lefty (1935), written for the Group Theatre and set at a taxi drivers’ union meeting, ends with the audience called to its feet with the cry “Strike!” In the 1930s it was staged in union halls across the country. Awake and Sing! (1935) shows a Jewish family in the Bronx crushed by the Depression. One character tells another to “go out and fight so life shouldn’t be printed on dollar bills.”

That both plays are back amid the cost-of-living crisis, mass strikes in Europe and an electoral revolt against Trump says something about the mood in the theatre audience. Odets’s later path, from the Group Theatre to Hollywood and to naming names before HUAC in 1952, is also worth remembering. It shows how the political pressures of the Popular Front and McCarthy years broke a generation of left-wing artists. Productions that present Lefty as period nostalgia will miss what made it explosive: it showed that a rank-and-file meeting could overturn a corrupt union leadership.
