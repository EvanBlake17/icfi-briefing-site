1. Hot US inflation report fuels rate-hike fears three weeks before midterms

The Bureau of Labor Statistics said Wednesday that consumer prices rose 0.6 percent in September and 3.7 percent over the year, up from 3.4 percent in August and above the 3.6 percent consensus. Gasoline rose 9.1 percent in the month and made up more than half the increase. The report revived expectations that the Federal Reserve will raise rates again. It also set off a tech sell-off on Wall Street that spread to Asia overnight (see World economy).

The political effects are direct. The midterms are November 3, and a Reuters/Ipsos poll finds about 78 percent of Americans blame Trump’s policies for rising prices. In the WSWS Perspective, Patrick Martin argues that workers “pay for the war twice”: at the pump, and through interest payments and social cuts that send money to bondholders.

Key facts: - Energy: Gasoline is up 34.2 percent year on year, energy overall 20.4 percent and fuel oil 74.2 percent. The fuel oil rise hits Northeastern households just as the heating season starts (BLS via WSWS). - Pump prices (AAA): Gasoline averages $4.33 a gallon and diesel $6.29. According to GasBuddy’s Patrick DeHaan, 2026 has already had 158 days with average gas above $4, more than any previous year. - Real wages: Down 0.5 percent in one month and 0.7 percent over the year. - Other components: Shelter rose 0.4 percent in the month. Food was flat overall. Computer software and accessories are up 28 percent over the year, which Parabellum’s Rami Cassis attributes to the AI boom. - The Fed: It raised rates by 25 basis points in September to 3.75–4.00 percent, its first hike in three years. CME FedWatch puts the odds of holding at 52.9 percent and of another quarter-point hike at 47.1 percent. - Household strain (WSWS Perspective): The 30-year mortgage rate is 7.4 percent, up from 6.3 percent. Credit card rates average 22 percent. Household debt totals $18.8 trillion. - War cost: Moody’s Analytics estimates the Iran war has cost the average household $2,000. Its chief economist said: “No wonder the war is so unpopular.” - Coming data: Retail sales and producer prices are due today.

2. Trump weighs direct US entry into Saudi war on Yemen as ships sink near Hormuz

The Saudi–Houthi war has become a front of the wider US–Israeli war on Iran, and Washington is now openly considering joining it. Asked on Saturday whether the US would join Saudi strikes on the Houthis, Trump said “We may. We’re gonna look at it.” On Tuesday he declined to say. Secretary of State Rubio said on October 5 that Washington would honor its defense agreement with Riyadh. According to NBC, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman urged Trump in September to strike the Houthis.

Fighting around the Gulf grew in the past 24 hours. Al Jazeera reports that two vessels sank near the Strait of Hormuz, one hit by a projectile and the other by a sea mine. Houthi strikes hit Saudi Aramco targets, including in Dhahran, where the company has its headquarters. Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Tehran “does not seek an endless war” but would keep fighting until Washington meets its demands. US forces destroyed a dhow they said was an Iranian command-and-control vessel.

Key facts: - Riyadh airport: A Houthi strike on King Khalid International Airport killed at least 13 people and wounded more than 300. Rubio said an American was among the dead. A follow-up strike killed a Pakistani resident and injured 11. - UN: Special Envoy Hans Grundberg told the Security Council that Yemen has returned to “full-scale war.” UNICEF says nearly 130,000 people have been displaced in three weeks. - Saudi bombing: Saudi aircraft hit Sanaa, Dhamar and Saada. The Yemen Data Project recorded 253 Saudi air raids in September, killing at least 66 civilians. - Ground war: The Houthis took Mocha port and Perim Island in September, which gives them control of Yemen’s Red Sea coast. The Saudi-backed Presidential Leadership Council announced a ground offensive on October 5. - US buildup: More than 100 US military advisers are reportedly supporting Saudi targeting and intelligence (CNN, September 10). AP reports that three aircraft carriers could be in the region by the end of October. NBC reported on October 7 that the military has drawn up options for resuming major combat against Iran, possibly before the midterms. - Allied involvement: France is sending troops and air defenses to protect the Saudi port of Yanbu. Britain is sending an RAF tanker to refuel Saudi jets. Pakistan confirms its forces are already deployed under an August 7 defense pact.

As Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS, US intelligence, targeting support and arms sales already make Washington “a direct participant” in Saudi operations, as it has been under three administrations.

3. Russian strikes black out 600,000 Ukrainians as SpaceX throttles military Starlink

Trump’s “ENERGY CEASEFIRE,” announced on Truth Social on Sunday, collapsed within hours, and Kyiv is now openly at odds with Washington. Zelenskyy said at least 600,000 people across Ukraine lost power Wednesday after Russian attacks. Ukraine also accused SpaceX of cutting the speed of Starshield, the military version of Starlink, during Russian missile attacks. Its units say this disrupted drone operations.

Zelenskyy said Ukraine learned of the “ceasefire” from the media, and that Putin answered every de-escalation proposal with “nyet.” Asked whether Russia had broken the deal, Trump said “I’m not sure about that” and claimed “violations on both sides.” The Kremlin has neither confirmed nor denied agreeing to it. Zelenskyy told Piers Morgan: “It’s a pity that we are losing strong partner.”

Key facts: - Starlink limits: Serhii Beskrestnov, Zelenskyy’s defense technology adviser, said the limits apply to terminals moving faster than 100 mph and caused “the failure of a number of missions.” The 7th Rapid Reaction Corps said: “The drones kept flying, but the interruptions made them harder to control.” One unit in Zaporizhzhia canceled operations. Ukraine learned of the change from SpaceX customer support after filing a complaint. - Musk: Starshield is owned and controlled by the US government. “Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS.” - Intelligence: Russian ballistic missiles hit Kyiv on Monday and Tuesday with no advance warning. Zelenskyy called it “the second time Ukraine was not warned.” Trump said: “We are helping them, and we are sharing intel.” There are reports that Washington threatened to cut intelligence if Kyiv kept striking Russian refineries. - Diesel deal: Trump says Russia will supply more than 300,000 tons of diesel, about 2.25 million barrels, which is less than one day of US consumption. Treasury has suspended the 2022 Russian fuel sanctions for six months to allow it. The IEA says Russian diesel output is down nearly 30 percent, and analysts doubt any significant exports will follow. - Democrats: Senators Shaheen, Schumer and Warren called the deal “a total betrayal of Ukraine, our European allies and American national security.”

4. Administration escalates pre-election offensive with Omar citizenship probe and California ballot ultimatum

According to NBC and the Wall Street Journal, federal agents searched Rep. Ilhan Omar’s Washington home and seized her phone, passport, computers and notebooks. Investigators are seeking to strip the Minnesota Democrat of her citizenship and ultimately deport her. A Minnesota grand jury has issued more than a dozen subpoenas, some to her relatives. The search took place 22 days before the election, breaking the Justice Department’s longstanding practice of avoiding overt steps against candidates in the run-up to a vote. Seizing a sitting lawmaker’s phone would normally also trigger the Constitution’s “speech or debate” protections.

On Tuesday, Acting Deputy Attorney General R. Trent McCotter gave California Secretary of State Shirley Weber and Los Angeles County Registrar Dean Logan 48 hours to review a federal list of “likely noncitizens.” He demanded that their mailed ballots be “segregated” and not counted, and threatened “emergency relief” if the state refused. As Barry Grey wrote on the WSWS, these measures are preparation for disputing or overturning an election the administration expects to lose. A Reuters/Ipsos poll puts Trump’s approval at a record low of 32 percent.

Key facts: - The Omar case: Investigators are examining whether Omar or her late father changed her age on immigration papers so that she was recorded as 17, not 18. HSI looked at a similar allegation under Biden and dropped it for lack of evidence. A criminal case would face a five-year statute of limitations, and denaturalization would require court proceedings. - Omar: “Make no mistake: this is an attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement.” DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin said last month that “something’s going to happen” with Omar. - Trump: He called Omar “a terrible person” and “a disaster,” while saying the matter was “up to the DOJ.” On October 8 he posted that Minnesota was being “taken over by Somali Immigrants.” - Noncitizen voting: Reuters estimates that about 30,000 noncitizens may have been added to voter rolls since 2000 through clerical errors, against roughly 170 million registered voters. When Texas ran the overhauled SAVE system, at least 21 percent of the 2,724 people it flagged turned out to be citizens. - Courts: On October 9, Judge Sparkle Sooknanan vacated the DOJ’s “list maintenance project,” which had shared voter data from 18 states with DHS and ICE. She wrote that the program “has removed United States citizens from the voter rolls.” Reuters reports that the DOJ has lost 26 of its voter-roll lawsuits against states and won none. - Election Day: The DOJ plans to send 1,000 staff to polling places, compared with 714 in 2024 and 289 in 2022. The NAACP has sued for records on the plan.

5. China opens Laos air-power base as North Korea tests submarine-launched cruise missiles

According to an AP analysis of satellite imagery, China’s new Ban Keun airfield, about 30 miles north of Vientiane near the Thai border, is its first overseas military facility built around air power. The site is run jointly with Laos and was opened at a September ceremony, though construction is still going on. It has a runway of about 6,560 feet, a parallel taxiway and an apron for about two dozen aircraft. There is room to extend the runway by at least 3,280 feet, enough to take bombers. Laos, a country of 8 million, is deeply in debt to China for a railway and other infrastructure.

North Korean state media said the military fired two kinds of “strategic” cruise missiles designed for submarine launch on Wednesday in its eastern waters. It did not say whether they were fired from a submarine or a test platform. The launches follow hypersonic missile tests on September 20 and October 3.

Key facts: - Beijing’s position: The Chinese Defense Ministry says the facility will train Lao pilots and is “not directed against any third party.” - Strategic role: The Institute for the Study of War says the base “would give the PLA alternate routes to access disputed areas of the South China Sea.” The IISS’s Evan Laksmana asked: “Does this mean the U.S. would have to attack a joint facility in Southeast Asia?” - Thailand: Its Defense Ministry does not see a threat but will keep monitoring the site. Vietnam, Cambodia and Myanmar have not commented. - North Korea: Kim Jong Un calls South Korea a “permanent enemy” and has rejected Trump’s calls to restart talks. Submarine-launched missiles are harder for neighbors to detect in advance.

6. UN warns of Asian and African food crisis as Hormuz fertiliser plan stalls

UN Under-Secretary-General Jorge Moreira da Silva told Reuters at the IMF–World Bank meetings in Bangkok that it is “absolutely critical” for Washington and Tehran to approve a UN fertiliser corridor through the Strait of Hormuz within weeks. If planting seasons are missed, he said, “the productivity of agriculture would significantly drop” and food prices would rise across parts of Asia and Africa. The plan was drawn up in March and could be running within 7–10 days of approval. It has been held up by the wider US–Iran deadlock, since Washington insists on addressing Iran’s nuclear program first.

Key facts: - Before the war: About one-third of global urea shipments and nearly half of seaborne sulphur went through the strait. Urea is the world’s most widely used nitrogen fertiliser. - The mechanism: A UN-staffed center in Oman would register vessels, verify cargo at Gulf ports and track ships through the strait. Five vessels a day would be enough to keep supplies moving to the countries most in need. - Warnings: The FAO warned in May that fertiliser shortages could tighten food supplies from late 2026 into 2027. Farmers in South Asia are already struggling with costs, and Spanish farmers protested rising production costs on Wednesday. - Background: The June interim US–Iran ceasefire collapsed, and the two sides now exchange proposals only through intermediaries.

MI5 declares a new era of state threats as Iran shifts to military targets

In MI5’s annual threat update on Tuesday, Director General Ken McCallum said Britain and Europe have “entered a new era of national security,” which he compared to the period after September 11. He said Iran is moving from targeting dissidents and Jewish sites to “harder targets, like military sites.” Prime Minister Andy Burnham told Parliament that the September 27 incident at RAF Fairford was “highly likely to have been coordinated in Iran, almost certainly directed” by the IRGC. In that incident, seven men in their 20s were arrested near the base with three vans that turned out to contain only gasoline. About a week later, the US withdrew its bombers from Fairford.

  • McCallum called Russia “aggressor No. 1,” with its services “once again escalating their attempts at hybrid warfare.”
  • MI5 now puts more resources into threats from Russia, Iran and China than it did during the Cold War.
  • He also warned that terrorists could use AI to develop bioweapons.

The speech prepares the ground for wider surveillance and repression at home as Britain is drawn further into the Gulf war.

Denmark rebuffs Trump’s Greenland drilling claims as Taiwan commits to Alaska LNG

On Wednesday Trump posted that “Massive Oil, Gas, and Mineral Exploration is getting ready to begin” in “our new and beautiful Greenland.” The Danish Foreign Ministry replied that the September 22 Arctic security agreement does not change Greenland’s extraction rules and gives US investors no preferential access. Greenland banned uranium mining and all new oil exploration in 2021. The agreement does allow expanded US activity at Pituffik, two new bases and a role in Trump’s “Golden Dome” missile defense, and it limits investment from non-NATO countries. Danish security adviser Jacob Kaarsbo warned that it is “extremely dangerous” to believe the deal has changed Trump’s aims.

  • Taiwan: At a Wasilla rally, Trump said Taiwan would buy 30 percent of the gas from the planned 807-mile Alaska LNG pipeline for 20 years and called it “one of the biggest deals ever made.” Taipei’s own figures are 6 million tonnes a year and up to $4.4 billion in investment, with deliveries no earlier than 2032.
  • Arms sales: A $14 billion US arms package for Taiwan has been stalled since Trump’s May summit with Xi. Trump calls arms sales a “very good negotiating chip.”
  • South Korea: Trump also claimed a $54 billion South Korean investment in the pipeline. Seoul says it still has to assess whether the project is commercially viable.

Albanian students storm Education Ministry in fourth month of anti-Rama protests

Protesters broke into Albania’s Education Ministry in Tirana overnight Tuesday and set a fire inside. On Wednesday, hundreds of students threw rocks at the building, and riot police answered with tear gas and water cannon. Students are protesting tuition fees and university conditions and are demanding that Prime Minister Edi Rama resign. The protests are part of the “Flamingo Revolution,” four months of daily rallies that began against a resort project in a protected area of southern Albania linked to Jared Kushner.

  • At least 16 more protesters were arrested. Deputy Prime Minister Albana Kociu called on citizens to “distance yourself from violence.”
  • In the US, a watchdog group has sued the administration over Kushner’s government work (NBC).

Fuel costs force Spanish NGO to halt Mediterranean migrant rescues

Salvamento Marítimo Humanitario (SMH) has postponed its next Central Mediterranean mission until next year. It says fuel prices have doubled and it “can no longer afford” search and rescue on one of the world’s deadliest migration routes. Its converted fishing boat will now work around Valencia instead. Open Arms has also cut back its missions, citing fuel costs and Italy’s practice of sending rescue ships to distant northern ports.

  • About 60,000 people have reached Europe by sea this year, down 39 percent. At least 2,292 have died or gone missing on the way (IOM).

Russia jails Japanese reporter on espionage charges

The FSB detained Yuki Imai, whom it identified as a Hokkaido Shimbun reporter, on Sakhalin the day before he was due to leave Russia. It accuses him of collecting information on Russian defense capabilities. A Moscow court ordered him held for nearly two months. Foreign Ministry spokeswoman Maria Zakharova tied the case to Tokyo’s refusal to address Russian concerns about Japanese “remilitarization.” Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara asked for “fair treatment” but did not condemn Moscow. It is the most prominent arrest of a foreign journalist in Russia since Evan Gershkovich in 2023. Putin visited one of the disputed Kuril Islands in August.

Israel has detained over 2,000 Palestinian children since 2023, prisoners group reports

A Palestinian Prisoner’s Society report released Wednesday says Israeli forces have detained more than 2,000 Palestinian children since October 2023. At least 350 are being held now. The total for Gaza is unknown because of mass disappearances. More than 25,000 Palestinians have been detained in the West Bank over the same period.

  • Recent arrests: At least six children were arrested in Hebron on Tuesday.
  • Mohammed Hamid, 15: He caught scabies in Ofer Prison, developed severe infections, needed heart surgery and had a leg amputated.
  • Walid Ahmed, 17: He died in Megiddo Prison in March 2025. In December an Israeli judge halted the investigation into his death, conceding there was evidence of starvation.
  • US citizens: Two American teenagers are still held, Abdalkareem Safi and 16-year-old Adam Karakrah. Israeli authorities reversed Karakrah’s bail.
  • Elsewhere: Gaza marked a year of “ceasefire” this week amid continued Israeli killings. Colombia, under President De la Espriella, opened an embassy in Jerusalem.

Indian police detain Cockroach movement leaders over purge of 130 million voters

Police in New Delhi detained several leaders of the “Cockroach” youth movement on Tuesday after they entered a historic protest site in defiance of a police order. The movement, whose supporters organize as the Cockroach Janta Party, demands that India’s chief election commissioner resign over the removal of 130 million people from voter rolls in a nationwide voter-list revision. Protesters say the purge favors Prime Minister Modi’s BJP. The scale of the purge, roughly one in seven registered voters, makes it one of the largest disenfranchisements anywhere.

Bangladesh arrests traffickers who sold workers to Russian army as cannon fodder

Bangladesh’s Special Response Battalion arrested Mizanur Rahman and Abu Taher. Police say they trafficked at least 30 poor Bangladeshis to Russia, each paying about $8,000 for promised jobs. Once there, the men had their passports and phones taken and were handed over to the military. Seventeen who arrived in May were killed on their first day of deployment after minimal training, and 20 deaths are confirmed in total. “Bangladeshis were used as bait to locate enemy positions,” said commander Mohammad Asaduzzaman. An estimated 28,000 foreigners from 136 countries are fighting for Russia. Ukraine says 16,000 foreigners serve in its army, nearly 40 percent of them from Latin America.

Costa Rica opens Bukele-style mega-prison

On Wednesday President Laura Fernández opened the High-Containment Centre Against Organised Crime (CACCO) in Alajuela. It holds 5,100 inmates, about 20 percent of the country’s prison population, and the first transfers are due at the end of October. “The CACCO will force criminals to face a new model – a tough-on-crime model,” Fernández said. Costa Rica has recorded over 870 homicides in each of the last three years.

  • Peru’s President Keiko Fujimori plans to tour El Salvador’s CECOT, which holds 40,000. Colombia’s President De la Espriella promised 10 mega-prisons. Brazil’s Flávio Bolsonaro promises five.
  • El Salvador’s “state of exception,” in place since 2022, has produced mass trials and detention without charge.

Pope Leo XIV undergoes lung cancer surgery

IMF stability report flags AI correction and weak banks

The IMF’s Global Financial Stability Report, released at its Bangkok meetings, names three “salient risks”: an AI-driven market correction, a sharp rise in sovereign bond yields, which are already at their highest in more than two decades, and cyberattacks. It warns that “weaker or less durable AI investments and profitability could be a shock that triggers a market correction.”

  • Banks holding 22 percent of global banking assets are classed as “weak,” up from 18 percent in the 2025 stress test.
  • US bank loans to nonbank lenders rose 18 percent this year, and exposure to private equity and private credit rose 30 percent.
  • Hyperscalers’ share of investment-grade bond issuance has risen from under 1 percent in 2024 to 10 percent.
  • Pension funds and insurers have nearly doubled their holdings of illiquid assets in a decade, which raises the risk of forced selling in a crisis.

The CDC reports 4,080 confirmed measles cases as of October 8, up 193 (5.0 percent) in a week. There have been 42 new outbreaks across 47 jurisdictions. The death toll is in dispute. The CDC confirms two measles deaths this year, while Pennsylvania alone reports five. The CDC’s recent switch to National Center for Health Statistics mortality data has drawn criticism and raises questions about undercounting.

  • “Will the ‘Celtic Alliance’ break up the United Kingdom?” (The Communist), by Ellen Morton. It correctly calls the SNP–Plaid–Sinn Féin summit “performative diplomacy” and records the SNP’s fall in membership from 126,000 to 56,000.
