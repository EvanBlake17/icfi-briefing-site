1. Lula wins a fourth term as Flávio Bolsonaro moves to annul the vote

Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won Sunday’s runoff with more than 64 million votes (51.79%) to just under 60 million (48.21%) for Senator Flávio Bolsonaro. Flávio had led the first round 47% to 45.2%, and Lula gained about 10.4 million votes between rounds. Analysts estimate three-quarters of the additional runoff voters chose him. Abstention fell from a record high in the first round to a record low in the runoff. Lula takes office January 5, 2027.

At a Sunday news conference Flávio said he would seek to annul the result, citing the chaotic last-minute extension of voting. A Supreme Court justice had extended voting nationwide minutes before the 5 p.m. close, at the request of both campaigns. Many polling stations did not get word until they were already shutting down. Electoral court president Kassio Nunes, a Jair Bolsonaro appointee, recognized Lula’s victory. According to the WSWS, Trump said the vote “looked like another rigged election.” AP reported he had not commented.

Key facts: - Markets: The dollar rose 8.65% to 5.42 reais, São Paulo futures fell 15% and tripped the circuit breaker, and the Treasury cancelled scheduled bond auctions (WSWS, citing Brazilian press). - Congress and states: Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party (PL) will be the largest bloc in Congress. It also won the governorships of Rio de Janeiro, Rio Grande do Sul and Santa Catarina. - Austerity ahead: Estadão estimates at least 39.8 billion reais in 2027 cuts are needed to fund Lula’s campaign promises. - Lula on security: “For working people, we will give protection. For the criminal, we will give prison.” - Brian Winter (Americas Quarterly): “This is a really uphill battle for Flavio Bolsonaro… They can make life difficult for Lula for weeks and months to come.” - Benjamin Gedan: False claims of fraud “are becoming commonplace, though that does not make them any less dangerous.” - Jair Bolsonaro is serving 27 years for the 2022–23 coup attempt.

In today’s Perspective, Tomas Castanheira writes that the result was a mass rejection of fascism, but it hands power to a “Broad Front” government that will deepen austerity. He compares it to Dilma Rousseff, who named banker Joaquim Levy finance minister after her 51.64% win in 2014. In his account, that austerity paved the way for the 2016 impeachment and Bolsonaro’s rise.

2. Washington weighs a new strike on Iran as the Gulf war spreads to Saudi Arabia

Speaking to Fox News on Sunday, Trump said he must choose between a deal and a renewed attack: “Do I finish it off, which would be very easy to do, or do I make a deal?” On Friday he said the US “may do another little Epic Fury,” the name of the US-Israeli campaign that began February 28. He also claimed the US now controls the Strait of Hormuz and accused Iran of inventing ship attacks to drive up oil prices before the midterms. A third carrier group is heading to the region. CBS sources say US diplomatic staffing has been cut. ABC reports that “Iran is increasing efforts to target Americans overseas.” This follows an Oct. 23 memo from Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton to Secretary of State Rubio, reported by Axios, which said Iranian plots against US officials had reached “advanced stages.” The memo named no targets and offered no operational evidence.

Tehran is publicly defiant but still talking. “Whether there is war or not, our policies remain the same,” said Foreign Minister Araghchi. Saudi Defense Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistani Field Marshal Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin met Iranian officials in Tehran on Monday. Nothing concrete was announced and the parties agreed to keep talking. Qatari mediators, coordinating with Washington, visited on Sunday.

Key facts: - Shipping: UKMTO confirmed that an outbound tanker was disabled by an unknown projectile on Oct. 23, with the crew safe. On Oct. 26 a vessel was on fire 63 nautical miles from Iraq’s Al-Faw. Iran claims to have struck four tankers on Oct. 22, which has not been corroborated. - Blockade: Kpler reports Iran’s shadow-fleet tankers ran out of offshore crude in mid-October. CENTCOM says the US naval blockade has cost Iran at least $25 billion in oil revenue. - Saudi front: The Houthis claimed an attack on Riyadh airport on Oct. 25, hours after it reopened from a two-week closure. The Saudi-led coalition reports intercepting a ballistic missile aimed at Riyadh on Oct. 26 and three aimed at the Eastern Province on Oct. 27. The Houthis say they hit oil pumping stations. - Egypt: Parliament authorized President Sisi on Oct. 26 to send troops to Saudi Arabia, a mission Sisi calls defensive. - Kuwait: Three men were sentenced to death on Oct. 26 for an alleged Iran-linked plot to assassinate the deputy prime minister. Eleven others got five years’ hard labor. - Iran’s home front: Parliament speaker Ghalibaf called for at least a 10% cut in gas use ahead of expected winter shortages. - Russia: Putin will meet Iranian President Pezeshkian and the Emir of Qatar at a Moscow energy summit.

Kevin Reed argued on the WSWS that the DNI memo is being prepared as a casus belli, with a renewed assault possible around the November 3 midterms.

3. Israel votes today with Netanyahu and Eisenkot neck and neck

Israelis vote today in an election that polls show as a dead heat between Benjamin Netanyahu’s bloc and the opposition led by former army chief Gadi Eisenkot. No party has ever won a 61-seat Knesset majority alone, and about a dozen parties are expected to enter. Both blocs say they will not govern with the Arab parties. That leaves a unity government, months of coalition talks, or new elections early next year as the likely outcomes. Netanyahu, 77, is on trial for corruption and faces an International Criminal Court arrest warrant. He has refused an inquiry into October 7 and calls his military campaigns a “war of redemption.” Eisenkot attacks him for offering “an eternal war,” but not for the war itself.

Ahead of the vote the army sealed off the West Bank. A shooting in Hebron on Friday killed two Israeli soldiers and wounded three. In response the military says it has operated in more than 400 locations and arrested 40 Palestinians. Crossings into Israel stay closed until after the vote, and most Palestinian permit-holders were barred from working in settlements on Sunday and Monday.

Key facts: - Hebron: More than 2,700 people displaced, including hundreds of children, and every entrance and exit sealed since Friday. Mayor Yousef Zuhair Al-Jabari: “People cannot reach or enter the city, and they cannot leave it.” Netanyahu: “We will act to destroy this place.” - West Bank toll this year: 112 Palestinians killed (Palestinian Health Ministry) and six Israelis. - Gaza: Over 74,000 Palestinians killed, according to local health authorities. - Iran: With Trump’s endorsement, Netanyahu has threatened to resume the war, destroy Iran’s nuclear program and topple its government. Channel 12 reported a rift with army chief Eyal Zamir over a new Iran directive, which Netanyahu called “rubbish.” Tehran says the result “makes no difference.” - Other issues: ultra-Orthodox draft exemptions, and allies pushing to revive the frozen judicial overhaul.

4. Vučić concedes as the Students’ List takes a parliamentary majority

Aleksandar Vučić conceded Sunday’s parliamentary election about 90 minutes after polls closed. That ends 14 years in which he dominated Serbian politics. By his own figures, the Students’ List won about 2.2 million votes to 1.7 million for his Serbian Progressive Party (SNS). He projects the List will hold 135–140 of 250 seats, with SNS at about 105. Peter Schwarz, citing the results, puts the List just over 53% and SNS at about 39%, with no other party winning seats. Monitors expected turnout near 70%, the highest since 2000. Vučić had resigned the presidency last month to run for prime minister. He admitted “The Students’ List has won,” but blamed independent media, unnamed European governments and an “American deep state.” Putin had endorsed him.

The List grew out of two years of protests after the November 2024 collapse of the Novi Sad railway station canopy, which killed 16. None of its candidates are students. It is headed by medical professor Ilija Srdanović, and former central bank governor Dejan Šoškić is tipped as prime minister of an “expert” government. Šoškić promises “no revanchism and no revolution.”

Key facts: - Monitors recorded dozens of irregularities, including ballot photography pointing to voter pressure and attempted vote buying. Diaspora voters were forced to vote in the middle of the night. - Program: eleven points under three headings: “Serbia or the Mafia,” “Knowledge is Power” and “Serbia Grows with Us.” Fixed-term contracts would be cut from 24 to 12 months and import monopolies broken up. No funding is specified. It is silent on military spending, taxation, EU and NATO membership, and Kosovo. - Social conditions: average wage €1,035 a month, minimum wage €550, youth unemployment 22% and a poverty rate of 20% (WSWS). - CBS notes the result follows Viktor Orbán’s defeat in Hungary, where youth turnout was also decisive.

Schwarz concludes that corruption cannot be reformed away on a capitalist basis. Either the working class takes the initiative across the Balkans, he argues, or a new “mafia” emerges as proxy for the EU, the US or Russia.

5. Trump’s Justice Department intervenes in the midterms a week before the vote

With the November 3 midterms a week away and record early voting reported in key states, the administration has opened several legal and administrative fronts.

  • Alaska: On Oct. 22, after voting had begun, DOJ sued for a court order barring certification of the Senate race if Republican Sen. Dan S. Sullivan narrowly loses. The theory is that votes for a similarly named candidate, Daniel J. Sullivan, should have gone to him. Trump says he directed the suit. District Judge Sharon Gleason was openly skeptical on Monday about its last-minute timing. She will rule by Wednesday on outside parties joining the case.
  • California: A 2–1 Ninth Circuit panel refused DOJ’s emergency demand to segregate about 20,000 mail-in ballots from alleged non-citizens. Two of the three judges, one appointed by Trump and one by Biden, called the emergency partly “self-constructed.” Los Angeles officials checked 200 names on the list and found nearly 40% said they were born in the US. A district court hearing is set for today.
  • Bounty: On Monday Trump announced a $1 million reward for reports of voter fraud, past or present, that lead to a conviction. The White House would not say where the money would come from. Last week he launched a separate whistleblower program paying 1% of recovered funds, with Trump deciding who gets paid.
  • Monitors: DOJ plans its largest-ever observer deployment, about 1,000 staff. The RNC has “election integrity” directors in 22 states and nearly 22,000 poll workers. Civil Rights chief Harmeet Dhillon says there is “no plan” to send ICE to polling places. DNC vice chair Jane Kleeb: “We’ve never had to deal with the president of the United States saying that he may seize ballots.”
  • Michigan: At a nearly two-hour rally in Port Huron, Trump branded Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed a “jihadist” and “Muslim Brotherhood member.”
  • Inflation: AP reports inflation at 3.7%, up from 3% when Trump took office, with prices outpacing paychecks over the past year. Filling a Ford F-150 costs about $100, up $30 in a year, and diesel is at a record. Trump claims inflation is below 2%. Democrats are competitive in Iowa, Kansas, Ohio and Texas.

Patrick Martin noted on the WSWS that Obama, at Detroit’s Cass Technical High School, offered “jokes and silence” on the danger of dictatorship. He did, however, call the Iran war a strategic failure.

6. Microsoft offers buyouts to 60,000 workers as AI spending surges

Microsoft has offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees in the US, Canada, India and the UK, roughly 27% of the 223,000 full-time staff it had at the end of June. No payout terms or deadlines were disclosed. Chief People Officer Amy Coleman wrote that “The Voluntary Exit Offer is new for us.” The move comes before Wednesday’s quarterly results and amid an AI build-out in which industry data-center investment is likely to top $700 billion this year. That spending is driving up the cost of memory chips, processors and electricity, and economists expect it to keep pushing inflation up through year-end.

Key facts: - Earlier cuts: In May Microsoft reportedly planned buyouts for about 8,750 US employees (7% of its US workforce). In July it announced 4,800 job cuts (2.1% globally), many in Xbox. - The shares hit an all-time high earlier this month and rose about 1% on the announcement. - The offer came the same day Anthropic’s IPO filing lifted the Nasdaq (see World economy).

The scale is without precedent for a voluntary program at a US tech firm. Calling it “voluntary” puts the restructuring on individual workers and avoids the notice requirements that come with mass layoffs.

7. Macron offers concessions as France’s youth revolt spills into Germany

The French government will replace Parcoursup, the national university admissions platform, in 2028, with “substantial change” promised for 2027. The movement began with high-school blockades in mid-September. On Oct. 17 it became France’s worst unrest in years: rioting, clashes with police, and protesters storming public buildings including Marseille City Hall. Macron has promised “concrete measures” to meet “legitimate demands.” After meeting Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu on Monday, two high-school student groups welcomed some concessions but called for protests to continue. At least 400,000 marched nationwide on Saturday by the authorities’ count, and nearly 800,000 according to organizers. Up to 70,000 police were mobilized, and in Paris the main march was confined to the city’s southeastern edge.

Key facts: - Repression: 7,950 people taken into custody by Oct. 13, 85% of them minors. A 15-year-old in Lens lost a hand to a police weapon, and deaths were reported in Paris on Oct. 17 and Oct. 19 (WSWS, citing French reports). - Student demands: transparent admissions, more teachers to cover absences, school renovation and smaller classes, against a background of record poverty and record public debt. - Germany: The “genz-regelt” initiative has called protests for Saturday, Oct. 31, against cuts, decaying schools, the cost of living and the return of conscription. Five cities were planned at first, independent organizers added 18 more, and more than 135,000 have signed up. The Sept. 25 school strike drew about 45,000 in some 100 cities. - German state response: The interior ministry is coordinating through the Joint Counter-Extremism and Counter-Terrorism Centre. Domestic intelligence is stepping up social-media monitoring and coordinating with France and Italy, and border turn-backs are being considered. Berlin police spokesman Florian Nath on the protests: “We will not allow that.” CDU spokesman Alexander Throm warned they would be “hijacked by left-wing extremist forces, sections of the migrant milieu and criminals.”

Britain rejects Canada’s military-finance bank

After a months-long courtship by Ottawa, the UK said on Oct. 25 that it will not join the Canada-based Defence, Security and Resilience Bank (DSRB). The bank is designed to lower borrowing costs for military spending by pooling allied credit, and Belgium, Turkey and Ukraine are among its supporters. London is pursuing its own Multilateral Defence Mechanism, and a joint statement promised the two would “work in close partnership.” ABC separately reports Prime Minister Carney saying Trump is moving closer to Canada’s demands in the trade dispute. This development is unverified.

Machado returns to a Venezuela run by Washington’s “interim” government

María Corina Machado returned to Venezuela over the weekend, appearing at a mass in Lara state and leading a caravan to Caracas. She left in late 2025 to collect the Nobel Peace Prize. US forces seized Nicolás Maduro in January, and Washington has since worked with Acting President Delcy Rodríguez, whose brother Jorge heads the National Assembly. Rubio said the interim authorities told the US “weeks and weeks” ago that Machado could return. He said the government has met “every timeline” for replacing the Supreme Court and electoral council before any election, for which no date has been set. He called the US-backed oil deal a way to attract private investment and said Machado “doesn’t have to agree with” it. He cited US “leverage” through sanctions relief and licenses, and said there was “definitely no talk” of prosecutions. Trump has not ruled out military action against Cuba.

  • Social conditions (WSWS, citing the Venezuelan Observatory of Social Conflict): 1,101 protests in September, 531 of them over electricity. The minimum wage has been frozen since March 2022 and is worth under 30 US cents. Zulia oil workers, paid $5 a week, walked out Oct. 19–20 and occupied PDVSA headquarters in Maracaibo.
  • Andrea Lobo writes on the WSWS that Machado “has not been brought home to challenge the protectorate,” but to channel working-class anger behind US-managed talks.

Anthropic IPO filing drives AI rally

On Monday Anthropic filed publicly to prepare for an initial public offering, having earlier filed a confidential draft S-1 with the SEC. The filing shows $16.3 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier, and a net loss of $80.7 billion. The filing set off a fresh round of AI buying in tech stocks, which carried into Asia on Tuesday. Japan and Korea led the gains while oil-importing markets lagged.

The pattern is familiar: an $80 billion loss and the largest voluntary workforce reduction in Microsoft’s history (see Top stories) were both met with buying.

Joint Economic Committee report: Trump’s oil stocks soared during the Iran war

A report by Joint Economic Committee Democrats, obtained by CBS News and based on Trump’s ethics filings, finds his oil and gas holdings rose to $22.5–81.6 million, from $12.6–46 million at the end of 2025. That is an average gain of 43% (LSEG data). Between March and August he bought $4.7–17.5 million in oil and gas stocks and sold $2–5.4 million. His largest positions are Chevron (up to $23.1 million, up nearly 25% since Feb. 20) and ExxonMobil (up to $18.4 million, up nearly 22%). September and October transactions have not yet been reported. Trump has boasted that US oil companies are making “hundreds of billions of dollars” from the conflict. White House spokesman Davis Ingle says the portfolio is managed independently and there are “no conflicts of interest.”

DOJ files a new national-defense charge against James Comey

Prosecutors have filed a criminal information, which requires no grand jury, charging former FBI Director James Comey with conspiring to disclose national defense information. The charge was filed under seal on Oct. 5 and made public Monday. They allege he directed FBI staff to brief a New York Times reporter for an Oct. 5, 2016 story on Yahoo email surveillance, to correct a Reuters report, and “thereby illegally transferred classified information.” Filing the information exactly ten years after the alleged conduct, DOJ argues, tolled the statute of limitations. Comey’s lawyers say the five-year limit has expired and that “the information is untimely and must be dismissed.”

  • A separate April indictment charges Comey with threatening Trump over an Instagram photo of seashells spelling “8647.” His lawyers are seeking dismissal as vindictive, citing a “yearslong campaign by the President to use the criminal process to punish Mr. Comey.”

Judge rules Maurene Comey’s firing unlawful

On Monday US District Judge Jesse Furman ruled that the Justice Department unlawfully fired Maurene Comey, James Comey’s daughter, from the Manhattan US Attorney’s office in July 2025. He ordered back pay through her December resignation and found violations of the Civil Service Reform Act, the separation of powers, the Administrative Procedure Act and due process. Walter Koenig, Star Trek’s Chekov, dies at 90

Walter Koenig died Sunday of old age at his North Hollywood home, aged 90. He joined Star Trek in its second season in 1967 as Ensign Pavel Chekov, a Russian officer on the bridge of a Starfleet ship at the height of the Cold War. Gene Roddenberry’s series had a multinational crew, with a Black woman communications officer, a Japanese-American helmsman and a Soviet-born navigator. It projected a future in which humanity had overcome national division and want. That outlook came from the liberal internationalism of the 1960s, and it is far removed from the militarized nationalism of today’s franchise entertainment. Koenig, born in Chicago to Jewish immigrant parents from the former Russian Empire, played Chekov through Star Trek: Generations (1994). He later created the Psi Corps telepath Alfred Bester on Babylon 5, a study of a security apparatus turned against its own people. He also wrote and directed, and was active in human rights and refugee work. George Takei called him “a writer, a teacher, a thoughtful man, and a loyal friend.” His son Andrew died by suicide in 2010.
