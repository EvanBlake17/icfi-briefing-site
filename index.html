1. Lula wins fourth term as Flavio Bolsonaro vows to annul Brazil’s election

Brazil’s Superior Electoral Court (TSE) declared President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva the winner of Sunday’s runoff at about 21:47 Brasília time. Lula, who turns 81 on Tuesday, overturned a first-round deficit. On October 4, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, 45, eldest son of the jailed former president, had led him 47.03 percent to 45.16 percent. No candidate who finished second in the first round had won a runoff since Brazil adopted the two-round system in 1989. Flávio led for much of the count, with 50.3 percent at 37 percent counted, until returns from the Northeast and the big cities came in. Lula’s new term starts January 5, 2027. He will face a Congress dominated by the right.

The far right has refused to accept the result. “It is impossible to recognize the results,” Flávio Bolsonaro said. “I am going to request the annulment of these elections.” He called the Supreme Court’s extension of voting a “dirty trick.” Justice Cristiano Zanin granted the extension at about the 5 p.m. cutoff, after Lula’s campaign said officials had cut election-day public transport in poor areas. Stations already closed stayed shut, so some voters were turned away. Federal Police are investigating alleged transport cuts in five states, and a Bahia court ordered the arrest of Salvador’s transport secretary. Jair Bolsonaro is serving 27 years for the 2022–23 coup plot. Eduardo Bolsonaro and Donald Trump Jr. both attacked the extension.

Key facts: - Result: 51.76 percent to 48.24 percent, a margin of about 4.3–4.4 million votes. Abstention fell from 21 percent in the first round, when 33.4 million people stayed home, the highest rate since 1998, to about 19 percent. - US role: On Saturday Reuters reported a campaign inside Trump’s State Department to help the Bolsonaros return to power, including a push for sanctions to weaken Lula. Trump declined to back a candidate and said only “we are watching.” Lula’s campaign called Trump’s 50 percent tariffs on Brazil and his comments on Jair Bolsonaro’s trial foreign interference. - Meta: Lula’s campaign accused Meta of “algorithmic manipulation” favouring Bolsonaro. Meta denied it but said some Lula ads were “mistakenly” deactivated. - Lula’s victory speech: Brazil “will not accept being anyone’s backyard.” He called the result “a defeat for denialism” and “a defeat for lies,” and pledged to regulate social media companies. - Regional reaction: Chile’s José Antonio Kast and Ecuador’s Daniel Noboa, both Trump allies, congratulated Lula. Mexico’s Claudia Sheinbaum said “the people of Brazil won.” The result halts a run of Trump-backed right-wing wins in Argentina, Chile, Colombia and Peru. - Markets: The Brazil ETF fell in Asian trading on news of Lula’s win.

Flávio Bolsonaro came within 3.5 points of the presidency. That reflects deep disillusionment with a PT government whose fourth term, with a hostile Congress and a business-friendly coalition, offers workers little beyond resisting the Bolsonaro clan and Washington.

2. Pentagon readies post-midterm strike options on Iran as war enters eighth month

The Trump administration is openly preparing a new round of attacks on Iran. The Hill reports that CENTCOM has given Defense Secretary Hegseth several strike options, focused on the period after the November 3 midterms. The New York Times reported Wednesday that Washington and Israel have drawn up joint attack plans for after the vote, and Axios said earlier this month that the Pentagon had told CENTCOM to finish preparing to resume major combat. The reported targets include energy facilities, infrastructure and nuclear sites. At a Friday rally Trump said the US “may do another little Epic Fury,” the codename for the war that began on February 28. On Saturday he said he was “thinking about doing it, and maybe tomorrow,” while also floating a “big, beautiful” deal. On Sunday he told Fox News, “There will not be a strike today.”

The pretext is being built in public. On Friday, Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton sent a memo to Secretary of State Rubio, first reported by Axios, claiming that Iranian “lethal plotting efforts” against Americans had reached “advanced stages.” It named no targets and gave no timeline. The State Department ordered non-emergency staff out of Saudi Arabia on October 21, and CNN reports reduced staffing at embassies in Lebanon, the UAE, Qatar and Jordan. In Sunday’s WSWS Perspective, Andre Damon argued that the timing is tied to Israel’s election on Tuesday and the US midterms, both expected to go badly for Netanyahu and Trump.

Key facts: - Forces: The USS Theodore Roosevelt, a third carrier strike group, is heading to the region with the 13th Marine Expeditionary Unit and about 10,000 more troops, all due by the end of November. The WSWS puts forces already deployed at 40,000–50,000. - Shipping: More than 120 attacks on ships have been reported in October, the most of any month in the war. Iran says seven tankers caught fire or were damaged in and around Hormuz this week. - Iran: President Pezeshkian said “the cowards [Americans] messed everything up,” blaming Washington for the collapse of the interim deal, but urged both sides to “conclude it.” The IRGC warned that even limited strikes would bring a response “several times greater.” Its spokesman said Iran’s doctrine has moved “from defensive to offensive” and that countries hosting US bases could become targets. Reuters reports that Tehran has plans to target undersea communications cables in the Gulf. - Regional diplomacy: Iranian security chief Mohsen Rezaei is due to host defence officials from Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan, described as the “Mecca Defence Alliance,” in Tehran. - Iran’s leadership: Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei succeeded his father, who was killed in the opening strikes, and has not been seen in public since early March. - Analysts: Chatham House’s Julie Norman warned of a “very high risk of miscalculation.” Military advisers are asking what another round would achieve that earlier rounds did not, she said, “and there is no clear answer yet.”

3. Houthis vow to keep striking Riyadh airport as Saudi coalition bombs Sanaa

Saudi Arabia reopened two terminals at Riyadh’s King Khalid International Airport on Sunday. The airport had been shut since October 10, when a Houthi-claimed attack killed 12 to 13 people and injured more than 300, the deadliest attack on any Gulf Arab state since the Iran war began. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree called the airport a “military target” that would “never be safe” and told airlines and travellers to stay away. Flightradar24 showed little traffic. The Saudi-led coalition has since struck Houthi-held territory, including Sanaa.

Key facts: - Sunday’s barrages: The coalition reported two drones and a ballistic missile fired at Riyadh and a cruise missile at Khamis Mushait. It later said it intercepted 10 ballistic missiles aimed at the south of the kingdom. Shrapnel injured two people. Emergency alerts sounded in Riyadh, Abha, Khamis Mushait, Jazan and Najran. - Earlier attacks: The coalition counted 20 Houthi attacks on the airport between October 1 and 11. The Houthis also say they hit Saudi oil facilities on Saturday. Saudi officials are investigating why air defences failed on October 10. - Bab el-Mandeb: Last month the Houthis seized territory on the Red Sea coast and are closing in on the strait, a chokepoint for Gulf oil. Fighting there breaks the UN-brokered truce that had largely held since 2022 and collapsed in July. - Oil: These attacks drove Brent’s 3.1 percent jump on Friday to $116.72, its highest settlement since April (see World economy).

4. Vucic concedes defeat to student-led list in Serbian election

Serbian strongman Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat on Sunday night in a snap parliamentary election. “It is clear that we have lost these elections,” he said. Citing a sample count, he projected 135–140 seats for the opposition Student List (“Students Win”) against 105 for his own list, in a 250-seat parliament where 126 is a majority. Vučić’s Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) has ruled since 2012. He resigned the presidency in September to run for prime minister, promising higher pensions and public-sector wages.

The result comes out of the mass movement that began in November 2024, when the canopy of the newly renovated Novi Sad railway station collapsed and killed 16 people. The protests grew into a two-year revolt against corruption and repression, led mainly by students and joined by teachers, farmers and workers. On Friday some 150,000 people, mostly young, rallied ahead of the vote.

Key facts: - Turnout: 64.9 percent by 6 p.m., the highest since 1992, among about 6.5 million eligible voters. Eighteen lists ran. - Candidates: The Student List ran doctors, teachers and other professionals, many with no political experience. Several established opposition parties stood down in its favour. No prime ministerial candidate has been named. - Irregularities: The CRTA watchdog recorded pressure on voters or election workers at almost a third of the 500 polling stations it monitored. CeSID received about 260 reports by 5 p.m. - Celebrations: Tens of thousands took to the streets of Belgrade and Novi Sad. Crowds took down the tents where SNS loyalists had camped in a central Belgrade park for more than a year. - EU: Ursula von der Leyen congratulated the Student List and offered support for “rule of law” reforms, an early sign that Brussels hopes to absorb the movement into its eastward integration agenda.

The movement has no defined social program. Its political future will be shaped by the pressures of the EU and Serbia’s bourgeois opposition parties, unless the working class, which is central to the mobilizations, intervenes on its own program.

5. Hundreds of thousands march in France as Le Pen launches presidential bid

Workers and young people marched across France on Saturday, October 24, a second weekend of mass protest against the cost of living, austerity and war. The Interior Ministry counted 410,000; the CGT put turnout at 800,000. About 70,000 police and soldiers were deployed. In Paris, rallies at Bastille and République were banned and people gathering in groups of two or more were fined. Lyon banned all demonstrations, and police there used tear gas. Some 250,000 joined the authorized Paris march from Porte d’Italie, which police surrounded and broke up at its final rally. Marseille organizers reported 100,000 marchers, Lille 35,000 and Toulouse 26,500.

The previous weekend (October 17), Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez had reported more than 1,800 arrests, with other counts above 2,000, and about 500 police, firefighters and paramedics injured. He blamed “several thousand ultra-left individuals.” The wave began in mid-September with high school students in a Paris suburb protesting classroom conditions.

The far right is moving to exploit the crisis. On Sunday Marine Le Pen opened her 2027 presidential campaign at a National Rally (RN) congress in Orléans before 8,500 supporters, while 20,000 people protested against it. The city shut down public transport and deployed about 3,000 police. Le Pen blamed Macron’s “incompetence” on security and accused Jean-Luc Mélenchon of encouraging violence.

Key facts: - Next steps: New demonstrations next Saturday and a national strike on November 5. The unions have still not called an all-out national strike. - RN program: A referendum challenging the primacy of EU law on migration and an annual €11 billion cut to France’s EU contribution. The RN has dropped euro exit. Jordan Bardella, 31, is positioned as head of government. - Le Pen: “This is a story to be written, a destiny to be fulfilled.” “We are going to re-establish the rule of law.” - Debt: France now pays more to borrow than Italy and Greece, according to Al Jazeera’s report on global debt ($365 trillion).

6. Trump Justice Department moves to contest midterm results in Alaska and California

Eight days before the November 3 midterms, the Trump administration has two federal lawsuits that would let it override state election results. In Alaska, the DOJ filed suit on Thursday to block certification of the Senate race if Republican Senator Dan S. Sullivan loses to Democrat Mary Peltola, who leads in the polls. The claim is that voters could be confused by the presence on the ballot of Dan J. Sullivan, a retired teacher listed as “Sullivan, Daniel J Jr.” State courts, including the Alaska Supreme Court on June 29, had already restored him to the ballot after the Division of Elections removed him. On Sunday television, Attorney General Todd Blanche defended the suit by citing the preamble of the 1993 National Voter Registration Act as authority to “protect the integrity of the electoral process.”

In California, a three-judge Ninth Circuit panel heard a rare weekend argument on the DOJ’s October 15 suit to set aside thousands of mail-in ballots sent to “likely noncitizens.” The suit rests on a 20,000-name list whose methodology has never been made public, and whose accuracy whistleblowers have questioned. Judge Mark Bennett, a Trump appointee, cited the Constitution’s Elections Clause and told DOJ lawyer Matthew Donnelly that the injunction would be “entirely inconsistent” with California law. “How can we do that?” he asked. He said the panel would rule “as soon as possible.”

Key facts: - Alaska reaction: The Peltola campaign called the suit a “despicable political threat,” and Marc Elias has filed a motion to intervene. Republican Senator Lisa Murkowski opposes the suit. Election lawyers in both parties say it has little legal chance. - California: Governor Newsom called it “a sad, desperate attempt by Trump to use his politicized Justice Department to try to suppress California voters.” Trump accused California Democrats of “BIG cheating” after his candidates did poorly in the state’s primaries. - Earlier litigation: Judge Indira Talwani’s nationwide injunction against the mail-voting provisions of Trump’s election executive order is still under DOJ challenge at the Supreme Court. - Stakes: Republicans hold 53 Senate seats, and about seven races are toss-ups. Polls favour Democrats to take both chambers.

The WSWS’s Patrick Martin calls the Alaska suit a new tactic in a pre-election campaign to contest results, with challenges to the certification and seating of Democrats expected on January 3, 2027.

7. Israel votes Tuesday with neither bloc projected to reach a majority

Israelis vote Tuesday, October 27, in the first general election since October 7, 2023. The final KAN poll before the Friday polling blackout gave the opposition bloc 52 seats and Netanyahu’s bloc 50 in the 120-seat Knesset. A majority is 61. Both blocs have ruled out working with the Arab parties, so the likely outcomes are drawn-out coalition talks, a minority government, or another election. If neither bloc can govern, Netanyahu could stay on as caretaker.

Key facts: - Blocs: Netanyahu’s side is Likud, Shas, United Torah Judaism, Ben-Gvir’s Otzma Yehudit and Smotrich’s Religious Zionism. The opposition is Gadi Eisenkot’s Yashar, Yair Golan’s Democrats, Lieberman’s Yisrael Beiteinu and the Bennett–Lapid “Together” alliance. - Eisenkot: “No Palestinian state will be established under the government we form.” Eight parties, including Likud and Yashar, reject a Palestinian state. - Netanyahu’s exposure: His corruption trial, under way since 2019, an ICC arrest warrant, and demands for an October 7 inquiry. He has said overthrowing Iran’s government remains Israel’s “primary objective,” and is reported to be pressing Washington for an attack as his polling falls. - Palestinian citizens: About 1.2 million of 7.3 million eligible voters. The Central Elections Committee barred the Joint List and Ra’am, but the Supreme Court reversed it. The court did disqualify Balad leader Sami Abu Shehadeh over remarks about October 7. At 65 percent turnout, Arab lists could win up to 15 seats. Three million Palestinians in Gaza, the West Bank and East Jerusalem cannot vote.

Middle East

Israeli strikes hit Islamic University as Gaza ceasefire toll passes 1,550

Israeli strikes killed at least eight Palestinians across Gaza on Sunday, a year into the nominal October 2025 “ceasefire.” Two attacks hit the Islamic University in Gaza City within a few hours. A drone strike wounded seven female students, one seriously, and an air strike then killed three security guards. Most of the campus had already been destroyed. Other deaths were in al-Mawasi (two), east of Deir el-Balah, on al-Rashid Street near Hamad Hospital, and in Sheikh Nasser, east of Khan Younis.

  • Since the “ceasefire”: At least 1,551 killed and 5,573 injured, per the Gaza Health Ministry.
  • Since October 2023: 74,544 killed and at least 175,692 wounded.
  • Occupation: Israel controls or restricts almost two-thirds of the Strip. Crossing closures and cuts to aid funding are worsening food insecurity.
  • West Bank: An Israeli assault on Hebron has displaced thousands as restrictions tighten.

Europe

Zelensky says US-Ukraine-Russia talks set for November in UAE

Zelensky told Ukrainian television on Sunday that a trilateral meeting of Ukraine, the United States and Russia will take place in November to restart stalled peace talks. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Friday that it is planned for the UAE, and other reports name Abu Dhabi. The announcements follow a round of shuttle diplomacy by US envoy Steve Witkoff, who met Zelensky and European leaders in Oslo on Tuesday and Putin on Wednesday.

  • Zelensky: “We are ready to talk. If it’s a territorial issue, it must be addressed at a leadership level.” Other questions would go to technical working groups.
  • Starlink: Kyiv says Starlink service has been restored as strikes by both sides on each other’s territory continue.
  • Washington: Democratic ranking members Smith, Meeks and Himes have pledged new Ukraine legislation, and Schumer accuses Trump of “aiding and abetting a dictator.” The Democrats are attacking the talks from the side of continuing the war.

Bulgaria’s presidential election heads to runoff on 22 percent turnout

No candidate won outright in Bulgaria’s presidential election on Sunday. Only 22.2 percent of registered voters had voted by 8 p.m., according to the Measure agency, among the lowest turnouts in the EU. The incumbent, Iliana Iotova, took an estimated 35–36.5 percent. She is backed by Prime Minister Rumen Radev’s Progressive Bulgaria and the Socialist Party, and seeks to keep Bulgaria in the EU while improving relations with Russia. Former caretaker prime minister Andrey Gyurov, backed by the pro-Western opposition, took 31–33.5 percent. As premier he signed a security pact with Ukraine. The runoff is November 1.

  • Issues: Energy costs, inflation, drone strikes on ships off the Black Sea coast, and the Ukraine war.
  • Powers: The presidency is largely ceremonial, but the president commands the armed forces, can veto legislation and signs treaties.

Storms Boelo and Austen kill one, black out 150,000 homes across northern Europe

Two back-to-back storms hit northern Europe over the weekend. A falling tree killed a 60-year-old man in southwest Scotland, and winds reached 135 km/h in Argyll and Bute. On Sunday more than 82,000 homes were without power in Britain, nearly 60,000 in France (288,000 at Austen’s peak on Saturday, mainly in Normandy and Brittany), and at least 10,000 around Freiburg in Germany. The hospital there limited itself to emergency cases, and mobile phone service failed. Hundreds of flights were cancelled, including at Schiphol. People were injured in Brussels, northern France and Nastätten in Germany, and three crew members on a freighter off Schiermonnikoog. Storm Austen blew away an unused pier in Brighton.

Asia-Pacific

North Korea accuses Seoul of fabricating DMZ mine blast as howitzer appears at border

In a letter to the UN Security Council on Sunday, Pyongyang said Seoul fabricated a September 21 landmine blast in the DMZ to portray North Korea’s border construction as an incursion. It warned that violations of its sovereignty could trigger “self-defence measures,” for which South Korea would be “wholly accountable.” Last week Seoul told the Council that North Korean troops planted the mine, in violation of the 1953 armistice, and the UN Command agrees.

  • Military display: Yonhap identified a self-propelled 122-mm howitzer on the Kaepung county coast, about 1.5 km from the Gimpo observatory. A Reuters witness confirmed it Monday and saw three armoured vehicles with guns pointed south and 20–30 soldiers building fences inside the DMZ.
  • Analysis: Lim Eul-chul of Kyungnam University called the display “highly unusual,” a signal that Pyongyang will enforce its new border “by force rather than dialogue.”
  • Warning shots: Pyongyang accuses South Korean troops of firing on its personnel. Seoul says it fires warning shots to push soldiers back across the Military Demarcation Line, the situation that produced the 2015 standoff.

Chinese Communist Party opens fifth plenum amid purge of Central Committee

About 300 members of the Chinese Communist Party’s Central Committee began a four-day closed meeting, the fifth plenum, on Monday. According to Xinhua, the agenda is a draft decision on “full and rigorous party self-governance.” Xi Jinping’s 14-year anticorruption drive has left eight Central Committee seats vacant and 15 members under investigation, so attendance is expected to be the lowest since Mao’s death in 1976. Five of the seven members of the Central Military Commission have been toppled, and the plenum may appoint replacements and restructure party control of the army. Xi is expected to seek a fourth term in about a year. In January he denounced “two-faced” officials. The meeting takes place amid weak consumer spending, a long property slump and confrontation with Washington.

Former Japanese prime minister Yasuo Fukuda dies at 90

Yasuo Fukuda, prime minister from September 2007 to September 2008, died of heart failure on Thursday, the Nikkei reported Monday. He was the first son of a prime minister (Takeo Fukuda, 1976–78) to hold the office. He spent almost two decades at an oil company before becoming his father’s political secretary, and served as chief cabinet secretary from 2000 to 2004 under Junichiro Koizumi. He resigned that post over unpaid pension contributions. He took over after Shinzo Abe’s sudden resignation and quit within a year, blocked by a divided Diet, the second of six prime ministers to leave after about a year. A diplomatic “dove,” he favoured closer ties with China and visited Beijing repeatedly after leaving office. In 2003 he drew condemnation for suggesting that provocatively dressed women invited rape. His son Tatsuo is a lawmaker tipped as a future prime minister.

Latin America

Machado returns to Venezuela as Washington backs Delcy Rodriguez

Opposition leader María Corina Machado announced on Sunday that she is back in Venezuela after nearly a year abroad, posting “I’m here. With you all” with images from a church in Lara state. She did not say how she returned. She had left secretly in late 2025 to collect her Nobel Peace Prize. Since the US removed Nicolás Maduro in January, Washington has backed his former deputy, interim President Delcy Rodríguez. Reuters reported in June that US officials asked Machado to delay her return.

  • Trump: He said he knew nothing of her return. “She’s a very nice woman,” but “we’re doing really well in Venezuela.” On Fox he said the US has “total control of Venezuela” and is “taking out millions of barrels of oil a week.” In September he said it was too soon for elections.
  • Oil: Last month energy companies committed to new Venezuelan projects at a signing overseen by Rodríguez and US Energy Secretary Chris Wright.
  • Machado’s agenda: Her adviser David Smolansky says she will meet earthquake victims and political prisoners’ families and push for an election date. Twin earthquakes in June killed at least 6,500 people. Machado’s movement is excluded from talks between a faction of the opposition and Rodríguez on overhauling the courts and electoral council.

That Washington keeps its own long-time regime-change candidate at arm’s length shows what the January intervention was for: the oil, not “democracy.”

Europe enters winter with gas storage at 72 percent

IEA chief Fatih Birol warned that “life will not be easy for Europe this year and also next year.” EU gas storage is 72 percent full against a usual 90 percent, a shortfall equal to the energy for 10–12 million homes. Prices are up 140 percent since February. Before the war, almost a tenth of Europe’s LNG passed through Hormuz. A third of Russian diesel deliveries to Europe have stopped, and Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries are adding to the diesel shortage. EU energy ministers are reported to be considering asking households and businesses to ration consumption. “Energy is not currently considered a business but a political leverage,” Birol said.

Trump calls El-Sayed a jihadist at Michigan rally

At a nearly two-hour rally in Port Huron, Michigan, on Sunday, Trump called Abdul El-Sayed, the Democratic Senate nominee, a “jihadist.” He said El-Sayed would be the only senator required to pass through a metal detector, claimed he wants Sharia law, and suggested he should be tried. Trump warned that a Democratic win would “open the floodgates and invite hordes of jihadists to our shores,” said Christmas could be cancelled, and repeatedly called El-Sayed “Mohammed.” El-Sayed is a US-born former public health official. There is no evidence for any of these claims.

  • Mike Rogers: The Republican nominee for the seat being vacated by Gary Peters shared the stage and repeated the Muslim Brotherhood claims. In 2012 he voted to continue aid to Egypt under Muslim Brotherhood president Mohamed Morsi. Rogers had earlier called for ending the Iran war.
  • Iran war: Trump said he has “no regrets.” Michigan gasoline averages $4.83 a gallon, up from $2.91 before the war.
  • Polls: A Marketing Resource Group survey finds only 35 percent of Michigan voters view Trump positively. Michigan is one of about seven toss-up Senate races.
  • El-Sayed’s response: He calls the attacks distractions from the cost of living and health care. Obama campaigned for him in Detroit last week (see WSWS coverage).

The rally shows the administration combining open anti-Muslim incitement with the war against Iran, as the midterm campaign closes.

Consumer sentiment sits at second-lowest reading on record

The University of Michigan’s final October consumer sentiment index came in at 48.0, the second-lowest reading on record after May’s 44.8. It is 10 percent below a year ago and 15 percent below February, before the Iran war. Among independents it fell to 42.4. Five-year inflation expectations rose to 3.5 percent from 3.2 percent in March. “Purchasing power continues to dominate consumer views of the economy,” said survey director Joanne Hsu, and the rise in inflation expectations “is not limited to one part of the political spectrum.” Headline CPI is 3.7 percent, and AAA puts the national average for gasoline at $4.29 a gallon. Al Jazeera reports more workers taking second and third jobs to stay afloat.

Ranchers turn on Trump over beef tariff pause

Trump’s 90-day suspension of some beef tariffs, announced in late August to bring down grocery prices, has angered cattle producers in swing districts in Kansas, Montana, Iowa, Texas and Colorado. It mainly benefits imports from Brazil, Paraguay and Argentina. The US Cattlemen’s Association, the National Cattlemen’s Beef Association and the Farm Bureau all oppose it. The administration announced $8 billion in farm relief in Kansas on October 19, where an unexpectedly close Senate race is under way.

  • Supply: The herd is near its smallest since 1951, driven mainly by drought. Other factors are meatpacking plant closures, labour shortages from ICE raids on Kansas packing plants, and the border closure over New World screwworm.
  • Costs: Record cattle prices have not produced the expected profits, because of high interest rates, war-driven fuel and fertilizer costs, and drought-driven feed costs.
  • Texas A&M economist David Anderson: “We really don’t have much evidence of herd expansion.”

Ebola outbreak passes 4,700 deaths as attackers kill nurse in DR Congo

Attackers killed a nurse and badly injured another health worker on Saturday in Bafwasende, Tshopo province, as they responded to an Ebola alert. It is the latest in a series of attacks on responders. In September a politician in North Kivu was beaten to death after defending the response publicly. WHO Director-General Tedros said responders “must be able to work safely.” Regional director Mohamed Janabi said: “Health is NOT a target.”

The outbreak, which began in May, is the deadliest in Congo’s history. The DRC Health Ministry reports more than 9,800 cases and more than 4,700 deaths; the US CDC figure is 9,668 confirmed cases and 4,649 deaths. It has reached at least eight provinces. It is the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no licensed vaccine or specific therapy.

  • Spread: Cases have been reported in four countries across eastern, central and western Africa, the widest spread of any African Ebola outbreak. Nigeria has its first case in 12 years: a foreign national who arrived from Uganda, fell ill October 7, and was first treated for malaria in Abuja. Forty-four contacts are being monitored. Kenya has nine cases, four of them health workers, all linked to one index patient.
  • Response: Africa CDC’s Jean Kaseya has called an emergency virtual meeting of health ministers for October 28. WHO is urging stronger border surveillance but opposes travel and trade restrictions. Uganda has ordered 21-day quarantine for citizens returning from the DRC.
  • Context: Distrust of a corrupt government’s handling of the outbreak, with some dismissing Ebola as a hoax, is fuelling violence.

US measles cases reach 4,588, Pennsylvania accounts for nearly 30 percent

CDC’s latest update, with data as of October 22, counts 4,588 confirmed measles cases this year, more than double last year’s total. There were 178 new cases in the past week and 508 in two weeks, more than the 465 reported in all of 2022–2024. There are 43 outbreaks, and 96 percent of cases are outbreak-linked; 47 jurisdictions are affected. Ten percent of patients have been hospitalized, including 16 percent of children under five. CDC acknowledges three deaths, but Pennsylvania alone reports five, with a sixth under investigation.

  • Pennsylvania: 1,338 cases across 44 counties (16 last year). Some parents are vaccinating infants early.
  • Other states: Wisconsin 310, Ohio 262, Kentucky 197, New York 162 (up from 126 a week earlier). Minnesota is reporting new clusters.
  • Vaccination: 96 percent of cases were unvaccinated or of unknown status. A poll finds most Americans are unsure of RFK Jr.’s position on the MMR vaccine.
  • Elimination: The US, which declared measles eliminated in 2000, is expected to lose that status this year. Andrew Pavia estimates true cases since 2024 are “probably greater than 20,000.”

Czech public broadcasters to strike as Babis coalition fast-tracks funding cut

The most significant cultural fight of the past day is in Prague. On Sunday, thousands of people, tens of thousands by some counts, filled Old Town Square against a bill to abolish household licence fees and fund Czech Television and Czech Radio directly from the state budget, cutting their funding by about 15 percent from as early as next year. Billionaire Prime Minister Andrej Babiš’s coalition, which includes two far-right parties, pushed the bill to a fast-track vote at 4:36 a.m. on Saturday. Protesters chanted that time back at the government. Babiš says the change is about “transparency.” Critics point to Orbán’s Hungary and Fico’s Slovakia, where putting broadcasters on the state budget was the first step to political control. Public media staff will strike on November 3, backed by unions, university rectors and churches. A rally is planned at Letná on November 15, two days before the anniversary of the 1989 Velvet Revolution.

The case shows a wider pattern. Across Europe and the US, governments are ending the independent financing of cultural and journalistic institutions and making them depend on the state budget, from the NEA and NEH to public broadcasters.

NBC’s Filmed in Gaza and the limits of “context”

NBC News’ 51-minute documentary Filmed in Gaza follows producer Samed Abu Zarifa and videographer Samir Al Boji. They were the network’s only reporting team on the ground from October 2023 until their evacuation in 2025, and their footage reached millions. The film has won an Online News Association award. It records air strikes, emergency medicine and the deaths of colleagues and friends, as well as NBC producer Lawahez Jabari’s efforts to get the two men out as their health failed. It comes as the Gaza Health Ministry’s count passes 74,500 dead and Israel bombs the Islamic University. The film confirms that the scale of the destruction can no longer be kept off network television, but also that the networks still frame it within Washington’s account of the war. Its opening titles start the story on October 7. (Full WSWS review summarized below.)

Opposition leader Luisa González arrested in Ecuador — Andrea Lobo

After the COB’s betrayals, Bolivian workers launch new wave of struggles against fuel hikes and IMF austerity — César Uco

President Rodrigo Paz abolished Bolivia’s last diesel subsidy on September 18, raising the price from Bs 9.80 to 17.95 a litre, nearly five times the Bs 3.72 of December 2025. A state of exception banning road blockades has been extended to the end of the year. The government has a $1.9 billion IMF program, and the IMF projects GDP to fall 3 percent in 2026. Since the June 29 currency float the boliviano has lost 68.5 percent against the dollar. Inflation reached 20.4 percent in 2025, a 36-year high, and poverty 44.7 percent. Twice this year COB leader Mario Argollo signed deals ending general strikes without restoring subsidies: on January 11, and on June 19, after a roughly 50-day uprising in which the Ombudsman recorded 22 deaths and 583 arrests. The COB now says it is “in opposition” but has named no date for action. Urban teachers held a 24-hour national strike on October 24, and police used tear gas and rubber bullets on October 21 and 24. Fourth insolvency in 6 years leaves 12,000 Galeria workers facing store closures in Germany — Marianne Arens

Galeria filed for insolvency in Düsseldorf on October 2. Up to 33 of its 83 remaining stores are to close in January. Federal insolvency pay covers 9,650 posts only until December 31, and pay for 1,600 food and restaurant staff is uncertain from November. The chain once employed 160,000 in 300 stores; about 7 percent remain. Arens blames René Benko’s “Signa model,” which sold off the properties and leased them back at rents of up to 20–30 percent of sales. Benko, convicted of fraud, has been free on an ankle tag since mid-October. She also blames current owners Bernd Beetz and Richard Baker’s NRDC, financed with loans at up to 15 percent from Bain Capital and Gordon Brothers. She documents Verdi’s role: the 2019 deal that paid Benko €70 million and took workers out of the sectoral agreement (wages now up to 30 percent below the sector rate), the 2020 “social contract” closing 42 stores, and further concessions in 2022 and 2024. Handelsblatt: “a horrible end is now simply more sensible than horror without end.” Verdi is again looking for an investor.

This week in history: October 26–November 1

  • 25 years ago (October 29, 2001): Japan’s Diet passed the Anti-Terrorism Special Measures Law under Koizumi, the first authorization since 1945 for Japanese forces to operate abroad, in logistical support of the US war in Afghanistan. It was passed with a secrecy law limiting access to military information and new police access to private email. Japan was looking for a share of Middle East oil after paying $13 billion toward the 1991 Gulf War and getting nothing from it.
  • 50 years ago (October 26, 1976): Apartheid South Africa declared Transkei “independent,” the first of ten Bantustans. About 3 million Xhosa speakers lost their South African citizenship, including more than a million working in the cities, to secure a supply of cheap migrant labour and divide the working class on ethnic lines four months after Soweto. The UN rejected it 134–0, with the US abstaining.
  • 75 years ago (October 29, 1951): Sixteen US Navy aircraft bombed a party and military meeting at Kapsan, North Korea, with 1,000-pound bombs and napalm, killing 510–530 people by the Navy’s own count. CIA-trained agents had found the meeting. It came four days after armistice talks resumed at Panmunjom. The US dropped some 635,000 tons of bombs on Korea, and 2–3 million Koreans died in the war.
  • 100 years ago (October 31, 1926): A shot grazed Mussolini’s coat in Bologna. Fascists lynched 15-year-old Anteo Zamboni on the spot, and historians doubt he fired. The attempt was used to justify the “exceptional decrees,” which banned all non-fascist parties, unions and newspapers, removed Gramsci and other deputies from parliament, created a political police and a special tribunal, and restored the death penalty.

Left Voice

It points to Minneapolis, where unions did not back a follow-up strike after Border Patrol killed Alex Pretti, and criticizes Lula’s government for “ultimately answering to imperialism.” But the program is to pressure union leaders, use the UAW’s call for a 2028 general strike “as a reason to act now,” and urge DSA to fund “committee-style bodies” and independent candidates. Its model is the Argentine FIT-U’s Myriam Bregman campaign. Position: it treats the bureaucratic apparatus as a vehicle to be pushed left rather than an obstacle to be replaced by rank-and-file committees, and treats DSA, a faction of the Democratic Party, as a partner. It sets out no perspective of international socialist revolution.
  • “Mamdani’s honeymoon is coming to an end for many on the left” (James Dennis Hoff, October 23). Only the headline could be checked. The title presents disillusionment with Mamdani as a “honeymoon” ending, not as confirmation of what DSA is as a class formation.

Assessment: Left Voice is putting itself forward as the left critic of DSA, to win disillusioned young people as Mamdani’s record becomes clear, while keeping them oriented to the unions and DSA. No references to the ICFI.

Socialist Appeal and RCP (IMT)

  • “After 10 October – the next steps for the RCP” (October 22–23). It reports a wave of youth strikes and uprisings in Europe and says the RCP has grown fastest since a Met Police raid on the party. The “next step” it names is Revolution Festival, a “school of communism.” Position: revolutionary rhetoric and party-building in place of a program for the working class. It says nothing on the role of the unions, on Starmer’s Labour government, or on the war.
  • “Europe: A mirror to Britain’s future.” It argues that the European upheaval alarms the British ruling class and is coming to Britain, and that communists must “prepare.” The analysis is impressionistic, with no account of the bureaucracies, which in France and Belgium are now limiting the protests to one-day strikes.

Assessment: The IMT is turning youth radicalism into recruitment for its events, with no strategy for organizing the working class independently of the apparatus. No attacks on the ICFI were found in any tendency’s output.

What the WSWS should cover today

1. Brazil: Lula wins fourth term as the Bolsonaros, backed from Washington, move to overturn the vote

Flávio Bolsonaro’s call to annul the election, with a Trump State Department reportedly working for his family’s return, is a direct threat of a second coup attempt in four years. The analysis must also cover the PT: a fourth Lula term with a right-wing Congress, a business coalition and austerity at the center will deepen the conditions that brought the far right within 3.5 points of power.

Recommended reading The Standard (AFP) Brazil’s Lula clinches poll victory, Bolsonaro seeks to annul election

2. Serbia: Vucic falls after two years of mass struggle. What next for the student movement?

The defeat of the SNS after the Novi Sad collapse is one of the most important results of the global youth rebellion the WSWS has been tracking. Von der Leyen’s congratulations show that Brussels plans to use the student list for EU integration. The WSWS should set out a program for the Serbian working class independent of the EU and the bourgeois opposition.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Serbia’s Vucic concedes election defeat to student-led movement

3. Ebola outbreak, now the deadliest in Congo’s history, spreads to four countries

More than 4,700 deaths from a strain with no vaccine, attacks on health workers fuelled by distrust of a corrupt state, and cases now in Nigeria and Kenya. This is a public health emergency shaped by imperialist looting of eastern Congo and cuts to global health aid. It fits the WSWS’s pandemic coverage.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Attackers kill nurse, injure health worker responding to DRC Ebola case

4. Israel’s election: a choice between two parties of genocide

Both blocs reject a Palestinian state. The opposition criticizes Netanyahu for failing to win the war, not for the slaughter, while Arab parties face disqualification. A piece on election eve would show that no Knesset coalition will change the course of war against Gaza and Iran, which is tied to Washington’s plans for after the midterms.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Israel’s election: Five key things to know

5. Trump’s anti-Muslim incitement in Michigan and the Democrats’ response

A sitting president calling a Senate candidate a “jihadist” who needs a metal detector is incitement against Michigan’s large Arab and Muslim working-class population, as war with Iran is being prepared. The piece could tie this to Damon’s treatment of El-Sayed and Obama and the Democrats’ own war record.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Trump dials up Islamophobia against Abdul El-Sayed at Michigan Senate rally

6. “Total control of Venezuela”: Machado’s return and the US oil grab

Trump’s boast that Washington runs Venezuela and is “taking out millions of barrels of oil a week,” while it sidelines its own long-time regime-change candidate, shows the real aims of the January intervention. It also bears on PSL’s claims about Venezuela’s “revolution.”

Recommended reading The Standard (Reuters) Venezuela opposition leader Maria Corina Machado returns to Venezuela

7. Czech public media workers strike November 3 against the Babis government

The overnight push to put Czech Television and Radio under budget control, by a billionaire-led coalition with far-right partners, follows the Orbán and Fico model. The November 3 strike is a chance to address journalists and cultural workers on defending democratic rights through the working class.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera Thousands protest in Prague against public media funding reforms

8. Europe faces winter of gas rationing as the war’s costs land on workers

Storage is at 72 percent, prices are up 140 percent, and EU ministers are discussing rationing for households. The IEA chief expects hardship this year and next. The cost of the Iran war is landing on European workers while US energy firms make “hundreds of billions,” the material basis of the protest wave from France to Belgium and Spain.

Recommended reading Al Jazeera ‘Life will not be easy for Europe this year,’ IEA chief warns
