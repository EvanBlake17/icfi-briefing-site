1. US strikes Qeshm island as Trump weighs a new-moon offensive

Trump on Friday confirmed new US strikes on Qeshm, the Iranian island in the Strait of Hormuz. They were the first since Tuesday’s midterm defeat, which Trump blamed on the war. He called them minor hits on air-defence systems and said the US knocked out an anti-aircraft device “in about 15 minutes, maybe five.” Iranian state media reported two missile strikes in the Suza area of Qeshm. Iran’s army said it destroyed a US MQ-9 drone over the island, and semi-official media reported no casualties. Central Command called the situation “relatively calm.”

The strikes followed White House war-planning meetings on Wednesday and Thursday. Officials who spoke to Fox News noted that November 9 brings a new moon, which they called favorable for military operations. Axios, citing two US officials, said Trump is weighing three options: new strikes, continued economic pressure, or a deal with significant concessions. Trump said the US is “dealing with them now” and has “made a lot of progress with Iran.” On Thursday he said: “I would rather not destroy the rest of the country if possible but maybe I’ll have to.” Qatar’s Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, speaking at Oxford, said Washington and Tehran should not wait for a “perfect moment” to make a deal.

Key facts: - Force posture: The USS George Washington and USS George H.W. Bush are in the Arabian Sea. The USS Theodore Roosevelt was due in Singapore on Friday and is at least two weeks from the Gulf (CBS News, cited by WSWS). Central Command is reportedly “finalizing preparations” but has received no new order. - Administration split: Vice President JD Vance posted on X: “The reason we lost is the war.” Axios reports that Vance and Witkoff favor a deal, while Hegseth and Netanyahu want combat resumed. - Shipping: The Greek-managed tanker MT Velos Sapphire was hit by a suspected missile near Bahrain on Thursday, and one seafarer was killed. A UK maritime agency counted at least 30 attacks on commercial vessels in the region in October, a record. The IRGC claims seven more tankers hit mines on Thursday and Friday. Maritime officials have not confirmed this, and Trump accuses Iran of fabricating such incidents. - Bounty: The State Department is offering up to $20 million and possible relocation for information on Iran’s nuclear programme. It cites Iran’s stockpile of 60 percent-enriched uranium and the suspension of IAEA inspections since June 2025. - Diplomacy: Trump spoke with Qatar’s Emir Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani. Rubio attends a G7 foreign ministers’ meeting in Paris on Monday and Tuesday. - Congress: Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) told CNN he will force a vote on impeaching Hegseth next week, and Hegseth called the articles a “sham.” Democrats face a December 11 government funding deadline and have not committed to cutting off war funds.

Andre Damon wrote on the WSWS that Democratic leaders have offered only war powers resolutions that Trump would veto, “symbolic measures” that leave the war’s funding intact.

2. Trump makes Stephen Miller chief of staff three days after the midterm rout

On Friday Trump named Stephen Miller, 41, as White House chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles from Monday. He called Miller “one of my most trusted advisors.” Miller has been deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security adviser since January 2025 and drove the mass deportation campaign. In his first term he shaped the 2018 “zero tolerance” family-separation policy, the travel ban and the attempt to end DACA. Al Jazeera notes that he endorsed the seizure of Nicolás Maduro and argued that the US had a right to take Greenland. He is named in Mahmoud Khalil’s July 2026 lawsuit alleging a conspiracy to target non-citizen Palestinians.

Key facts: - Miller on Fox News after the vote: Democrats are a “party of freaks, weirdos, losers and crazies” who are “unworthy of power.” He called for a moratorium on immigration from “third world countries.” - Earlier statements: In May 2025 he said the administration had “set a goal of a minimum of 3,000 arrests for ICE every day.” He has said the world “is governed by strength, that is governed by force, that is governed by power.” - Minneapolis: After federal agents killed Renee Good and Alex Pretti in January, Miller called Pretti an “assassin.” Pretti was lawfully carrying a licensed weapon. - Democratic reaction: Hakeem Jeffries, the likely next Speaker, called Miller a “dangerous xenophobe and reckless extremist.” Chuck Schumer said Trump “learned nothing” from the election. - Republican unease: Marjorie Taylor Greene warned that the Miller–Wiles shake-up will sink Republican chances in 2028.

Patrick Martin argued on the WSWS that Democratic objections center on the threat Miller poses to their plans for bipartisan cooperation, including on escalating the US-NATO war against Russia.

3. Paraguay’s Senate ousts its president 44 to 0 over the Ueno Bank collapse

Paraguay’s Senate removed President Santiago Peña on Friday by 44 votes to 0, with one abstention. The Chamber of Deputies had voted 78 to 0 to impeach him after Congress rejected his offer to resign. Vice President Pedro Alliana, 52, was sworn in as the country’s 53rd president, and Congress declined to open proceedings against him. Prosecutors placed Peña under house arrest on suspicion of breach of trust and other crimes. The ruling Colorado Party, in power almost continuously since 1948, backed the removal, led by former president Horacio Cartes, Alliana’s patron.

The crisis began on November 3. The central bank opened a “resolution procedure” against Ueno Bank, Paraguay’s fifth-largest by deposits, after its solvency ratio fell to 5.56 percent against a 12 percent minimum. The bank’s app, cards and transfers went down overnight. About three million clients were locked out of accounts and pension payments, nearly two-thirds of the country’s adults.

Key facts: - Public money: About $800 million in public funds fueled Ueno’s growth, including about $355 million in state pension money (Al Jazeera). The WSWS, citing Reuters and El País, puts the Social Security Institute’s exposure at about $380 million and says only 30 percent of deposits are insured. - Peña’s ties: He was identified in 2023 as a shareholder in Ueno’s parent company and says he sold in early 2025. The WSWS reports that he appointed Carlos Carvallo, former head of the bank’s predecessor, as central bank president, and Ueno got its licence two months later. A warrant has now been issued for Carvallo’s arrest. - Charges: At least 27 people face criminal charges, including lead shareholder Miguel Vázquez, for alleged fraud, breach of trust, misappropriation and influence peddling. - Insider lending: Related-party lending reached $628 million against a legal limit of about $80 million (WSWS). - Markets: The guaraní fell 8.8 percent against the dollar in a week (WSWS). - Peña’s farewell: “I take my leave with gratitude, while fully acknowledging the anguish of those who are currently awaiting answers.”

Cartes said the impeachment would restore “the republic’s institutions,” but the Colorado Party that built the bank’s state backing is now posing as its prosecutor. Juan Martinez and Andrea Lobo argue on the WSWS for nationalising the banks under workers’ control.

4. Death toll from Russian strike on Zaporizhzhia aid point reaches 50

Ukraine’s Emergency Service said on Friday that the death toll from a Russian bombing of a humanitarian aid distribution point in Komyshuvakha, Zaporizhzhia region, has reached 50. It is one of the deadliest attacks on civilians this year. It came a day after the six-day localised truce in Oleshky, brokered by Melania Trump, expired. That truce allowed 800 residents to be evacuated.

Russia then hit Kyiv with ballistic missiles. Mayor Vitali Klitschko wrote: “The enemy is hitting infrastructure with ballistic missiles.” Two people were injured and a building housing a kindergarten was damaged, and debris struck a five-storey apartment block. Zelensky had warned that Russia was preparing strikes on energy targets and bridges.

Key facts: - Scale of bombardment: In the week to November 6, Russia launched about 2,346 drones and more than 80 missiles. The peak came on November 3, with 370 drones and 76 missiles. - Post-war guarantees: Zelensky said Kyiv wants specific Western guarantees on post-war security support. Talks in Brussels included the US, UK, France, Germany, the EU, the World Bank and BlackRock. - Abu Dhabi: US-led Ukrainian–Russian talks are due to open in Abu Dhabi, the first since February.

BlackRock’s presence at the table over Ukraine’s post-war “security” shows that reconstruction is being set up as an investment opportunity.

5. Ebola passes 10,000 cases in Congo as massacre empties a treatment post

The Africa Centres for Disease Control has recorded more than 10,000 Ebola cases in the Democratic Republic of Congo in the current Bundibugyo-strain outbreak, with more than 5,300 deaths. Ituri is the epicentre, and North Kivu accounts for almost half of new infections. The outbreak has passed 2018–2020 as the DRC’s largest and deadliest.

Overnight, armed men believed to belong to the ISIL-affiliated Allied Democratic Forces raided Cantine, about 50 km west of Beni in North Kivu. They killed at least 33 people and burned more than 50 houses and a health post. Most victims were shot, and others were burned or hacked with machetes. Christian Kakule, medical director of the Kelekele Hospital Centre, said 180 patients fled, including suspected cases and one confirmed Ebola case. He warned that when probable cases are “out in the open,” they can spread the disease further.

Key facts: - Security failure: Civil society leader Justin Paluku called the raid “a major security failure.” - Armed groups: About 100 armed groups operate in eastern DRC, and the Rwanda-backed M23 holds key cities. The ADF killed 66 people in July 2025.

Massacres are now scattering patients from treatment centres, with the region already under armed occupation. Together these could turn the epidemic into a regional catastrophe, and the WSWS has not yet covered it (see coverage suggestions).

6. Herzog to pick a prime minister-designate as 67 Knesset members back Eisenkot

President Isaac Herzog said he will name a candidate to form a government on Sunday after the inconclusive October 27 election. In consultations, 67 of 120 Knesset members endorsed former army chief Gadi Eisenkot and 46 endorsed Netanyahu. United Torah Judaism withheld support from Netanyahu because ultra-Orthodox Israelis have not been exempted from military service.

Key facts: - Seats: Netanyahu’s right-wing and religious bloc holds 53 seats. The four Jewish-led parties backing Eisenkot hold 48, and Arab-led parties a record 19. A majority is 61. - Arab-led parties: Ra’am (8 seats) is willing to join an Eisenkot government. The Joint List (11) would support him from outside. Eisenkot rules out including either unless they meet conditions, including recognising Hamas as a “terrorist organisation.” Ra’am’s Mansour Abbas supports disarming Hamas but will not use the label. - Ahmad Tibi (Joint List): Rejecting partnership would prove Israel is an “ethnocracy, not an absolute democracy for all citizens.” - Timeline: The first nominee gets 28 days, extendable by 14. If all attempts fail, the Knesset dissolves and a new election follows within 90 days, with Netanyahu as caretaker. Analyst Nehru Jamhour told Al Jazeera that a drawn-out impasse is Netanyahu’s preferred outcome.

The “alternative” to Netanyahu is a former chief of staff who led the military through the Gaza war. He demands that Palestinian citizens’ parties endorse the state’s language on Hamas as the price of propping him up.

EU prosecutors raid Spain’s rail agencies after the Urda crash

Stocks steady after Thursday’s rout as hiring beats forecasts

US measles cases reach highest level since 1991

The CDC’s November 6 update reports 5,182 confirmed measles cases in 2026 as of November 5, the highest count since 1991. That is more than double 2025’s 2,289 and eighteen times 2024’s 285. Cases span 47 jurisdictions, including 18 among international visitors, in 43 outbreaks, and 4,993 cases (96 percent) are outbreak-linked. Children under five account for 18 percent and ages 5–19 for 41 percent.

The underlying cause is falling vaccination. Kindergarten MMR coverage fell from 95.2 percent in 2019–20 to 92.4 percent in 2025–26, below the 95 percent needed for community protection. The CDC estimates this leaves about 280,000 kindergartners unprotected.

  • Death counts: The CDC counts four deaths. Pennsylvania, the hardest-hit state with 1,487 cases, reports six. The CDC first included two unvaccinated Pennsylvania deaths, then removed them after Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. questioned whether measles caused them.
  • New definitions: The CDC says it is working with state epidemiologists on a “standard definition” of measles deaths. Experts say this breaks with long-standing WHO and CDC practice. Pennsylvania counts any death within 30 days of symptom onset unless an unrelated event caused it.

Elimination status, declared in 2000, is effectively gone. The federal health apparatus is now revising the definition of a death to shrink the toll, the same method of concealment used throughout the COVID pandemic.
