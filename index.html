1. Israel’s election ends without a majority as Palestinian citizens turn out in record numbers

Tuesday’s Knesset election left neither bloc with a governing majority. With just over 55% of ballots counted (Times of Israel figures cited by Al Jazeera), Netanyahu’s Likud-led coalition had 56 of 120 seats, Gadi Eisenkot’s opposition bloc 49, and the unaligned Arab-led parties 15. Exit polls differed. Channel 12 gave Netanyahu 52 and Eisenkot’s Yashar alliance 49. Channel 13 had them tied at 51. Likud, at about 27 seats, remains the largest single party. Arab-led lists were projected at about 19 seats, nearly double their previous 10. Turnout was 77.7% according to the Central Elections Committee, the highest since 1999 and 7.1 points above 2022.

The deadlock leaves the Palestinian citizens of Israel holding a block of seats that neither Zionist camp will accept as partners. Netanyahu argues that Eisenkot cannot govern without them. Eisenkot has ruled out any arrangement with Arab parties, citing a “lack of shared values.” On election day Netanyahu posted on X that Arab voters were “Muslim Brotherhood supporters” who were “raiding the polling stations and stealing the elections.” He shared a photo of a long queue outside a polling station and called the vote “a matter of life and death for us.” The legal center Adalah reported slowed or stopped queues in Arab-majority towns and missing Arabic ballot slips, and filed complaints with the election committees. WTI crude moved back above $100 early Wednesday, and analysts cited the deadlock as one factor.

Key facts: - Netanyahu: “Likud party is the largest party – nobody can compete with us.” He has called for a “strong, right-wing” government. - Eisenkot: “The old era is over, and Israel is starting a new era.” He said the projections showed “complete lack of confidence in Netanyahu and the October 7 government.” - Yair Lapid: “The Netanyahu bloc has suffered a crushing defeat… a total collapse.” Foreign Minister Gideon Saar called opposition claims “premature.” - Arab parties: Mansour Abbas (United Arab List) called Netanyahu’s posts a “scare tactic to mobilise right-wing voters.” Joint List leader Yousef Jabareen said Netanyahu knows “we can put an end to his bloody reign.” Faten Gattas (Joint List), per Reuters: “We refuse the occupation and racism, and we will remain here.” Balad’s Sami Abu Shehadeh: “There are racists for whom these pictures are frightening.” - Timeline: The final tally is due Friday and official results on Nov. 4. President Herzog then nominates a prime minister-designate, who has up to 42 days to form a coalition. Analysts expect smaller parties to decide the outcome.

The election offers the working class no way out of the war. Netanyahu and Eisenkot differ over who should run it, not over whether to wage it.

2. Ebola passes 10,000 cases as unpaid health workers strike in Congo

The Democratic Republic of Congo’s Ministry of Public Health reports 10,060 confirmed cases and 4,887 deaths in the Bundibugyo-strain outbreak declared on May 15. It is the DRC’s deadliest and fastest-growing Ebola outbreak and the second-largest on record, after West Africa in 2014–16. CIDRAP calls it the fastest-growing outbreak ever recorded. It now covers 66 health zones in eight provinces. There is no approved vaccine or treatment for the Bundibugyo strain. The US CDC’s slightly lagging count is 9,964 cases and 4,841 deaths, a case-fatality rate of about 48%.

Containment is failing. About 80% of new cases fall outside known transmission chains. Africa CDC says at least 95% of new cases must come from known contacts for the outbreak to be controlled. Health workers have struck over wages unpaid since August, and treatment centres are overwhelmed. Maternal deaths have doubled in some provinces as the outbreak crowds out other care. A nurse was killed and another health worker attacked outside an Ebola clinic in Tshopo province. WHO Director-General Tedros: “Everyone working to protect communities from this deadly disease must be able to work in dignity and safety.”

Key facts: - Spread: Nigeria is the fourth African country affected. Its index patient, a 42-year-old Ugandan woman married to a Swiss national, has been airlifted to Switzerland with her asymptomatic husband, and 52 contacts are being monitored. Africa CDC says both the Kenyan and Nigerian index patients passed through Uganda’s Entebbe International Airport on Oct. 3. It is investigating the airport as a possible site of transmission, since the Nigerian patient never travelled to the DRC. - Kenya: 9 confirmed cases and 2 deaths; 20 contacts released after 21 days and 98 still in quarantine. - Vaccine trial: A three-arm trial began in Bunia on Friday. It tests Merck’s Ervebo, which was developed for the Zaire strain, against experimental vaccines from Moderna, Oxford and the Serum Institute of India. Regeneron has extended its BARDA contract for the antibody treatment Inmazeb, which is also proven only against Zaire.

Five months in, the front-line workers fighting the outbreak have gone unpaid since August, and none of the available countermeasures were designed for this strain.

3. Russia hits Kyiv’s South Bridge as Finland and Norway open a line to Putin

Overnight into Tuesday, Russia struck Kyiv with 16 ballistic missiles and more than 280 drones and hit the South Bridge over the Dnipro. Zelenskyy said Russian forces struck again while repair crews were responding, calling it “a serious escalation in Russia’s campaign targeting our logistics.” Russia’s Defence Ministry said it targeted transport infrastructure in Kyiv, a logistics hub in Kharkiv region, industrial sites, and cargo vessels it said served military purposes. Deaths and injuries were reported in Kharkiv and Pavlohrad, with no figures given. In Russian-held Zaporizhzhia region, a Ukrainian drone hit a bus, killing one and injuring 30. A drone attack in Russia’s Bryansk region killed one and wounded six.

The same day, Finland’s President Alexander Stubb and Norway’s Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre held a 90-minute call with Putin, described as “frank and substantive.” Stubb: “Ending the war requires direct discussions.” Ukraine’s Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said the call had been coordinated with Kyiv. Other European capitals objected openly. Denmark’s Frederiksen said she was “very sceptical” and urged Europe to “stick together rather than going solo.” Sweden’s Kristersson said earlier German and French contacts had produced “very modest” results and that Russia seeks submission, not peace. Finnish MEP Mika Aaltola warned of a “lack of coordination.” The Kremlin readout said lasting peace is possible “only if the legitimate interests of the Russian Federation are fully and unconditionally taken into account.” It blamed the “confrontational policies” of EU and NATO states, and Lavrov dismissed the call as Europe angling for a seat at the table.

Key facts: - Starlink: CBS News obtained photos showing a Ukrainian air-defence radar’s Starlink connection falling to 0.1 MB/s on Oct. 19. That afternoon a Russian Geran-4 jet drone killed five people in a Kharkiv park, including a 1-year-old girl. SpaceX had throttled Ukraine’s access six days earlier. Musk wrote on X that “Ukraine military violated Starlink terms of service and used it for offensive drone strikes.” He said the US government controls changes to Starshield, the military network that runs on the same satellites. The White House says Trump did not order the restrictions. - Kherson: A localized ceasefire took effect Tuesday in the Oleshky area so aid can reach the Russian-held town. Trump credited Melania Trump with securing it.

That the call happened at all, and that Copenhagen and Stockholm denounced it, shows the European powers split on how to manage a war they are all financing. A private oligarch’s decision to cut off Ukraine’s air-defence links shows how far military infrastructure now depends on him.

4. Pentagon puts the Iran war’s cost at $47.1 billion as munitions run short

A US Central Command estimate obtained by CBS News puts the cost of the war with Iran, now entering its ninth month, at at least $47.1 billion through mid-October. The figure excludes damage to US bases across the Middle East, many of which have been hit by Iranian missiles and drones. Three months ago Defense Secretary Hegseth told Congress the cost was about $37.5 billion. The Congressional Budget Office estimated last month that the war costs $2–3 billion a month while it continues. The administration’s supplemental military funding request, made earlier this year, has not passed.

On the diplomatic front, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian met Qatar’s Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani in Moscow on the sidelines of the 8th Gas Exporting Countries Forum summit, which Putin hosted. Iranian state TV said they “reviewed the current state of relations” and discussed energy cooperation. The readout did not mention the war. Qatar is both a mediator and a target, since Iranian missiles and drones have struck it. A Qatari foreign ministry spokesman urged Tehran to stop threatening Gulf states, and a Qatari delegation has visited Tehran to help set up a resolution with Washington. Iran says the US is “not serious” about talks.

Key facts: - Cost breakdown (CENTCOM): about $29 billion to replace expended munitions, $6.1 billion for lost equipment, and about $11.9 billion in incremental costs. - Stockpiles: The Pentagon inspector general warned last month of “strategic inventory shortfalls.” In September Trump claimed “virtually unlimited amounts of Mid to High Grade Ammunition.” - Duration: UN Ambassador Mike Waltz told the Financial Times the war could last another year. - Energy: Brent was around $115 at Wednesday’s Asian open, up 59% from before the war, amid fighting between Saudi Arabia and Yemen’s Houthis.

Patrick Martin noted in today’s WSWS Perspective that the administration is reported to be planning to resume bombing Iran after the Nov. 3 election. He argued that the costs of the “illegal war” are being loaded onto workers through prices and wages.

5. France debates a “rioter pays” law as Europe’s protest wave meets emergency powers

France’s National Assembly is debating a “rioter pays” bill this week. Rights groups and trade unionists say it guts the right to protest. Anyone who “knowingly joined a group” would be jointly liable for damage caused by any member, up to €10,000. The state could recover the money by seizing welfare benefits from those convicted, or from parents’ benefits when the defendant is a minor. The Human Rights League (LDH) and trade unionists signed a column in Le Monde demanding its withdrawal. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez defends it as “joint responsibility – not a collective one.” The National Rally supports the principle. RN senator Edwige Diaz objected only to the €10,000 cap. Nuñez offered his resignation last week after security forces were overwhelmed, and Macron refused it. Speaking in Marseille on Oct. 26, Macron said order was being upheld “without a state of emergency” but that one was not “taboo” if things deteriorated.

Student representatives have vowed to keep protesting despite the government’s education package. The package caps some class sizes, offers €4 billion in school renovation loans and €1 billion for campuses, indexes grants to inflation, and adds 15,000 university places. Nuñez said 1,435 people were arrested at Saturday’s demonstrations. A larger national day of action is called for Nov. 5. The beneficiary in the polls is the far right. One survey puts Le Pen at 32–33.5% against Mélenchon at 17%, with a runoff expected in May 2027.

Key facts: - Arrests: about 3,700 since Oct. 17, citing Interior Ministry figures (WSWS). - Police bill: A “presumption of legitimate use of weapons” bill passed its first reading on July 7 and goes to the Senate this autumn. Amnesty International calls it a “license to kill.” - Italy: President Mattarella has signed a decree that allows arrests on the basis of video footage. It extends preventive custody of up to 12 hours to anyone aged 16 or over, creates a “violent participation” offence carrying up to five years and €20,000 in fines, and makes parents liable for their children. On Saturday, tens of thousands marched in Milan, Rome, Turin, Bologna, Florence, Naples, Genoa and smaller cities, called out by the online “Movimento 17 Ottobre.” Milan received 5,000 police, 500 paratroopers, armoured vehicles and drones (WSWS). - Spread: The WSWS reports the movement extending to Spain, Belgium, Serbia, Albania and Greece.

Alexandre Lantier wrote on the WSWS that the 2015 emergency, imposed under Hollande, allowed warrantless searches, protest bans and house arrests, and that its curfew and protest-ban powers were moved into ordinary law in 2017. On that basis he warns that Macron is preparing repression while the RN leads the polls.

6. Washington opens its doors to Flávio Bolsonaro after he flees Brazil

A senior State Department official told CBS News on Tuesday that Flávio Bolsonaro and his family will be allowed to enter the United States. He lost Sunday’s presidential runoff to Lula and then left the country. Brazil’s Supreme Court had served him on Monday with a sealed order to surrender his regular and diplomatic passports by noon Tuesday. It is unclear whether he complied. Flávio has refused to concede and has made unsubstantiated fraud claims. He is under investigation for money laundering and is linked to his father’s Jan. 8, 2023, coup attempt. The WSWS reports that the Milei government sheltered him in Argentina first and that he has applied for US asylum.

Lula called the departure “shameful”: “A person who sneaks out of their country must have something to hide.” Trump has called the election “rigged,” without evidence, and said of Flávio’s entry: “It would be OK with me.”

Key facts: - Result: Lula won more than 64 million votes to under 60 million for Flávio, a margin of nearly 4 points. - Family: Brother Eduardo Bolsonaro lives in Texas. He was convicted in absentia in June of illegally lobbying the Trump administration and sentenced to four years, and a warrant has been issued for his arrest. Jair Bolsonaro is serving a 27-year sentence under house arrest for the 2022–23 coup plot. After losing in 2022 he too fled to Florida, for about three months. - Ties: Flávio visited the White House in May.

Eric London argued on the WSWS that Washington is giving a would-be coup leader a base from which to contest the result. Mahmoud Khalil and Rümeysa Öztürk, by contrast, were detained for opposing US foreign policy, and Central American asylum seekers are turned away.

Middle East and North Africa

Jordan bombs southern Syria after the Karak church massacre

On Monday Jordan struck suspected ISIL sites in southern Syria, in retaliation for Friday’s gun attack on a Greek Orthodox church in Karak. That attack, claimed by ISIL, killed at least nine people and wounded 34. The strikes killed at least seven people in Deraa and Suwayda, including five members of one family, three of them children. Syria’s emergency ministry said it responded to four sites. Foreign Minister Ayman Safadi said the strikes aimed at “neutralising confirmed threats and imminent terrorist operations.” In a call with Syrian counterpart Asaad al-Shaibani he expressed “deep regret” for civilian deaths. Jordan says the operation was coordinated with Damascus, which denies it. The Nassib crossing, the only Jordan–Syria border post, reopened Wednesday morning, according to SANA. The two governments agreed to form a joint security committee.

Egyptian court orders Mada Masr shut

The New Cairo Misdemeanour Court on Tuesday ordered the closure of Mada Masr, one of Egypt’s last prominent independent news outlets, and the confiscation of its equipment. Editor-in-chief Lina Attalah was fined 3 million Egyptian pounds (about $53,400) for running an unlicensed website under Article 105 of the 2018 media law. She was taken into custody and released hours later, once the outlet paid. On her release she learned she had been sentenced in absentia in 2024 to one year in prison for “false news.” A retrial is set for Nov. 11.

  • Licence: Mada Masr applied for a licence in 2018 and 2020, learned of a rejection only in 2022, and has had a new application pending since June 2023. The site has been blocked inside Egypt since 2017, and this is the first of six cases against it in six years to reach trial.
  • Trial: The court refused to let the defence review the case file, submit evidence or call witnesses. With eight of Attalah’s lawyers present, the judge appointed a defence lawyer of the court’s own choosing over her objection.
  • Reactions: Amnesty’s Yasmine Ahmed called it “a crushing blow to press freedom in Egypt.” The Journalists’ Syndicate will appeal. The same day, prosecutors renewed the detention of six journalists from the fact-checking site Matsadaash.

Europe

Protesters storm pro-Vučić Informer TV in Belgrade

On Monday evening, the second night of celebrations after the Student List ended the Serbian Progressive Party’s 14 years in power, about 30 protesters burst into the Belgrade newsroom of Informer TV. They interrupted the broadcast and surrounded its owner and editor, Dragan Vučićević, with some demanding his arrest. Police cordoned off the pro-government Pink TV, where another crowd had gathered. The Independent Association of Journalists of Serbia filed on Tuesday to revoke Informer’s licence. Public broadcaster RTV in Vojvodina opened its evening news by apologizing for “uncritical, one-sided” reporting.

  • Media: The monitor CRTA found national TV gave government figures three-quarters of politicians’ airtime and portrayed the Student List negatively more than 80% of the time. Serbia ranks 104th of 180 in the 2026 RSF index, its worst ever.
  • Student List: The list has no clear leader. Its lead candidate, cardiologist Ilija Srdanović, urged supporters to stay “calm and dignified until the end of the electoral process.”
  • Vučićević: “On the very first day of [Student List] power, they want to ban the media.”

Spain deports migrants from Ceuta ahead of snap election

Spain deported 16 migrants from African, Middle Eastern and Asian countries from its North African enclave of Ceuta to Morocco, NGOs told Reuters. They were the first such deportations since late July, when about 80,000 people crossed from Morocco over 48 hours and at least 100 died. About 8,000 remain in a city of 80,000 residents, some sleeping on the streets. The Interior Ministry says the asylum claims had been rejected and that the EU considers Morocco “safe.” Amnesty’s Virginia Álvarez said “Morocco does not respect many human rights.” The crisis dominates the campaign for the Nov. 29 snap election. Tens of thousands have marched, and Vox leader Santiago Abascal calls Sánchez a “traitor.”

Russian court jails IT worker for 13 years over $1,500 in donations

The 2nd Western District Military Court convicted Yuri Belenkiy, 49, a former IT director at DiHouse who holds a Bulgarian residence permit, of financing “terrorism.” He was sentenced to 13 years, three in prison and ten in a strict-regime colony, and fined 500,000 roubles. According to RIA Novosti, he made 15 crypto transfers totalling just over 120,000 roubles (about $1,500) between 2022 and 2024 to Ukraine’s armed forces and the Azov battalion. Citing law-enforcement documents, Reuters reports that Binance supplied his transaction history at Moscow’s request, although it left the Russian market in 2023. Binance says it “does not make or enforce the laws of any jurisdiction.” The rights group First Department says about 10% of Russia’s 2025 treason and espionage cases involved donations to Ukrainian organisations.

Africa

Gunmen kill 62 in Nigeria’s Taraba state

Armed bandits attacked the remote village of Binnari in northeastern Taraba state on Friday. They killed at least 62 people and set fire to homes, shops, food stocks and motorcycles, according to Ladan Ayuba of the National Emergency Management Agency. Thousands have been displaced. Police spokesman Victor Mshelizah said the toll would rise as bodies are recovered. Amnesty International Nigeria called for an impartial investigation and reparations, saying official statements are “not enough.” Armed groups in the north fund themselves through kidnapping and illegal mining, and the Institute for Security Studies links the violence to arms smuggling across the Sahel.

Tanzania’s jailed opposition leader on hunger strike before election anniversary

CHADEMA leader Tundu Lissu ended a brief hunger strike on Tuesday after prison authorities allowed him a private meeting with his lawyers, who had been refused entry. The prison service denies that he was on hunger strike. Lissu survived 16 bullets in a 2017 assassination attempt. He was arrested in April 2025 after a rally demanding electoral reform and is charged with treason, which carries the death penalty, over a speech prosecutors say incited people to disrupt the 2025 election. Judgement is due Nov. 6. A government-appointed inquiry found at least 518 people were killed in the violence after last year’s Oct. 29 vote, which returned President Samia Suluhu Hassan. CHADEMA puts the toll at about 2,000. The party has declared seven days of mourning from Thursday.

Asia-Pacific

Australian establishment uses attack on MP Helen Haines to call for curbs on speech

Federal parliament suspended normal business on Monday for tributes to independent MP Helen Haines. She was struck repeatedly by a four-wheel drive outside her Wangaratta electorate office a week earlier and has since lost both legs. An orange cardboard cockatoo, the emblem of her campaigns, sat in her seat. Opposition Leader Angus Taylor said her return would be a “resounding moment for democracy.” Home Affairs Minister Tony Burke relayed her husband’s request that politicians consider how their words affect “vulnerable listeners.”

Police have charged Brendan Nugent with serious violence offences but not terrorism, and are examining whether he had an acute psychotic episode. Oscar Grenfell reported on the WSWS that Nugent’s posts describe chronic pain and financial hardship, and that he could not get NDIS support while on a disability pension. His lawyer says he has idiopathic transverse myelitis. Even so, Albanese spoke of “lowering the temperature.” Deputy PM Richard Marles said an “isolated, random, terrible event” explanation “feels inadequate.” Liberal MP Julian Leeser called it “an act of political violence,” and One Nation’s Barnaby Joyce linked it to the August 2025 pro-Palestine Harbour Bridge march. Grenfell argues the episode is being used to extend a “police-state framework” of hate-speech and protest laws ahead of struggles against war and austerity.

Justice Department demands Washington set aside 405 ballots six days before the vote

On Monday, federal prosecutors Neil Floyd and Pete Serrano wrote to Washington Secretary of State Steve Hobbs. They demanded that he segregate the ballots of 405 registered voters the Justice Department says are non-citizens, and gave him until today to comply. Floyd claimed more than 1,500 illegal ballots have been cast and called it “only the tip of the iceberg.” The Department of Homeland Security is expected to send a second list “imminently.” Hobbs said the list includes people who appear to have last voted in the 1990s, one of them in 1994, and that the federal system wrongly flags citizens. He said the timing was meant to cast “unfounded suspicion” on the results and that his office “will only respond to requests that follow due process.” Late Monday an appeals court dismissed DOJ’s bid to force California to set aside about 20,000 mail ballots.

  • Voter-roll suits: The administration has sued 30 states and D.C. for unredacted voter rolls. Of those suits, 27 have been dismissed and three are pending, including the dismissed Washington suit.
  • Context: In today’s Perspective, Patrick Martin places the ballot challenges alongside the racist campaigning of Trump and Kennedy in Michigan (see WSWS coverage).

H5N1 spreads to 27 Washington dairy herds after first US human case of 2026

Washington state reported its first H5N1 detection in dairy cattle on Oct. 8. Within a week the count rose from 9 to 27 herds, 26 in Yakima County and one in Benton County. On Oct. 15 a Yakima County dairy worker tested positive, the first US human H5N1 case of 2026. The CDC still counts 72 human A(H5) cases since February 2024 and rates the public risk “low.” It no longer publishes animal detections, which are now reported by USDA, so tracking spread in herds depends on state reporting. Abroad, Cambodia reported its sixth human case of 2026. The CDC counted 12 human infections outside the US, with three deaths, between August 2025 and June 2026.

CDC: drug-resistant infections now kill 50,800 Americans a year

A new CDC antimicrobial-resistance threats report, based on 2023 data, estimates 9.7 million drug-resistant infections and 50,800 deaths a year in the US. The 2019 report estimated 2.8 million infections and 35,000 deaths, though the CDC says changed methods make direct comparison unreliable. Hospital-associated resistant infections alone caused more than 42,000 deaths and cost more than $12 billion.

  • Candida auris (urgent): 4,523 infections in 2023, a tenfold increase since 2019. Resistant infections are fatal in 29–62% of cases.
  • Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (urgent): 14,878 cases, 67% of them acquired in the community.
  • Gonorrhoea: added to the urgent list, with few effective antibiotics left.
  • Meningococcus: ciprofloxacin-resistant cases more than doubled from 2019 to 2023. The first macrolide-resistant pertussis cases since 2018 were found in 2024 and 2025.

The report follows Merck’s withdrawal of an antibiotic for multidrug-resistant infections from the US market. Drug makers earn little from antibiotics, so the pipeline keeps shrinking even as resistance spreads.

Arts and culture

Kennedy’s “Arabs don’t produce literature” and the record he erased

The most significant cultural statement of the past 24 hours came from a cabinet secretary. Campaigning in Grand Rapids, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. declared that Arabs “don’t produce science” and “don’t produce literature.” Today’s WSWS Perspective answers with the record. Arabic and Islamic scholarship transmitted and extended the classical heritage, and gave European languages “algebra” and “algorithm.” The modern record includes Naguib Mahfouz’s Cairo trilogy and the poetry of Mahmoud Darwish and Adonis. Jokha Alharthi’s Celestial Bodies won the International Booker in 2019, and Zaha Hadid won the Pritzker. The Perspective also lists what US and Israeli wars have done to that culture: the burning of Iraq’s National Library, the killing of hundreds of Iraqi academics, and the destruction of every university in Gaza. The slur is aimed at Arab-American voters in Michigan, but it also serves a cultural policy that treats an entire civilization as having nothing to contribute.

On the same day, Mahmoud Khalil presented No Land to Stand On: Notes from Detention at Columbia (see United States). The memoir belongs to a growing body of prison writing that documents state repression of opposition to the Gaza genocide.

Pawlikowski’s Fatherland: Thomas Mann in the ruins of 1949

Paweł Pawlikowski’s Fatherland stars Hanns Zischler as Thomas Mann and Sandra Hüller as his daughter Erika. It follows Mann’s 1949 return to a Germany split in two, the trip during which Klaus Mann, who had asked Erika for protection, takes his own life. A Jacobin review by Corey Robin reads it as a tragedy of liberal humanism: “noble” but powerless before barbarism, with Mann a “preening moralist” whose refusal to collaborate cost him nothing. That reading leaves out the real history. Mann’s postwar position was shaped by the onset of the Cold War, which within three years drove him out of the United States. A film that sets the Manns against the background of a partitioned, rearming Europe deserves attention now, as Berlin reintroduces conscription.

Dakkota workers denounce Detroit plant closure: “They don’t care about us” — Jerry White

The plant builds instrument panels for Stellantis’s Jeep Grand Cherokee and Dodge Durango. It opened about five years ago with a $1.5 million state grant and a $3.5 million tax abatement, on the site of a closed high school. CEO Aaron Rivers blames lost work to a competitor. Global index provider threatens to cut Indonesia off from international investment — Nick Beams

MSCI has escalated its threat against Indonesia. In January it warned of a downgrade from “emerging” to “frontier.” It now says that unless problems are fixed by June, Indonesia could be moved to “standalone” status alongside Argentina, Ukraine and Zimbabwe.
