1. Washington readies a “short, intense” post-election assault on Iran

On Thursday the commander of US Central Command, Adm. Brad Cooper, briefed Trump at the White House on options for resuming the war on Iran. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine attended. According to the Wall Street Journal, Washington has told Gulf Arab governments to prepare for a “short, intense campaign” after Tuesday’s midterm elections. Trump said: “We may do another little Epic Fury.” Operation Epic Fury is the name of the bombing campaign that began on February 28 and killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei. Trump also told reporters the war will “get wrapped up soon.”

Sen. Chris Murphy (D-Conn.) received a classified briefing. Without disclosing details, he said the White House is assembling the means to strike civilian infrastructure across the region. “The plan is insane,” he wrote. “It will not achieve objectives and will set off a new cycle of escalation.” In Tehran, Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi warned of an “earth-shattering blow” if Iranians are harmed again. Al Jazeera, citing social media reports, says drones targeted Riyadh overnight. The Revolutionary Guards said four oil tankers caught fire and exploded in the Strait of Hormuz while sailing “unauthorised routes,” and Oman reported rescuing 25 people from one burning vessel. Germany complained publicly about soaring energy prices. As Kevin Reed noted on the WSWS, the February strikes came right after diplomatic talks in Geneva, so promises to hold off until after the vote are a statement about the electoral calendar, not about restraint.

Key facts: - Deployments (WSJ, via WSWS): Additional air defenses, munitions and supplies have been sent to the region. Personnel have been moved from vulnerable bases and diplomatic staff reduced. An extra Patriot battery has gone to Israel amid interceptor shortages. The USS Theodore Roosevelt carrier group and the USS Makin Island Amphibious Ready Group are expected by late November. - Back channel: The Washington Post’s David Ignatius reports that Vice President Vance wrote to Iran’s Supreme National Security Council secretary, Mohsen Rezaei, offering to meet and discuss a settlement that includes the nuclear program. Iranian media confirmed the letter arrived, but Tehran has not formally responded. - Negotiating gap: Washington demands an end to enrichment, removal of enriched uranium and guarantees for Hormuz shipping before any substantial economic relief. Tehran demands an end to the blockade, sanctions relief, access to frozen assets and a halt to hostilities. - Mediation: Pakistan’s military spokesman, Lt Gen Ahmed Sharif Chaudhry, told Reuters the army is “cautiously optimistic.” Army chief Field Marshal Asim Munir has visited Tehran twice this month, most recently with Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin. Qatari mediators arrived in Tehran on the same day. Chaudhry warned of “powerful spoilers and detractors” who believe the conflict serves them. - New regional bloc: Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey signed the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement on August 7, under which an attack on one is an attack on all. Chaudhry said Iran would be “most welcome” to join.

2. Appeals court again blocks Trump’s bid to set aside 20,000 California ballots

On Friday the 9th Circuit Court of Appeals rejected, for the second time in less than a week, the Trump administration’s petition to set aside about 20,000 California mail ballots that it says belong to “likely non-citizens.” The majority was Mark Bennett, a Trump appointee, and Gabriel Sanchez, a Biden appointee. “We decline the United States’s invitation to contravene the Elections Clause by undermining the Constitution’s plain text,” they wrote, and they voiced “major concerns” about disenfranchising eligible voters. Judge Patrick Bumatay, also a Trump appointee, dissented and called the petition a “reasonable request.” The administration sent its list to California on October 12 and sued on October 15. California asked for proof that anyone on the list is a foreign national and says none has been provided.

The administration is still pursuing the fight through other channels. The Justice Department issued dozens more subpoenas on Thursday to Democratic election officials and campaign aides in Alaska and California. In California, grand jury subpoenas went to local election officials after the courts rejected the ballot demand. Trump demanded that Democrat Mary Peltola and independent Dan J. Sullivan both be removed from Alaska’s Senate ballot, which he has no legal power to order, and tens of thousands of votes have already been cast. Asked whether he would accept the results, he said he would “if they are honest elections.” As Patrick Martin argued on the WSWS, given Trump’s record on January 6, 2021, that answer is effectively a pledge to block certification wherever Republicans lose.

Key facts: - Accuracy of the lists: A Los Angeles County official reviewing 7,035 allegedly noncitizen names found that nearly 40% were born in the US (WSWS). Nevada said in September that its federal list wrongly flagged citizens and that “none of the names was correct” (Al Jazeera). The DOJ’s letter to Washington state alleges about 400 cases among 5.1 million registered voters. - Prosecutions: The administration has charged 28 foreign nationals in California with election-related offences. Some told the Los Angeles Times they had never registered to vote, and some share names with eligible voters. - Stakes: California elects 52 House members on Tuesday, and its new district map could shift control of the House. Fourteen Senate seats are considered competitive. - Background: In July, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin claimed that 250,000 noncitizens were registered to vote in California, New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Nevada. Noncitizen voting in federal elections is illegal in every state.

3. Ebola death toll in Congo passes 5,000 as no vaccine exists for the strain

Government figures announced on Friday put deaths in the Democratic Republic of Congo’s Ebola outbreak above 5,000, with more than 10,000 infections since the outbreak was declared in May. It is the deadliest Ebola outbreak in Congo’s history and the second-largest ever recorded, behind the 2014–2016 West African epidemic, which infected more than 28,600 people and killed more than 11,000. The cause is the rare Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine or treatment. Al Jazeera, citing Reuters, says authorities are “yet to find a way to control” it, and blames delayed detection, armed conflict and overstretched health services. Africa CDC chief Jean Kaseya has admitted the continent is “not winning.”

As Benjamin Mateus argued on the WSWS, survival in this epidemic depends on citizenship and class. A Swiss couple was evacuated, in the WHO Africa director’s words, “because the couple are Swiss citizens,” while Congolese patients die in overflowing wards.

Key facts: - Mortality (health ministry, October 28, via WSWS): There have been 10,274 confirmed cases and 4,992 confirmed deaths, a case fatality ratio of 48.6%. Of the 7,758 patients whose illness has ended, about two in three died. Mortality tracks access to care: 34% in Bunia (Ituri) against 67% in Beni (North Kivu). - Spread: The virus has reached 67 of 178 health zones in eight of the country’s 26 provinces. North Kivu’s treatment centres are 86.3% full, and 154 patients are being treated outside them. At least 269 frontline health workers had been infected by October 18. In Tshopo, where health staff are on strike, contact tracing reaches only 69% of contacts. - Regional: According to the US CDC, there have been 31 cases outside Congo, including one diagnosed in France. Nigeria confirmed its first case on October 20, the fourth African country to do so after Uganda and Kenya. - Travel bans: On Friday Saudi Arabia suspended travel to and from Kenya, stopped issuing visas to Kenyans, and barred anyone who has been in an affected country in the past 21 days. Uganda imposed a 21-day quarantine on citizens returning from Congo. - Vaccines: The WHO-backed Tumaini trial began vaccinating contacts in Bunia on October 23 with Ervebo, which is licensed against the Zaire strain and has no proven effect against Bundibugyo. About 620,000 doses of a Bundibugyo-specific Oxford/Serum Institute vaccine have been made, but they cannot be used until regulators approve them. - Money: Donors have given $962 million. More than $700 million of it cannot be accounted for because most organisations have not reported how they spent it (Kaseya, October 15).

4. Germany’s “GenZ regelt!” protests draw 150,000 registrations as France braces for a third weekend

About 150,000 people have registered to protest today in roughly 30 German cities, including Berlin, Frankfurt and Cologne. The movement calls itself “GenZ regelt!” (“Gen Z has got this!”). It cites the cost of living, crumbling schools, government spending cuts and political decisions made with little regard for young people. Police are deploying thousands of extra officers. In Berlin, luxury jewellery and clothing stores have boarded up their windows.

France is entering its third consecutive weekend of mass protests. More than 40 Paris metro stations will close, face coverings are banned at demonstrations, and the interior minister has warned of a “very high risk” of disorder. Two weeks ago, protests over living costs and school conditions turned into large-scale looting and rioting, and last weekend saw further clashes. Alain Di Crescenzo, president of France’s Chamber of Commerce, told France Inter that “shopkeepers can’t wait for Saturday to be over” and that Halloween masks are “not helping matters.” Al Jazeera describes a wave of largely youth-led protests spreading across Europe over rising prices, deteriorating schools and a lack of future prospects. In Portugal, youth encampments are calling for an Iberian general strike on November 11 (WSWS).

Key facts: - The German mobilisation is the movement’s first mass test in Europe’s largest economy. It comes as Berlin complains of soaring energy prices tied to the Hormuz closure. - The French government’s response consists of closing metro stations, banning masks and pre-emptive police deployments. Business groups are publicly urging a firm crackdown. - Joseph Kishore’s Perspective today counts six weeks of marches by hundreds of thousands in France. He quotes the Wall Street Journal: “Don’t think it can’t happen in the US given the current state of American politics.”

5. US approves Patriot missiles for Ukraine that will not arrive until the 2030s

On Friday the State Department approved a possible sale to Ukraine of 500 PAC-3 Patriot interceptors, with parts and support, worth an estimated $2.1 billion. Two people briefed on the decision told Reuters the missiles have not yet been built and are not expected until “a couple of years into the 2030s.” Ukraine will pay through the US JUMPSTART programme, using contributions from Canada, Germany and Norway, and national funds financed by the EU’s Ukraine Support Loan. Lockheed Martin is the prime contractor. Zelenskyy said Kyiv waited almost a month for Washington’s sign-off while Russia escalated its strikes on energy and civilian infrastructure. The delays followed Trump’s diesel deal with Moscow and his criticism of Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries.

At the G20 foreign ministers’ meeting in Atlanta, Washington cancelled the traditional family photo after European ministers refused to stand with Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov. German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul said: “There will be no family photo featuring our European partners together with Mr Lavrov.” Secretary of State Marco Rubio opened the meeting with an appeal for cooperation on trade, energy, food and freedom of navigation. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas pressed for a special tribunal and claims commission against Russia. Separately, Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre held a 90-minute call with Putin. It was the first publicly acknowledged conversation between European leaders and Putin since July 2025, and Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen rebuked the two for “going solo.”

Key facts: - Arms industry: In July the US Army awarded Lockheed a contract worth up to $58.6 billion for Patriot interceptors. The company plans to triple PAC-3 capacity by the end of 2030, to 2,000 a year. Heavy munitions use in the Iran war has raised concern about US air-defence stockpiles. - G20: The US has excluded South Africa, a founding member. China sent Executive Vice Foreign Minister Ma Zhaoxu instead of Wang Yi. No joint communiqué is expected, and leaders meet in Miami on December 14–15. Trump’s sanctions waivers let Russia export diesel to ease US fuel prices before Tuesday’s vote. - Diplomacy: Støre said Zelenskyy had asked them to talk to Putin, and noted that Oslo hosted talks a week ago with Zelenskyy and US and NATO representatives. Talks between US, Russian and Ukrainian officials are planned in Abu Dhabi in November, and Xi has proposed a Trump–Putin meeting at APEC on November 18–19 (WSWS). - Jordan Shilton argued on the WSWS that the Nordic initiative is not a genuine peace effort but a product of US–European rivalry over the spoils of the war.

6. Brazil summons Argentina’s envoy over Flávio Bolsonaro’s escape to Miami

On Thursday Brazil’s Foreign Ministry summoned Argentina’s chargé d’affaires, María Emilia Cortés, to explain how Flávio Bolsonaro, Lula’s defeated runoff opponent, crossed Argentina and left for the United States despite a court order to surrender his passport and remain in Brazil (Reuters sources). Argentine Foreign Minister Pablo Quirno denied on social media that any formal request for explanations had been received. Milei’s spokesman described the escape as a rescue from communism, and Flávio thanked Milei for his help without giving details. Since landing in Miami, Flávio has called himself Brazil’s “legitimate president-elect.” He has not conceded, and he claims irregularities over a Supreme Court decision to extend voting hours, an extension both campaigns had requested.

Lula won Sunday’s runoff with 51.8%, his fourth non-consecutive term. Flávio is under investigation in a money-laundering probe linked to the Banco Master scandal. Investigators are examining a multimillion-dollar payment he allegedly received from the bank’s former head, Daniel Vorcaro, reportedly to finance a film about his father. “I didn’t leave Brazil, I left for Brazil,” Flávio said in an Instagram livestream, accusing Justice Alexandre de Moraes of fabricating charges.

Key facts: - His father, Jair Bolsonaro, is serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a coup to stop Lula taking office. Trump answered that conviction with sanctions and tariffs on Brazil. - Brazil and Argentina have already recalled their ambassadors once, in August, after a series of public disputes between Lula and Milei. - Trump has suggested the election was rigged while saying he would not take sides. The escape route ran through Argentina, a government openly aligned with the Bolsonaros, to the US. - Guilherme Ferreira writes on the WSWS that the international “left” is hailing the result as a model for defeating fascism, while about 45% of voters rejected each candidate throughout the campaign.

China’s fifth plenum expels a record 33 Central Committee members

The Chinese Communist Party’s four-day fifth plenum closed with a communiqué on Thursday. It expelled 33 Central Committee members, 24 full members and nine alternates, for unspecified “grave violations of discipline and the law.” Sixteen of the 33 are top military officers. They include two former Politburo members: Ma Xingrui, the former Xinjiang party secretary, and Zhang Youxia, formerly the senior vice-chairman of the Central Military Commission (CMC). Zhang Shengmin was promoted to the Politburo, the first promotion between party congresses since 1994, and the CMC now consists of only Xi and Zhang Shengmin. Only 279 members attended, against the 376 elected in 2022, so more than a quarter were absent without explanation. The communiqué warns of a “grave situation marked by weak, hollow, and watered-down Party leadership.” It names Xi “the core” and adds “Xi Jinping Thought on Party Building” to party doctrine ahead of next year’s congress, where he is expected to seek a fourth term.

  • Of the 44 uniformed full members elected in 2022, only Zhang Shengmin and Defence Minister Dong Jun have been neither purged nor disappeared (Asia Society Policy Institute, cited by WSWS).
  • The US–China trade truce, extended by three months at the September Washington summit, expires in January.

El Salvador flies troops and helicopters into Haiti via the Dominican Republic

El Salvador’s Foreign Ministry says it will send more than 2,000 soldiers to Haiti. Their stated mission is to find four Salvadoran aviation technicians who disappeared on October 18, when gunmen ambushed their vehicle at Croix-des-Bouquets east of Port-au-Prince, and to fight the gangs. Bukele threatened the gangs in a post written in Haitian Creole and has coordinated with Prime Minister Alix Didier Fils-Aimé. It is unclear whether the troops will join the UN-backed Gang Suppression Force (GSF) or operate separately. The GSF was authorised for 5,550 personnel but had just over 2,000 in mid-October. The WSWS has tracked the logistics. A US military C-130 flew the first reinforcements in on October 23. A Colombian Air Force C-130H carried more troops on October 27. On October 29, seven Salvadoran combat helicopters left the Dominican Army’s El Rodeo base at Jimaní for Haiti. Axios reports that Trump told Bukele: “Do what you have to do.”

  • Haiti’s electoral council has moved the first round of presidential and parliamentary elections from December 13 to July 18, 2027, and the runoff to October 4, 2027. Haiti has not held an election since 2016.
  • 5.94 million Haitians face acute hunger, 1.8 million of them at emergency levels, and the figure is projected to reach 6.35 million by mid-2027. More than 5,700 people have been killed this year and 1.5 million displaced.
  • House Foreign Affairs Chair Brian Mast: “I hope the mission is revenge, retribution and destruction of the gangs.”

Israel demolishes Hebron homes as the siege displaces nearly 5,000

On Friday Israeli forces blew up the homes of brothers Ahmad and Hammouda al-Jaabeh, blamed for the October 23 attack near the Farsh al-Hawa checkpoint that killed two soldiers. Army engineers spent at least nine hours rigging the houses with explosives. Hebron’s municipality says the eight-day siege has displaced 4,969 Palestinians from 780 families. At least 120 people have been arrested and at least 13 businesses demolished. Ein Deir Bahha has been emptied of residents, and hundreds of families in al-Jalda remain trapped. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich has approved faster demolitions in Area C. Elsewhere in the West Bank, soldiers and settlers attacked olive harvesters near Bethlehem and Nablus. Analyst Raed Nairat called the Hebron operation “effectively collective punishment, with the aim of breaking the Palestinians’ will.” Netanyahu remains caretaker premier after the October 27 election.

Polish police enter the Constitutional Tribunal after its president is suspended

Police entered Warsaw’s Constitutional Tribunal after Law and Justice (PiS) supporters gathered to protest the suspension of its president, Bogdan Święczkowski. Police detained three people after “dangerous projectiles” were thrown. Święczkowski is accused of blocking judges chosen by the current parliamentary majority from taking their seats for six months, which has largely paralysed the court. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek has asked for his immunity, and his deputy’s, to be lifted. Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, one of the judges President Karol Nawrocki has refused to swear in, now heads the tribunal. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said restoring the rule of law “requires decisive action.” Former Justice Minister Zbigniew Ziobro called it a “blatant, criminal attack on the constitutional order,” and Nawrocki turned his plane back from a trip to Senegal. Two rival factions of the Polish ruling class are now using police and courts against each other.

EU and Canada launch an “Alliance for the Future” against US tariff pressure

Prime Minister Mark Carney hosted European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President António Costa in Montreal on Friday. They signed a digital trade agreement and launched an “Alliance for the Future,” which Ottawa presents as preparation for Canada to become the EU’s first “associate member.” The term has no legal basis, and some member states dispute it. A joint statement defines “true sovereignty” as secure access to critical minerals, semiconductors, payment systems, energy, vaccines, space communications and AI. The European Investment Bank will invest in Canadian critical minerals. Canada joins the EU’s €90 billion Ukraine loan with an initial €500 million. Carney, who is in his own trade dispute with Trump, claimed more than 20 measures on “affordability, opportunities, and sovereignty.” The summit took place as Carney’s government pushes Bill C-39 through Parliament to codify federal powers to end strikes (see WSWS coverage).

Port Sudan families spend a full month’s wage on water

In Deim Mayo, a district of Port Sudan with no piped water, Amina Khalafallah spends 10,000 Sudanese pounds (about $1) a day on water for her four children, which adds up to her entire monthly salary. A distributor who charged 4,000 pounds to fill a donkey cart in April 2023 now charges 16,000. A 50,000-litre tanker costs 1.5 million pounds, against about 450,000 before the war, mainly because of fuel costs. The Arbaat Dam, which supplied half the city’s water, collapsed in August 2024. The African Development Bank says supply meets less than 40% of demand. UNDP-backed repairs could serve about 230,000 people in a city now estimated at one million, swollen by people displaced from the fighting. Emad Hafez of Port Sudan Teaching Hospital warns of cholera, diarrhoeal disease, malaria and dengue. According to UNDP, the war, now in its fourth year, has pushed average Sudanese incomes back to early-1990s levels.

Friday’s session was dominated by a selloff in AI-linked stocks after disappointing big-tech earnings. Fake-poll scandal prompts FCC threat to broadcasters’ licences

Wedgewood Polls admitted that its battleground surveys in Texas, Ohio, Michigan, Kansas and Maine were fabricated. It called them “an experimental project” to expose weaknesses in polling, then deleted the statement. The admission came after the site FiftyPlusOne flagged inconsistent raw data and response identifiers. In August, Median Strategies admitted faking results as a “short-term social experiment.” The New York Times had listed 15 Wedgewood surveys from June to October and has removed them. Data journalist G. Elliott Morris said the firm was “trying to cover up for their fraud.” Michigan Democrat Mallory McMorrow says a Wedgewood poll showing her in single digits contributed to her suspending her Senate primary campaign in July.

The episode is being used to expand state control over the media. FCC Chair Brendan Carr says fake polls have seen “a significant surge” in 2026 and has warned broadcasters that amplifying them could cost them their licences. The sole Democratic commissioner, Anna Gomez, objected. Trump called the polls “ARTIFICIAL” on Truth Social and said the case could strengthen his $15 billion defamation suit against the Times. The University of Washington’s Center for an Informed Public found that right-wing media quickly used the scandal to cast polls favourable to Democrats as untrustworthy, laying the groundwork for disputing the results.

Two Chinese nationals sentenced to 30 months for spying on the US Navy

Liren “Ryan” Lai, 40, and Yuance Chen, 39, were each sentenced to 30 months in prison and three years of supervised release, the Justice Department announced on Friday. Both pleaded guilty to conspiring to act, and acting, as unregistered agents of China’s Ministry of State Security from at least May 2021. Lai, who entered the US on a tourist visa, recruited Chen, a permanent resident. They arranged cash “dead drops” of $10,000 and $20,000, photographed a naval facility, recruits and active-duty sailors, and compiled files on military personnel as potential intelligence targets. Assistant Attorney General John Eisenberg called it a “brazen” scheme. The sentencing follows the Pentagon’s designation of Confucius Institutes as a national security threat. Trump himself said in August that “we spy on them too.”

Pope Leo becomes the first pontiff to vote in a US general election

The Vatican confirmed that Leo XIV, born Robert Prevost in Chicago, mailed an Illinois ballot before Tuesday’s midterms. Illinois is choosing a successor to retiring Senator Dick Durbin and deciding whether to re-elect Governor JB Pritzker. Leo requested the ballot on October 3, about ten days before surgery to remove a cancerous lung tumour, and left hospital on October 17. State records show Prevost voted in Democratic primaries in 2008 and 2010 and Republican primaries from 2012 to 2016. AI-found “PHP2Shell” flaws force emergency patching across the web

Governments and companies worldwide have urged website administrators to update immediately after the PHP Foundation flagged critical security flaws, nicknamed “PHP2Shell.” Independent researchers found them using AI. They had been in the code for more than a decade, and humans alone would have found them too complex to exploit. Some government and corporate sites briefly went offline to patch. PHP, created in 1995, runs about 70% of the world’s websites, including Facebook and Wikipedia. The PHP Foundation that maintains it runs on a small budget.

  • Anthropic’s Project Glasswing used its Claude Mythos model to find more than 23,000 vulnerabilities across more than 1,000 open-source projects in a month. Google’s latest Chrome update fixed more than 1,000 bugs, more than its previous two dozen updates combined. Mozilla found more than 400 vulnerabilities in April, against a previous record of 76.
  • Alpha-Omega, funded by Anthropic, Amazon, Google, Microsoft and OpenAI, spends only about $7 million a year helping open-source developers. Apache’s volunteer security team estimates it has about ten times the usual number of issues to handle.
  • OpenUK’s Amanda Brock: “It’s simply not possible with the resources in place to fix all of the known vulnerabilities.” MIT’s Peter Slattery expects some flaws to be exploited before they are fixed.

This shows the private appropriation of socially produced knowledge. The trillion-dollar corporations whose products run on volunteer-maintained code contribute almost nothing to maintaining it, while selling AI tools that expose its weaknesses faster than unpaid maintainers can fix them.

NASA plans “Lunar Reactor 1” by the end of 2030. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty bans national claims on the Moon, but a reactor “safety zone” could exclude rivals in practice. In 1978 the Soviet satellite Kosmos 954 scattered radioactive debris over about 124,000 square kilometres of northern Canada.
